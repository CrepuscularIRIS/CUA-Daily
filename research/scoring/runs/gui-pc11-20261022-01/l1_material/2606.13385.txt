title: Who Pays the Price? Stakeholder-Centric Prompt Injection Benchmarking for Real-world Web Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13385

§Abstract
LLM-based web agents are increasingly deployed in real-world settings such as e-commerce, where they interact extensively with untrusted web content while executing actions that carry direct financial consequences. This makes them vulnerable to prompt-injection attacks, in which seemingly benign web content conceals adversarial instructions that manipulate the agent's behavior. Existing security benchmarks adopt an \textit{attack-centric} perspective, focusing on the technical feasibility of injections while overlooking the nuanced distribution of resulting harms. In practice, however, prompt-injection risk is victim-dependent: a single exploit can produce asymmetric consequences for different stakeholders, and the same attack pattern may exhibit substantially different effectiveness depending on whom it targets. To capture these properties, we introduce StakeBench, a stakeholder-centric benchmark that systematically categorizes and attributes harm in real-world web agent systems for online shopping. In general, StakeBench decomposes prompt-injection risk into 12 concrete attack objectives across three stakeholder classes, realized by 22 reusable templates and instantiated into 264 executable adversarial cases spanning 12 product categories, with each case evaluated along complementary outcome- and process-level metrics. Evaluating four deployable agent-backbone configurations across 3,168 attacked runs, we find substantial and heterogeneous vulnerabilities: no attack objective is reliably resisted by current LLM-based web agents, and outcomes span four qualitatively distinct modes. These patterns are missed by conventional attack-centric, single-metric evaluation, underscoring the need for stakeholder-aware assessment of LLM-based agents in real-world deployments.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language model (LLM)-based web agents are increasingly deployed in high-stakes, real-world environments such as e-commerce, where they autonomously search, compare, and transact on behalf of their users ( Yao et al., 2022a ; Deng et al., 2023 ; Lai et al., 2024 ) . Unlike conventional LLM applications, these agents perceive content from open web pages and execute actions that directly alter the environment, often with irreversible financial consequences ( Zheng et al., 2024 ; Koh et al., 2024 ; Fang et al., 2025 ) . By continuously turning untrusted web data into rankings, recommendations, and executed transactions, they place security and reliability at the intersection of trustworthy data mining and agentic decision making. However, web agents remain vulnerable to prompt injection attacks ( Perez and Ribeiro, 2022 ; Wei et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) , where adversarial instructions hidden in untrusted environmental content steer the agent away from the user’s intended task. Such attacks succeed even in their simplest form, e.g. , a plainly written malicious instruction placed in a product review or page comment can hijack agent behavior without sophisticated attack engineering ( Yi et al., 2025 ; Schmotz et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025 ) . The resulting harm is no longer confined to model output but materializes as executed actions, such as unintended purchases, leaked personal information, or unauthorized modifications to third-party content ( Ruan et al., 2023 ; Alizadeh et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025a ) . Because these actions affect multiple stakeholders ( e.g. , end users, third-party sellers, and the host platforms), prompt injection against web agents is fundamentally a system-level security problem with multi-party harm . Yet existing evaluation resources do not capture how this harm is distributed across the affected parties. Existing benchmarks ( Zhang et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Levy et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2026 ) evaluate prompt-injection risk along axes such as application scenario, injection surface, or attack mechanism, and typically summarize outcomes as an aggregate attack success rate. This attack-centric view establishes where an injection can be delivered and whether it lands, but leaves two deployment-relevant questions unanswered. (1) Who bears the resulting harm? For example, an injected review that steers the agent toward a particular product may still leave the user with an acceptable purchase, while disadvantaging competing sellers and eroding the ranking integrity the platform maintains. Because these parties make their own deployment and defense decisions, an aggregate score is actionable for none of them. (2) How does the compromise manifest? A compromise that completes the delegated task normally, one that visibly derails it, and one that destabilizes the agent without realizing the adversarial objective are all scored alike under attack success, yet they differ in detectability and in the defenses they call for. Answering either question requires organizing evaluation around the entity bearing the harm and measuring each execution along more than one axis, which to our knowledge no existing prompt-injection benchmark does. To address these limitations, we introduce StakeBench , a benchmark that reframes prompt-injection evaluation for web agents around who pays the price: rather than only asking where an attack can be delivered, it asks which party bears the harm once one succeeds. We instantiate StakeBench in online shopping ( Lyu et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026a ) , a domain characterized by direct financial consequences, parties with structurally conflicting interests, and abundant attacker-controllable content surfaces such as reviews, ratings, product metadata, and images. StakeBench models three principal stakeholder groups, User, Seller, and Platform, and organizes their potential harms into a taxonomy of 12 concrete harm objectives. To operationalize this taxonomy, we construct 22 reusable attack templates and instantiate them across the 12 native product categories of a functional e-commerce environment, yielding 264 executable adversarial cases. Each case is a complete multi-step agent trajectory whose outcome is decided from executed actions and the final environment state rather than from an isolated model response.
