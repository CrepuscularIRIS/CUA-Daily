arxiv: 2610.23307 (2610.23307v1)
title: MobileInject: Evaluating Reasoning-to-Action Security of MLLM-Based Mobile Agents under Cross-Modal Consistency Injection
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23307

§Abstract
Multimodal large language model (MLLM)-based mobile agents are rapidly evolving from conversational assistants into autonomous systems that perceive, reason over, and act on smartphone environments. This capability also exposes a new security risk: adversaries can embed malicious objectives in cross-application mobile content and steer agent behavior through indirect multimodal injection. Although prompt injection has been studied in desktop and web settings, its cross-modal properties in mobile ecosystems remain poorly understood, and existing security analyses are still limited. To close this gap, we introduce MobileInject, the first benchmark for evaluating mobile agents against multimodal injection attacks and reasoning-to-action security risks. MobileInject provides a high-fidelity interactive mobile evaluation framework covering 11 scenarios, 9 security-risk categories, and 108 executable risk instances. It further introduces Cross-Modal Consistency Injection to exploit corroboration across modalities, two enhanced attack methods, Multi-Role Collaborative Deception and Reasoning-Camouflage Injection, to intensify trust manipulation and reasoning hijacking, and a Reasoning Security Diagnosis Framework for fine-grained failure analysis. Systematic experiments show that mobile agents are broadly vulnerable to multimodal injection, and that many failures stem from reasoning hijacking at early stages. These findings establish MobileInject as a systematic cross-modal benchmark for studying and improving the security of mobile agents. Code release details are provided in the Appendix Open Science section.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are increasingly evolving from passive conversational interfaces into autonomous mobile agents capable of operating in real-world smartphone environments [ 38 , 25 , 19 ] . Unlike conventional multimodal assistants that primarily perceive images and generate textual responses, MLLM-based mobile agents close the loop between perception, reasoning, and action. They can observe mobile screenshots, parse interface structures, switch across applications, click buttons, scroll interfaces, enter text, and complete multi-step user tasks. This transition from multimodal understanding to mobile task execution substantially expands the capability boundary of foundation models, enabling personalized agents to act as autonomous operators for communication, scheduling, information retrieval, payment, file management, and other daily mobile workflows [ 24 , 1 ] . However, this leap in execution capability also significantly expands the attack surface and amplifies the consequences of security risks [ 8 , 21 , 37 ] . In traditional LLM settings, adversarial instructions are usually delivered through explicit prompts [ 20 , 23 , 40 ] ; in mobile-agent settings, however, malicious objectives can be embedded into the broader mobile ecosystem and encountered during normal task execution [ 8 , 37 ] . For example, adversarial content may be hidden in SMS messages, emails, calendar events, webpages, social media content, or other cross-application content. Once perceived by the agent, such content may enter its decision context through visual or textual observations, thereby influencing its reasoning, planning, and action execution. Therefore, attacks against mobile agents are no longer limited to inducing unsafe text generation, but may lead to more threatening consequences, such as privacy leakage, unauthorized operations, financial misconduct, data disclosure, or persistent changes to system and application states. Mobile-agent security is particularly challenging because the mobile ecosystem exposes a broad, temporally distributed, and multimodal attack surface. First, a mobile agent operates in a continuous environment jointly formed by notifications, messages, emails, calendars, webpages, social media feeds, and third-party application states. Malicious payloads can hide across different applications, pages, and time points, and can be triggered during perception, application switching, or cross-application execution. Second, a mobile agent relies on heterogeneous observations, including visual observations such as screenshots and rendered UIs, as well as textual observations such as XML hierarchies, accessibility trees, UI attributes, and structured interface representations. Because mobile agents commonly ground decisions on both rendered and structured views of the same interface state, attacker-authored content can naturally surface in synchronized visual and textual channels during normal execution. This creates a joint attack surface that single-channel evaluations cannot characterize. More fundamentally, attacker-authored content can enter and manipulate the agent’s reasoning through these observations, displace the trusted user goal, and thereby hijack subsequent mobile actions.
