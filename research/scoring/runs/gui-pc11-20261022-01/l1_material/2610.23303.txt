title: MobileJailBench: Red Queen Jailbreak Benchmarking for Mobile Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23303

§Abstract
MLLM-based mobile agents are rapidly gaining autonomy in perception, planning, and closed-loop execution in dynamic mobile environments. However, this autonomy also broadens the attack surface and amplifies the risks of malicious misuse, including privacy leakage, device compromise, and large-scale phishing. Existing jailbreak evaluations leave three critical gaps for mobile agents: they focus on conversational attacks instead of mobile workflows, use static task sets with fixed difficulty, and rely narrowly on attack success rate rather than comprehensive security assessment. To close these gaps, we present MobileJailBench, the first evolving jailbreak benchmark for mobile agents, comprising three key components: (1) a benchmark comprising 520 tasks spanning three jailbreak paradigms and 14 risk categories, built on a high-fidelity mobile sandbox that we construct, enabling realistic workflows across over 20 widely used apps; (2) Dual-Dynamic Evolution, which combines Failure-Distilled Jailbreak Evolution with Weakness-Driven Benchmark Expansion; and (3) Stratified Adjudication Framework for assessing security awareness, execution behavior, and risk consequences. Extensive experiments show that current mobile agents are particularly vulnerable to implicit jailbreaks, as their safety alignment remains largely superficial and fails to generalize from conversational refusal to dynamic mobile interactions. Moreover, our dynamic evolution mechanism sharpens practical threat boundaries by continually uncovering new vulnerabilities, reaching a 100% ASR on Qwen3.5-122B. MobileJailBench provides a reproducible, continuously evolving framework for tracking the true security boundaries of mobile agents.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs), together with agentic scaffolding, are transforming mobile automation from passive assistance into autonomous mobile agents that can perceive screens, reason over stateful contexts, and execute multi-step actions in dynamic smartphone environments ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2024b ; Rawles et al., 2025 ) . Unlike conventional language agents that return text, mobile agents operate directly on application interfaces—navigating across apps, manipulating UI elements, invoking system functions, and completing cross-application workflows on behalf of users ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Deng et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) . This shift from text generation to embodied device operation substantially expands the practical capability boundary of foundation models. However, this leap in capability also fundamentally intensifies the security implications of mobile agents. They can access sensitive data, act across applications, and execute operations that change the state of a personal device. Consequently, security failures are no longer limited to harmful outputs; they can directly translate into harmful actions with immediate real-world consequences. In this setting, jailbreak attacks are especially alarming because they can subvert safety alignment and turn an otherwise benign assistant into a practical tool for malicious use. Once jailbroken, a mobile agent may leak sensitive data, disrupt system functionality, or conduct large-scale phishing and social engineering attacks. Yet existing jailbreak evaluations remain poorly matched to mobile agents. First , they mostly test conversational attacks rather than mobile workflows involving interface navigation, app switching, and irreversible state changes ( Deng et al., 2024 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) . Second , they rely on static task sets with fixed difficulty ( Mazeika et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) , failing to capture the adaptive nature of practical red-teaming. Third , they focus narrowly on attack success rate (ASR) ( Shen et al., 2024 ) , without diagnosing whether the agent recognizes risk, how it behaves under jailbreak pressure, or what harms its actions actually produce. These limitations are especially problematic in mobile settings, where agents operate in dynamic, stateful, and cross-application environments ( Rawles et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) .
