title: Control OSWorld: An AI Control Environment for GUI Computer Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03818

§Abstract
AI agents that operate a computer through its graphical user interface (GUI) are being widely deployed. AI control studies how to prevent an AI system from causing harm even if it is misaligned and actively trying to do so. Most control research to date has focused on coding agents, leaving computer use largely unexplored. We introduce Control OSWorld, a control evaluation that pairs 318 tasks from OSWorld with 81 harmful side tasks (e.g., exfiltrating a private file) that an agent must complete without being caught. We use Control OSWorld to create and study control monitors that detect malicious agents interacting with a GUI. We find that a weaker monitor can reliably distinguish honest from malicious trajectories produced by a more capable agent when it sees the full trajectory (97% recall at 3% false positive rate). However, when the monitor has to score each step before it is executed, recall drops at low false positive rates because each action is judged with less context. Monitor performance also depends on what the monitor can see: access to the agent's visible text is critical, while screenshots provide little additional value.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
In January 2025, the CEO of OpenAI designated that year as “the year of AI agents” ( Altman, 2025 ) . Almost two years later, we do indeed see AI agents, mostly powered by large vision and language models, deployed in a variety of scenarios. Coding agents like Claude Code and Codex are already used at an enormous scale ( O’Laughlin et al., 2026 ) , and trends indicate that adoption will keep rising. Alongside coding agents with a text-only interface, computer use AI agents are also being developed, capable of using a computer with a monitor similarly to how a human uses it ( Anthropic, 2026a ; OpenAI, 2026a ; Google, 2026 ) . Autonomous AI agents with broad access permissions can be a liability: in the instantly-famous “HuggingFace incident” ( OpenAI, 2026b ) , a group of AI agents developed by OpenAI hacked into the HuggingFace servers using multiple zero-day vulnerabilities in OpenAI’s and HuggingFace’s infrastructure. The agents did this to study the scoring function of the cybersecurity benchmark they were being evaluated on ( METR, 2026b ) . Preventing safety failures like this requires a multi-layered defense-in-depth approach ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) . One layer comes from AI alignment—making sure that the goals of the AI are aligned with ours. Extensive empirical evidence and conceptual arguments indicate that AI alignment is a difficult and currently unsolved problem ( Carlsmith, 2023 ; Meinke et al., 2024 ; Schoen et al., 2025 ; Lynch et al., 2025 ; Lynch et al., 2026 ) . AI control ( Greenblatt et al., 2023 ) is the second layer of defense: assuming an AI agent is misaligned, AI control techniques aim to prevent the agent from causing harm. Following the introduction of AI control by Greenblatt et al. (2023) , the field has developed a standardized evaluation framework for AI control measures, used across many subsequent studies ( Bhatt et al., 2025 ; Kutasov et al., 2025a ; Terekhov et al., 2026b ) . According to this framework, control interventions are tested on a special kind of a sabotage evaluation benchmark ( Benton et al., 2024 ) , where benign tasks are paired with malicious side tasks that an agent needs to execute covertly. While computer use agents may become increasingly common due to their promise to assist with a wide range of administrative tasks, little AI control research is focused on them. In this paper, we introduce Control OSWorld , a control setting for computer use AI agents. As a source of benign tasks, we take OSWorld ( Xie et al., 2024 ) , a widely adopted benchmark for measuring progress on computer use capabilities. This benchmark comes with programmatic verification of task completion and flexible options for environment isolation. We pair OSWorld tasks with custom malicious tasks, also verified programmatically, that the agent needs to perform covertly. We then run multiple models as “attackers” and “monitors” on Control OSWorld to test how good the models are at covert subversive actions in a computer use environment, and how easy it is for the monitors to flag suspicious activity. Overall, our contributions are as follows: • Control OSWorld , a control setting pairing 318 OSWorld main tasks with 81 programmatically verified malicious side tasks, with per-step side task scoring that identifies the exact step at which the side task is completed. • An ablation of monitor inputs (actions, visible text, screenshots). The monitor’s success in flagging malicious actions is mostly driven by the agent’s explanatory text, while screenshots surprisingly provide little additional uplift to the monitor once text is present. • A comparison of full-trajectory and per-step monitoring. Monitors have high accuracy at separating honest from attack trajectories when scoring the full trajectory, but lose recall at low false-positive rates when they must flag the side task before it executes. • An analysis of agent response to different prompts requesting harmful tasks. Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.8 perform malicious side tasks when framed as a covert secondary objective, but refuse when the same actions are requested directly by the user. Opus refuses 26 of 81 as plain requests and none of 318 under the covert objective framing. Prompt optimization can reduce the agent’s tendency to verbalize the hidden objective in its visible text (leakage 26.1% \to 1.9%). Code, side tasks, and experiment configs are available at https://github.com/mariasrap/control-osworld .
