title: Don't Retrain, Remix: Adaptation of Web Agents using Mixture Hypernetworks
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22539

§Abstract
The web is not static or uniform. Websites that serve the same purpose can look and navigate very differently. Even within a single site, successive redesigns can change how familiar features are presented and accessed. Humans adapt to this by recognizing familiar interaction patterns and reusing what they already know. We give web agents a similar ability with WebMix, a mixture hypernetwork that adapts them to unseen interfaces by composing previously trained low-rank adapters. WebMix begins with a bank of adapters trained on source interfaces. At deployment, a scout agent explores the target interface and summarizes its observations into a compact manual. A lightweight router uses this manual to predict mixing coefficients over the adapter bank, which combine the existing adapters into a new one for inference. Adapting to a new interface takes only a scouting run and a forward pass, without gradient updates on the target. With Qwen and Llama models, we evaluate domain transfer on TimeWarp and WebArena-Lite-v2, and version transfer on TimeWarp. In all three settings, WebMix matches or outperforms in-context learning and LoRA baselines, often matching the average success of specialists trained directly on those interfaces. On TimeWarp domain transfer, it gains about 8 percentage points on average over the best baseline with both models. These gains do not come at the cost of seen interfaces, where WebMix keeps its lead, and extend to version transfer. Adapting to a new interface can therefore rely on exploration and composition instead of retraining.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Humans compose and transfer skills to unfamiliar interfaces with little effort. A person who has booked flights on American Airlines’ website can book the same flight through their credit card’s travel portal, following the same search, filter, and checkout flow in a layout they have never seen. The same holds across time: when a site is redesigned, its regular users are slowed down for a few visits, not retaught. A fine-tuned web agent can do neither. Mind2Web ( Deng et al., 2023 ) reports a steady drop in performance from its cross-task split to its cross-website and cross-domain splits. TimeWarp ( Ishmam and Marino, 2026 ) , which renders the same sites in the styles of six eras of web design, shows that an agent trained on one era degrades on the next. Frontier models reduce this brittleness through scale ( Zheng et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2024 ; Anthropic, 2024 ; AI, 2025 ; Müller and Žunič, 2024 ) , but their closed weights and serving cost raise privacy and deployment concerns ( Qi et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Small open-weight models that run on commodity hardware are the practical alternative ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ; Qwen Team, 2026 ) , and this brittleness hits them hardest. They become competent web agents only after fine-tuning on demonstrations, typically through a low-rank adapter (LoRA) ( Hu et al., 2022 ) on a frozen base, and every new site or redesign calls for a new corpus and a new adapter. Their performance is therefore bounded by the demonstrations they are trained on, and web demonstrations are expensive to collect: each one requires a human operating a live site, and the site may be redesigned before the corpus is large enough. We therefore ask whether an agent can get more out of the demonstrations it already has. Facebook has altered its interface repeatedly since 2004, but it has always had a search bar. Amazon and Wikipedia serve different purposes entirely, yet browsing a catalog of products and browsing an encyclopedia of articles are the same operation: filtering, paging, and selecting from a list. If an adapter trained on one interface has already learned these operations, that knowledge ought to be transferable to the next. Recent work adapts small web agents with less supervision. It reduces target-interface demonstrations to one or two examples ( Verma et al., 2025 ) , synthesizes trajectories through autonomous exploration of the target ( Murty et al., 2024 ) , transfers reusable action routines across websites ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , or learns lightweight alignment vectors from deployment-time interaction ( Chen et al., 2025 ) . All of these still treat each new interface as a separate adaptation problem. They reuse knowledge about websites, but none of them learns how to adapt, so the next interface starts from scratch again. A hypernetwork can learn such an adaptation rule, because it generates an adapter from a description of the target ( Ha et al., 2017 ; Charakorn et al., 2025 ) . For web interfaces, however, generating adapter weights directly is hard. A family of interfaces provides only a handful of conditionings from which to regress millions of weights, and LoRA factors are not identifiable, so adapters that behave alike can lie far apart in weight space ( Charakorn et al., 2025 ) . We introduce WebMix , a mixture hypernetwork ( Qu et al., 2023 ) that learns to adapt web agents by composing LoRA adapters trained on familiar interfaces. A scout briefly explores a new interface and summarizes its observations in a compact manual. Conditioned on this description, a small router predicts one mixing coefficient per adapter and combines their weight updates into a single adapter for the frozen base policy. We train the router on source demonstrations through the composed policy and keep the adapter bank fixed. Adaptation then requires only scouting and one forward pass, with no further gradient updates. On TimeWarp ( Ishmam and Marino, 2026 ) , WebMix achieves 56.1% success on the unseen News domain with Qwen-3.5 4B, exceeding transferred LoRA by 10.6 percentage points. Across held-out domains, average gains reach 8.3 points for Qwen and 7.5 points for Llama-3.1 8B, nearly doubling Llama’s success and matching the average performance of target-trained specialists for both models. Improvements extend to held-out TimeWarp versions and WebArena-Lite-v2 domains, where Qwen gains 3.2 and 4.6 points, respectively. The benefits also extend to seen interfaces: with target domains included in training, Qwen achieves 42.4% overall success on WebArena-Lite-v2, outperforming LoRA trained on all domains by 4.3 points. Our contributions are the following: ✥ We introduce WebMix , a mixture hypernetwork that learns to compose existing LoRA adapters from a scouted interface description.
