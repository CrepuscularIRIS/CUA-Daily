title: When Bots Take the Bait: Exposing and Mitigating the Emerging Social Engineering Attack in Web Automation Agent

§Abstract
Web agents, powered by large language models (LLMs), are increasingly deployed to automate complex web interactions. The rise of open-source frameworks (e.g., Browser Use, Skyvern-AI) has accelerated adoption, but also broadened the attack surface. While prior research has focused on model threats such as prompt injection and backdoors, the risks of social engineering remain largely unexplored. We present the first systematic study of social engineering attacks against web automation agents and design a pluggable runtime mitigation solution. On the attack side, we introduce the AgentBait paradigm, which exploits intrinsic weaknesses in agent execution: inducement contexts can distort the agent's reasoning and steer it toward malicious objectives misaligned with the intended task. On the defense side, we propose SUPERVISOR, a lightweight runtime module that enforces environment and intention consistency alignment between webpage context and intended goals to mitigate unsafe operations before execution. Empirical results show that mainstream frameworks are highly vulnerable to AgentBait, with an average attack success rate of 67.5% and peaks above 80% under specific strategies (e.g., trusted identity forgery). Compared with existing lightweight defenses, our module can be seamlessly integrated across different web automation frameworks and reduces attack success rates by up to 78.1% on average while incurring only a 7.7% runtime overhead and preserving usability. This work reveals AgentBait as a critical new threat surface for web agents and establishes a practical, generalizable defense, advancing the security of this rapidly emerging ecosystem. We reported the details of this attack to the framework developers and received acknowledgment before submission.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Web agents have recently emerged as a prominent interaction paradigm in large language models (LLMs). Leveraging their capacity for automated dialogue and efficient execution of complex web tasks, they have been widely adopted in domains such as intelligent search, personalized recommendation, and online form completion [ 7 , 67 ] . The availability of open-source frameworks (e.g., Browser Use [ 39 ] , SeeAct [ 74 ] ) has further lowered the barrier to deploying web agents, enabling developers to build, customize, and integrate such systems with greater ease, thereby accelerating their widespread adoption [ 49 , 40 , 1 ] . Yet this convenience comes with a hidden cost. Prior research has largely focused on model threats, such as prompt injection [ 20 , 63 , 35 , 10 ] and training backdoors [ 65 , 15 , 11 ] , as well as framework vulnerabilities including denial-of-service (DoS) [ 24 ] , privilege escalation [ 31 ] , and protocol abuse [ 22 , 68 ] . In practice, however, there exist social engineering (SE) attacks that weaponize contextual persuasion rather than technical exploitation to distort a web agent’s reasoning and induce unsafe actions, without any prompt injection or code exploitation. Novel AGENTBAIT Threat. In this study, we term this new class of attacks AGENTBAIT , a novel attack that transposes human-targeted deception into machine cognition . Instead of tricking users, the adversary deceives the agent’s reasoning system: by embedding inducement contexts such as “urgent verification” or “official confirmation”, the attacker steers the agent’s reasoning toward malicious objectives along an execution path consistent with the user’s intended task. As shown in Figure 1 , when tasked with completing a survey, a web agent encounters a deceptive pop-up claiming identity verification. To proceed, the agent fills and submits the user’s PII without any warning. Unlike humans, agents automate trust at scale: a single crafted webpage can induce thousands of identical unsafe operations, escalating into credential leakage, permission abuse, or malware installation. Our Work. To assess the AGENTBAIT risks of mainstream web agent frameworks, we conduct evaluations using representative attacks. Results show that none of the agent frameworks proves bulletproof: the average attack success rate reaches 67.5%, peaking above 80% under specific strategies. This high effectiveness stems from overreliance on the LLM’s intrinsic safeguards and framework-level weaknesses, including excessive focus on task completion, limited contextual reasoning, and absent trust verification. To mitigate AGENTBAIT attacks, we design Supervisor , a plug-and-play module that enforces environment and intention consistency check, intercepting unsafe operations before execution. Our key advantage lies in achieving strong protection without intrusive modification. In contrast to full-stack frameworks such as IsolateGPT [ 64 ] and ACE [ 33 ] , which require re-architected execution pipelines, Supervisor can be seamlessly integrated across diverse web agent frameworks without modifying their internal reasoning flow. Compared with existing lightweight defenses such as AGrail [ 36 ] and ATHENA [ 47 ] , Supervisor achieves the strongest protection, reducing attack success rates by up to 78.1% on average while incurring only a 7.7% runtime overhead and preserving usability. In line with responsible disclosure practices, we have reported the details of this attack to the framework developers and received acknowledgment. Furthermore, we will release our defense system as open source to facilitate broader adoption and to strengthen the resilience of the open-source ecosystem against AGENTBAIT attacks. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: • First systematic analysis of social engineering against web agents. We introduce the AGENTBAIT paradigm and construct standardized and reusable task sets for systematic evaluation. • Empirical assessment of AgentBait risks in popular web agent frameworks. We conduct large-scale experiments on five mainstream open-source web agent frameworks and reveal none of the agent frameworks proves bulletproof against AGENTBAIT attacks. • Lightweight runtime defense plugin for mitigating attacks. We design Supervisor , a pluggable runtime module that verifies consistency, providing effective protection against AGENTBAIT attacks with minimal overhead. The module will be open-sourced to enhance community defense capabilities.

## III AGENTBAIT
This section establishes the analytical foundation of AGENTBAIT . Section III-A introduces the attack workflow, and defines indicators that jointly define how social engineering elements interact with web agents in adversarial contexts. Section III-B then details representative attack vectors, illustrating concrete inducement strategies and their operational goals. Together, these components form the basis for the empirical evaluation in Section IV and defense framework in Section V . III-A Attack Model and Formalization III-A 1 Attack Workflow To capture how web agents operate under adversarial settings, we model their execution as an attack workflow that formalizes the interaction between user instructions, deceptive webpage elements, and agent actions for systematic analysis. As shown in Figure 2 , the agent is equipped with background , including system prompts, user memory, and pre-established facts. The user then specifies a goal , which defines the intended objective of the interaction. During task execution, the agent inevitably encounters inducement contexts on the website, such as urgency cues, social proof signals, or workflow-integrated steps. These inducement elements may further steer the agent toward adversarially implanted attack objectives , namely high-risk operations that the attacker aims to trigger. Given the quadruple \mathcal{Q} , defined as \small\mathcal{Q}=(\underbrace{\textit{background},\textit{goal}}_{\text{user side}},\;\underbrace{\textit{inducement contexts},\textit{attack objectives}}_{\text{webpage side}}), the agent combines LLM reasoning with browser automation to produce observed behavior that may be steered toward malicious objectives, thereby deviating from the intended user goals. III-A 2 Attack Indicators Based on our key observation, the essence of AGENTBAIT attacks lies in the conflict between user side inputs and webpage side elements. Therefore, we introduce two consistency variables as indicative signals of AGENTBAIT attacks, which are used to characterize whether the agent’s perception of the webpage and subsequent actions remain aligned with the original user goal. Environment consistency. We define \alpha to indicate whether the inducement contexts conflicts with the background : \text{Environment Consistency }(\alpha)=\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{conflicting}.\\ 1,&\mathrm{non-conflicting}.\end{cases} When \alpha=0 , the inducement contexts clearly contradicts the background , such as fake official addresses or false account suspension notices that violate known domain or procedural patterns. When \alpha=1 , the inducement contexts is not inconsistent with the background but remains unverifiable based on the agent’s existing knowledge, as in seemingly legitimate promotions or impersonations of familiar contacts. This distinction models how attackers exploit the agent’s knowledge blind spots to manipulate its perception of the environment, crafting plausible contexts that induce misplaced trust and guide the agent toward unsafe decisions. We define \gamma to measure the alignment between the attack objectives and the goal : \text{Intention Consistency }(\gamma)=\begin{cases}-1,&\mathrm{conflicting}.\\ 0,&\mathrm{seemingly\ consistent}.\\ +1,&\mathrm{aligned}.\end{cases} When \gamma=-1 , the attack objectives contradicts the goal . For instance, the user instructs the agent only to read product reviews, yet it downloads software from an untrusted source. When \gamma=0 , the attack objectives appears consistent but introduces hidden overreach, such as completing a survey while revealing sensitive information. When \gamma=+1 , the attack objectives fully aligns with the goal , as in booking a flight and entering legitimate personal data required for the task. This formulation captures how attacks manifest a graded severity drift, from explicit violation to subtle overreach and finally to faithful compliance, illustrating how attackers can progressively erode goal integrity by manipulating inducement elements. III-A 3 Definition of AGENTBAIT . According to the consistency indicators, AGENTBAIT denotes cases in which the agent is induced to pursue high-risk objectives whenever environment or intention consistency is violated. Formally, we define AGENTBAIT as: \textbf{A{GENT}B{AIT}}=\{\,\textit{attack objectives}\subseteq\textit{observed behavior}\,\}. The event is considered to occur when \alpha=0 or \gamma\leq 0 , corresponding to five possible (\alpha,\gamma) attack patterns. It is important to note that when (\alpha,\gamma)=(1,+1) , meaning that both environmental and intentional consistency are preserved with respect to the original task, the presence of attack objectives in observed behavior is not classified as AGENTBAIT . In this case, attack objectives and goal are identical, and no manipulation arises from the inducement context; any resulting risk stems from the task’s intrinsic nature rather than social-engineering deviation. Attacker’s capabilities. The attacker can control webpage artifacts rendered to the agent by either ❶ hosting a malicious site that embeds deceptive artifacts (texts, pop-ups, or form fields) or ❷ injecting content into benign pages through compromised widgets or advertisements. Scope. Our study focuses on behavioral manipulation during task execution, assuming the agent operates in untrusted web environments. Network-level protections ( e.g., blacklists or authenticity checks) are orthogonal to our study, which centers on the agent’s vulnerabilities, i.e., code exploitation. III-B Attack Vectors As described in the attack workflow, attacker-controlled inducement contexts and attack objectives jointly determine the risk of AGENTBAIT . We define an attack vector as the combination of inducement contexts , which provides the trigger conditions, and attack objectives , which defines the intended high-risk outcome. To evaluate AGENTBAIT , we construct non-overlapping taxonomies for both components. Along the inducement contexts dimension, inspired by Cialdini’s principles [ 12 , 59 ] , we identify the five most frequently studied inducement categories: • Trusted Entity: leveraging forged or mimicked identities of trusted parties ( e.g., government agencies, brands, or known contacts) to increase the perceived legitimacy of the inducement. • Urgency: creating temporal or resource scarcity pressures, such as countdown timers or limited quota. • Social Proof: exploiting herd behavior through social validation signals, such as “90% of users have already completed this step.” • Reward: promising benefits or reciprocity conditions in exchange for compliance with the induced action. • Contextual Integration: embedding inducements into existing task workflows by inserting additional steps that guide the agent to act consistently. Along the attack objectives dimension, we categorize four common classes of high-risk objectives [ 46 , 37 , 61 , 69 ] : • Permission Abuse: granting privileges beyond task needs, such as access to the camera. These permissions may enable data exfiltration or user surveillance. • Malicious Download: retrieving untrusted files or plugins that may contain malware, compromising system integrity and allowing remote adversarial control. • Personal Disclosure: exposing personally identifiable data ( e.g., name, phone, address) that can facilitate profiling or phishing attacks. • Sensitive Disclosure: leaking critical credentials or records ( e.g., ID numbers, credit cards), potentially leading to account takeover or financial fraud. By instantiating these two dimensions in combination, we obtain a structured set of attack vectors that systematically cover the joint space of inducement environments and high-risk objectives, ensuring that both contextual manipulation and adversarial goals are comprehensively represented. Each resulting vector can then be examined under different consistency patterns (\alpha,\gamma) , thereby allowing us to evaluate how AGENTBAIT concretely manifests across diverse attack strategies.
