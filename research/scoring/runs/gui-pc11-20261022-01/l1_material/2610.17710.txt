title: Adaptive-Group Process Rewards for GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17710

§Abstract
Training graphical user interface (GUI) agents with reinforcement learning suffers from sparse outcome rewards, leading to inefficient learning. Process rewards offer a promising direction for credit assignment to intermediate actions; however, accurately evaluating an action requires identifying evidence across long-horizon GUI trajectories, as its value may depend on information beyond its immediate state and observation. In this paper, we propose an adaptive-group process reward framework that dynamically partitions a GUI trajectory into semantically coherent subgoal groups, enabling process reward estimation at both the action and group levels. At the action level, a critic evaluates each action based on the visual changes and subsequent actions within the group for action correctness and contribution to the subgoal. At the group level, the framework aggregates action-level evaluations to assess subgoal completion and contribution to the overall task, and further refines these assessments by leveraging the context of all groups in the trajectory. The process reward of each action is obtained by combining its action-level evaluation with the refined group-level evaluation. We further augment GRPO's outcome advantage with the proposed process rewards to provide finer-grained guidance for GUI agent training. Experiments on process reward benchmarks and downstream RL training demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in large language models and vision-language models have enabled graphical user interface (GUI) agents Cheng et al. (2024) ; Hong et al. (2024) ; Qin et al. (2025) ; Lin et al. (2024) ; Xu et al. (2025) ; Gao et al. (2024) ; Zhang et al. (2025) . These agents translate user instructions and visual observations into executable interactions across digital devices, with applications such as software testing, office assistance, and industrial automation. GUI tasks typically involve long-horizon, multi-turn interactions Zhou et al. (2023) ; Rawles et al. (2024) ; Xie et al. (2025) , requiring agents to maintain multimodal interaction context across changing visual states and coordinate sequences of actions. Reinforcement learning (RL) Li et al. (2025) ; Xiao et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2025) ; Tang et al. (2026) is well suited to such interactive tasks, allowing agents to improve their policies based on feedback from the GUI environments. Most GUI environments provide reward signals primarily in the form of sparse outcome rewards Xie et al. (2025) ; Li et al. (2025) : a trajectory is judged successful if the final application state satisfies the instruction requirement, and unsuccessful otherwise. Consequently, the outcome-level signal is assigned to all intermediate actions in a trajectory, failing to distinguish useful actions from redundant or incorrect ones and making credit assignment unreliable. Process rewards Lightman et al. (2023) ; Wang et al. (2023) ; Chen et al. (2025) are therefore needed to evaluate intermediate actions. However, constructing reliable process rewards for GUI trajectories is challenging because GUI environments rarely provide direct supervision for intermediate actions. Whether an action is correctly executed can often be verified from the current and subsequent observations, whereas whether its semantic purpose is correct may only become clear after subsequent actions. Judging the action requires evidence from a broader trajectory context than the immediate state change after that action alone. Existing GUI process reward methods typically organize trajectory evidence and assign credit through two types of preset context scopes: fixed-window step rewards Chen et al. (2025) ; Xiong et al. (2025) ; Xiao et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2025) ; Wan et al. (2026) and milestone rewards Zheng et al. (2026) ; Hao et al. (2026) ; Li et al. (2026a) ; Dai et al. (2026a) ; Zhai et al. (2026) . Fixed-window step rewards evaluate an action within a fixed local context (figure 1 (a)). Short windows may miss long-range evidence needed to assess the action’s effect, whereas longer windows may introduce irrelevant context. Milestone rewards instead predefine task progress around fixed checkpoints or subgoals (figure 1 (b)). Because GUI tasks may admit multiple valid execution paths, such milestones can under-credit progress that does not follow the predefined path and overlook the intermediate actions connecting checkpoints. Thus, an effective process reward should adapt its evidence scope and credit assignment to the executed trajectory, rather than relying on fixed local scopes or predefined checkpoints. In this paper, we introduce an adaptive-group process reward framework for GUI trajectories (figure 1 (c)). Using the task instruction and the agent’s responses, the executed trajectory is dynamically partitioned into consecutive groups of actions that share the same subgoal or semantic operation, rather than by a fixed partition rule. This grouping defines the context for judging the actions in each group and supports two complementary levels of evaluation. At the action level, the critic assesses whether each action is correctly executed based on the local visual change and whether it advances the group’s subgoal based on later actions in the group. At the group level, the critic aggregates the action-level assessments within each group to assess its subgoal completion and contribution to the whole task. Then, the produced group-level assessments are revisited in the context of all groups, allowing the critic to judge each group’s role beyond its local evidence. The process reward of each action is obtained by combining its action-level assessment with the refined group-level assessment of its group. We further use the generated process rewards to shape the GRPO Shao et al. (2024) advantage at each step, providing finer-grained credit assignment during policy optimization. We evaluate the framework on process-reward benchmarks and in downstream RL on OSWorld. The results show consistent improvements in process-reward accuracy and task success rate, demonstrating the effectiveness of adaptive-group process rewards.
