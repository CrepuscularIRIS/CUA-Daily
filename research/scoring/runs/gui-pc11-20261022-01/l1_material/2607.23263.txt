title: SeekJudge: A Practical Reward Framework for Reinforcement Learning in Computer-Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23263

§Abstract
Deciding whether a trajectory actually fulfills its instruction determines how we measure computer-use agents on long-horizon graphical-user-interface tasks and how we train them with reinforcement learning (RL). This judgment has long relied on rule-based evaluation. Rule-based evaluation often disagrees with human intention and becomes outdated when an app updates or its online content drifts. Existing model-based judges attempt to address these problems, but their judging accuracy remains limited. We propose the \textbf{SeekJudge} framework, whose four agents, a Condense, a Ground, a Seek and an Analyze agent, reach a verdict through a Seek--Analyze loop over the trajectory. To train a specialized model on densely labeled trajectories, we propose a seed-driven distillation pipeline that expands a few human-labeled seed trajectories into $10$K such trajectories. To evaluate step-level judgments, we build CUAStepBench, a human-annotated benchmark that pairs trajectory verdicts with dense step labels. Beyond accuracy, SeekJudge costs far less than a closed-source large model. We further propose rollout overlap, a reward-server design that reduces the overhead of a reward model in RL training. To our knowledge, SeekJudge is the first model-based reward to match or surpass native rule-based reward in online RL, measured by downstream success rate on held-out RL test goals. In offline judging, SeekJudge-9B also exceeds rule-based evaluation by $9.5$ F1 on AgentRewardBench.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Computer-use agents (CUAs) have moved from constrained synthetic environments such as MiniWoB ( Shi et al., 2017 ) to open-ended tasks across web, mobile and desktop platforms ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Deng et al., 2023 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Rawles et al., 2025 ) , and their trajectories have grown from tens of steps to hundreds of steps ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) . Deciding whether the long trajectory an actor produces fulfills the instruction determines how we measure a model and how well downstream reinforcement learning or rejection fine-tuning works. For a long time, judging a trajectory has relied mainly on rule-based evaluation ( Xie et al., 2024 ; Rawles et al., 2025 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) . Rule-based evaluation suffers from three problems. (1) A rule disagrees with human intention. Many valid answers can satisfy the same goal, yet a rule cannot enumerate them all ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) . The extreme case is an open-ended instruction such as drawing a picture, where acceptable outputs are unbounded and no rule can judge the result. On an existing CUA benchmark, a blind human re-check of every trajectory where rule-based evaluation and a Codex judge give opposite verdicts agrees with the judge more often than with the rule, as Figure 1 (a) shows. (2) A rule needs a dedicated parser per application and breaks across application versions. A rule reads internal state through layout or internal APIs, so each new application needs its own parser or a mock app, and the parser stops working once the application moves to a new version. (3) A rule’s ground-truth answer becomes outdated. Even when the application stays the same, the content of a live website keeps changing. A rule relies on a predefined answer, so once that content changes the answer drifts or the task becomes unsolvable, for example when a task asks for the CEO of a company and the company later appoints a new one. Covering live online services therefore incurs a heavy maintenance cost ( Xue et al., 2025 ) . These problems of rules motivate a shift from rule-based to model-based evaluation, but existing frameworks have not yet made this paradigm mainstream, because their judging accuracy remains limited ( Xue et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Rosset et al., 2026 ) . These frameworks put many images into one context while only a small high-fidelity fragment determines the verdict, and thus they may overlook the decisive evidence. Even though the decisive image is always present, performance degrades increasingly with the number of trajectory images added to the same forward pass, as Figure 1 (b) shows. We decompose judging a trajectory into two subtasks: localization, which identifies the image holding the decisive evidence, and extraction, which reads the decisive detail from that image accurately. Based on this decomposition, we propose SeekJudge , a multi-agent model-based judge that separates the two subtasks. Its Seek agent localizes on a text timeline, and its Analyze agent extracts the decisive detail from one image at a time. Its Condense agent records the state transition of each step and its Ground agent identifies the element that each action hits, which together build this timeline from the trajectory. SeekJudge also has the fine-grained scoring, low cost and low latency that a reward model inside RL needs: • On judging quality , SeekJudge returns a continuous score for the trajectory and classifies every step, which gives a graded reward and step-level judgments. • On efficiency , the whole system is a single 9 B open-source model, hundreds of times cheaper than a closed-source large model, and its peak context stays 4 – 6\times smaller than that of existing model-based judges. Rollout overlap further runs part of the judging while the environment executes actions, so the reward model adds less time to the rollout. In summary, our contributions are as follows. (1) We propose SeekJudge, a multi-agent framework that separates localization from extraction, together with rollout overlap that makes it faster inside reinforcement learning. (2) We construct CUAStepBench, a CUA reward benchmark that pairs human trajectory verdicts with dense step labels, spanning 177 applications across platforms. (3) We train SeekJudge-9B for the framework. To our knowledge, it is the first model-based reward to match or surpass native rule-based reward on downstream test success in online RL. It also exceeds existing model-based judges on offline reward benchmarks.
