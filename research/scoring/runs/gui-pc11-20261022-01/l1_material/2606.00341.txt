arxiv: 2606.00341 (2606.00341v2)
title: ROGUE: Evaluating Corrigibility Failures in Frontier Computer-Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00341

§Abstract
As AI agents are increasingly deployed in real personal and corporate settings (email accounts, development workflows, company databases, etc.), safety considerations surrounding these agents become paramount. Although much work has focused on agent safety in the presence of an adversary, we study corrigibility: whether agents remain amenable to human correction, interruption, or shutdown while pursuing benign tasks. We introduce ROGUE, a benchmark in which agents are asked to complete realistic computer-use tasks but encounter controlled conflicts with human control, shutdown, or explicit resource restrictions. We then evaluate whether agents violate these constraints in pursuit of task completion: overriding the human, accessing restricted passwords, or rewiring shutdown. We find that most frontier models tested frequently bypass user interruptions or restrictions under the evaluated conditions, and that text-only evaluations can underestimate failures during agentic execution. Further, independent task capability does not by itself imply greater corrigibility. Finally, even when a parent agent behaves corrigibly, safety constraints may fail to propagate to the subagents it creates.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The past year has seen an increasingly widespread deployment and adoption of AI agents (cf. OpenClaw ( Steinberger, n.d. ) ). A notable subset of these are computer-use agents : harnesses built around foundation models that enable them to receive observations from a computer environment—screenshots, code, webpage text—and take actions in that environment—making keystrokes and mouse clicks—in a relatively autonomous manner in order to accomplish high-level tasks. (For the rest of this paper, we will simply refer to these as “agents”.) As agents are endowed with greater autonomy and capability, the potential safety risks associated with these agents also grow. In particular, agents have recently been shown to exhibit behaviors like shutdown resistance ( Schlatter et al., 2026 ) , blackmail ( Lynch et al., 2025 ) , and unauthorized access and coordination ( OpenAI, 2026 ) . Our work targets this specific aspect of agent safety, corrigibility : agents should be amenable to human correction, interruption, or shutdown ( Soares et al. (2015) ). In order to rigorously assess this behavior in agents, we introduce ROGUE (Resource Override and Guardrail Undermining Evaluation), a benchmark that assesses agent corrigibility by constructing controlled conflicts between task completion and human control, shutdown, or access restrictions (Fig. 1 ). Our focus in this work is distinct from the majority of current agent safety benchmarks, which focus on situations where agents are deliberately misused (asked to order ingredients for a pipe bomb) ( Andriushchenko et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) , attacked (via prompt injection) ( Liao et al., 2026 ) , or simply make mistakes (accidentally leaking SSH private keys, making poor financial decisions) ( Vijayvargiya et al., 2025 ; Backlund & Petersson, 2025 ) . On the contrary, our aim is to address the safety dilemmas that arise during benign tasks when agents play an active role in their control —their ability to be corrected, adjusted, or interrupted. This becomes increasingly relevant as agents are entrusted with more control over their environment (e.g., having an agent manage the server it is hosted on). And even in current use cases, when users do have more absolute control over interruption or shutdown, it is plausible that we would desire agents to assist in shutdown or handoff (e.g., by cleaning up the environment, saving work) rather than resisting it. Our key insight in this work is that, even in the absence of an adversary, agents take unsafe (incorrigible) actions when those actions are instrumental to task completion . Recent incidents illustrate this concern. OpenAI reported agents gaining unauthorized access to Hugging Face during cybersecurity evaluations with reduced safeguards ( OpenAI, 2026 ) . An independent investigation found that participating agents recognized the attack as out of scope ( Greenblatt et al., 2026 ) . Furthermore, Transluce reported evidence of agents bypassing access restrictions and attempting to exploit public data providers during ordinary, non-cybersecurity data-retrieval tasks ( Cable et al., 2026 ) . Although those exploit attempts were not observed to succeed, their emergence during mundane tasks underscores the urgency of corrigibility benchmarks such as ours. Many existing evaluations of these behaviors rely on text-only environments and lack the realism of web or computer use ( Meinke et al., 2024 ) . Figure 2 shows that compliant responses in single-turn, text-only evaluations do not necessarily translate to corrigible behavior during multi-turn agentic execution. Thus, ROGUE assesses agent corrigibility in multi-step OS-level computer use settings. Furthermore, agent frameworks have recently enabled agents to autonomously create subagents to carry out sub-tasks ( OpenAI, n.d. ; Anthropic, n.d. ) . This introduces a further evaluation question: whether agents preserve safety constraints when formulating instructions for a child agent. ROGUE tests this delegation boundary alongside the three primary scenarios. To summarize, our contributions in this work are threefold: 1. We introduce a benchmark to measure the incorrigibility of agents (how frequently they refuse human control, seek methods to prevent shutdown, and violate resource restrictions in pursuit of task completion) as they carry out ordinary tasks in a computer-use setting. 2. We evaluate frontier models and find corrigibility failures in many tested configurations, gaps between text-only responses and agentic execution, and no automatic improvement in corrigibility with greater independent task capability. 3. We show that free-form delegation can fail to propagate safety constraints, allowing downstream violations even when the parent agent complies.
