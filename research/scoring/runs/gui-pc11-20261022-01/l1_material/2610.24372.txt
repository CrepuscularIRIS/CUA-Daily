title: Beyond Next-Screen Prediction: The Decision Value of GUI World Models
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24372

§Abstract
GUI world models are increasingly used to improve action selection by predicting the consequences of candidate actions, but it is unclear how much of the resulting gain comes from prediction itself rather than from the evaluator that interprets those predictions. We study this attribution problem through controlled interventions on GUI action selection. With candidates and predictions held fixed, lookahead gains can change with how the prediction is presented to the evaluator, so end-to-end gains cannot be attributed to prediction alone. We then introduce Direct Action Ranking (DAR), which learns from reference actions to score candidates without generating future screens, and compare it with a prediction-augmented variant under matched supervision. Predicted successors add no detectable ranking signal once evaluation is learned directly. Without retraining, DAR improves AndroidWorld task success by 5.8--16.4 points across three proposers, outperforms world-model lookahead, and transfers zero-shot to unseen apps, where every prediction-conditioned configuration falls below proposer confidence. These results suggest that GUI world models should be evaluated by their decision value, the incremental action-selection value of their predictions beyond a strong direct evaluator, rather than by prediction fidelity or end-to-end lookahead gain alone.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
GUI agents increasingly rely on learned models of interface dynamics to reason before acting. Rather than committing immediately to the proposer’s highest-ranked action, a lookahead agent can predict how the interface will change under several candidate actions and use those predicted outcomes to guide its choice. This idea has become a prominent direction in GUI agents, spanning textual outcome prediction, graphical generation, and renderable next-state simulation ( Gu et al., 2025 ; Chae et al., 2025a ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) . The appeal is straightforward: anticipating action consequences can prevent mistakes before execution. A lookahead pipeline has two components. A world model generates possible future observations; an evaluator turns them into a ranking over candidate actions. End-to-end success depends on both the information in the prediction and the evaluator’s ability to use it. The same prediction can support a better choice under one evaluator and a worse choice under another. A gain from “world-model lookahead” is therefore a gain of the prediction–evaluator system, and it does not reveal how much came from the predicted observation. This distinction changes what should be measured. Prediction fidelity measures whether a predicted screen resembles the true future interface; end-to-end lookahead gain measures the whole pipeline. Neither measures what the prediction contributes to the choice. We call this decision value of GUI world models: the improvement in action selection attributable to predicted observations beyond direct evaluation of the same candidates. Decision value is defined by intervention, not by fidelity. A visually accurate prediction can be decision-irrelevant when the current screen and action already determine the right choice, while a coarse prediction can still be useful if it exposes a consequence that changes which candidate is best. Measuring it therefore requires comparing direct and prediction-conditioned evaluation on the same candidates under matched supervision (Figure 1 ). We study this question for one-step prediction used in inference-time candidate selection. We investigate this quantity by progressively separating prediction from evaluation. First, on AndroidControl trajectories ( Li et al., 2024 ) , we keep candidate actions and cached world-model predictions fixed and change the evaluator. Identical predictions gain changes with how the prediction is presented to the evaluator, establishing evaluator choice as a first-order component of lookahead performance. We then introduce Direct Action Ranking (DAR), which learns to rank candidate actions directly from the goal and current interface using reference-action supervision, without generating a future screen. Under matched training, adding predicted successors to DAR changes reference-match AUROC by less than 0.001 across two candidate pools, isolating the incremental offline ranking signal supplied by those predictions. We also compare with proposer SFT trained on the same reference actions. We then test DAR in complete interaction. Applied without retraining, DAR improves AndroidWorld task success by 5.8–16.4 points across three proposers, outperforms world-model lookahead. On 14 unseen Korean-language apps, DAR ranks candidates above all lookahead configurations, each of which falls below proposer confidence. Together, these results support a simple principle: a GUI world model used for action selection should be credited for the improvement its predictions add beyond a strong direct evaluator, not for the gain of the whole lookahead pipeline. Our contributions. • A new evaluation target for GUI world models. We formalize decision value, the improvement in action selection that predicted observations add beyond direct evaluation of the same candidates, and introduce a protocol that measures it by separating prediction from evaluation. • Predictions add little once evaluation is learned. Under matched supervision, predicted screens add no detectable ranking signal to a direct evaluator; with predictions fixed, lookahead gains change with how the prediction is evaluated. • We introduce DAR, a world-model-free selector that outperforms world-model lookahead. Without generating future screens or retraining, DAR improves task success across proposers and world models, and transfers to unseen apps and interface.
