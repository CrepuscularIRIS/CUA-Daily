title: Latent World Action Model for GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14228

§Abstract
Pixel-space world models have recently emerged as a promising approach for GUI agents, predicting future screens prior to execution and subsequently re-perceiving them for action selection. However, this two-stage paradigm faces two key challenges in GUI environments: (L1) the high computational cost of pixel reconstruction and (L2) the need to reconstruct large and diverse appearance-level details beyond what is required for action selection. In practice, this screen-generation objective can induce an identity shortcut problem, where future-state prediction largely preserves the current state rather than capturing action-induced changes. In this work, we reformulate GUI world modeling by shifting the prediction target from full-screen reconstruction to action-induced future information in latent space. Concretely, we leverage a large multimodal model as the latent world model, whose shared representations support two dedicated heads for both action selection and predictive transition modeling. The action head directly translates these latent representations into action selection, while the transition head captures action-conditioned future information. To mitigate the identity shortcut, we further introduce an action-aware contrastive objective that encourages the transition representations to capture discriminative action-induced changes. Extensive experiments across offline and online GUI settings show that our method achieves up to 31.9% and 40.6% relative gains in success rate over pixel-space world models, respectively, while substantially reducing inference latency by up to 98.9%. Our code is available at https://github.com/sjyu001/latent2world.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advancements in large multimodal models (LMMs) have accelerated the development of graphical user interface (GUI) agents ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Rawles et al., 2023 ; Lee et al., 2024 ; Gou et al., 2024 ) —an increasingly indispensable technology in the modern digital era. These agents are designed to complete complex, user-specified tasks on mobile platforms by automatically executing sequences of actions. By reducing manual interaction, they facilitate mobile app use across diverse real-world domains, ranging from commercial services such as e-commerce ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) to accessibility support for people with visual or motor impairments and older adults ( Huq et al., 2026 ) . Among such efforts, recent works ( Xu et al., 2026 ) have explored world models that simulate the consequences of actions proposed by GUI agents ( e.g. , button clicks and scrolling) prior to direct execution. Such foresight enables GUI agents to anticipate potential failures and adjust their strategies accordingly, improving robustness in long-tail and irreversible scenarios ( e.g. , payments and account deletion), as well as in long-horizon tasks involving extended action sequences. One promising approach is visual-space prediction ( Zheng et al., 2026 ; Luo et al., 2026 ) , where GUI world models forecast a corresponding next GUI state for each candidate action in a visually renderable form ( e.g. , a screenshot or renderable HTML). These predicted future states are then re-perceived by a separate action-scoring model, which selects the candidate that best advances the task goal. This two-stage “Render-and-Re-Perceive” pipeline provides explicit spatial and visual information, such as element locations and layouts, that can support grounded GUI action planning beyond relatively concise textual descriptions ( Gu et al., 2025 ) . However, explicitly reconstructing future screens introduces an expensive and unnecessarily broad intermediate task into action selection. (L1) Autoregressive or diffusion-based screen synthesis incurs substantial computational latency, posing a bottleneck for responsive GUI interaction (Figure 1 ). Moreover, (L2) GUI transitions exhibit large and varied appearance changes (Figure 2 ), requiring pixel-space world models to capture diverse future appearances. Yet, action selection does not require reconstructing all such visual details, but rather understanding the future consequence of each action. In practice, pixel-space world models may retain the current screen despite substantial next-screen changes (Figure 4 ), a behavior we term an identity shortcut . We hypothesize that full-screen reconstruction encourages reusing current-screen information instead of modeling action-induced changes. This distinction raises the question: how can a GUI world model learn future information useful for action selection without reconstructing the future screen? Our key insight is that GUI world modeling should focus on future information that helps distinguish which candidate action best advances the task goal. This perspective requires connecting what an action will cause with whether that outcome is useful for the task. Training on annotated actions teaches a model which action to select, but does not explicitly require it to anticipate the resulting state. Conversely, learning to predict observed next states provides information about action consequences, but does not directly teach the model how to use that information for action selection. We therefore jointly train shared representations for both candidate-action scoring and action-conditioned future-state prediction. The predictive objective encourages these representations to capture action-induced future information, while the action-selection objective connects them to task-relevant decisions. Accordingly, we implement the predictive objective in latent space, enabling the model to learn from future states without reconstructing their full visual appearance. Specifically, given the task goal, current GUI screenshot, action history, and candidate actions proposed by a frozen GUI agent, we employ a LoRA-adapted LMM to produce action-specific hidden representations. A lightweight action head maps each representation to a scalar score, and the resulting candidate distribution is trained against the ground-truth one-hot distribution identifying the annotated action. In parallel, a transition head predicts a next-state visual embedding from the shared representation of the observed action, using an embedding of the actual next screenshot as its target. However, simply minimizing the distance to this target may allow the previously observed identity shortcut . We therefore train the transition head with an action-aware contrastive objective, aligning its prediction with the next-state embedding while separating it from the current-state embedding and embeddings of unrelated transitions.
