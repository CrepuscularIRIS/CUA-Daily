title: Q-Reflector: Learning GUI Action Reflection with Generated Conditions
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05587

§Abstract
Mobile GUI agents require accurate action reflection for reliable long-horizon execution, assessing whether each action was successfully executed and whether its outcome constitutes a valid step toward the task goal. When evaluated as standalone reflectors under a common prompt, frontier VLMs and specialized GUI models exhibit imbalanced recognition of valid and invalid transitions. We propose Q-Reflector, a standalone reflector based on Qwen3-VL-8B that learns GUI action reflection as supervised structured prediction. Given task context, the executed action, and paired screenshots, it generates pre- and post-conditions specifying the action's prerequisites and intended local effect, then assesses execution success and task validity through a structured rationale and binary verdict. Training combines supervised fine-tuning, teacher-student distillation, and reward- and preference-based refinement. On 1,082 manually verified AndroidWorld transitions, Q-Reflector achieves 82.16% overall accuracy, exceeding all evaluated baselines and showing more balanced performance than GPT-5.2 and GUI-Owl-1.5-8B. On 117 MobileWorld tasks, Q-Reflector achieves task success rates of 46.15% with GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct and 35.90% with MAI-UI-8B, improving over no external reflection by 17.09 and 14.53 percentage points, respectively. Under the same condition-generation setting, it outperforms the strongest reflection baselines, GPT-5.2 and Claude Haiku 4.5, by 3.42 and 5.98 percentage points, respectively.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Mobile GUI agents built on vision–language models (VLMs) can navigate applications and carry out multi-step workflows through visual perception and low-level interaction ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . Reliable execution also requires judging whether an action succeeds and whether its resulting transition constitutes a valid step toward the task goal. Action reflection guides the agent’s decision to continue, retry, recover, or stop. Accepting an invalid transition can allow execution to continue despite a failed or task-irrelevant action, while rejecting a valid transition can trigger unnecessary recovery or interrupt useful progress. When reflection runs as a separate call after each action, its inference cost accumulates over a long task ( Li et al., 2025 ) . Figure 1 contrasts useful preparation with an ineffective action: a valid step need not finish the task, but it must achieve a useful local effect. Action reflection requires more than identifying changes between screenshots. A visible change may result from a successfully executed but task-irrelevant action. Conversely, a valid intermediate step may prepare for a later action or recover from an error without completing the task. Opening a date picker, for example, is a valid preparatory step in booking an appointment. The reflector must determine what local effect the action should produce, whether that effect occurred, and whether it constitutes a valid task step. Existing approaches include prompted reflection modules ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) and GUI models trained to verify action outcomes and recover from execution errors ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) . GUI-Owl-1.5’s training pipeline, for example, synthesizes reflection on action outcomes to guide subsequent action generation ( Xu et al., 2026 ) . When evaluated as standalone reflectors under a common prompt, both frontier VLMs and specialized GUI models exhibit imbalanced recognition of valid and invalid transitions. On our 1,082 manually verified AndroidWorld transitions ( Rawles et al., 2025 ) , GPT-5.2 achieves 95.10% negative-class accuracy but only 51.39% positive-class accuracy. Under the same reflection prompt, GUI-Owl-1.5-8B shows the opposite pattern, with 92.33% positive-class accuracy and 57.78% negative-class accuracy. Reliable reflection therefore requires both accepting valid intermediate steps and rejecting failed or task-invalid ones. We formulate GUI action reflection as supervised structured prediction. The reflector first generates action-specific preconditions and postconditions, specifying the action’s prerequisites and intended local effect. It is instructed to derive these conditions from the task context, action, recent history, and screenshot before execution. It then checks the observed transition against the conditions and separately assesses task validity through a structured rationale before producing a binary decision. Training supervises the conditions, rationale, and decision together. We introduce Q-Reflector , a compact standalone reflector based on Qwen3-VL-8B ( Bai et al., 2025a ) . Given the task goal, current action, recent action history, and paired screenshots, it generates the conditions, rationale, and decision within a single post-action call. When a transition is judged invalid, the action-generating model receives feedback extracted from the rationale. We train Q-Reflector on 880 manually reviewed transitions collected by Agent-SAMA from Mobile-Eval-E and SPA-Bench ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025b ) , using supervised fine-tuning, teacher–student distillation, and reward- and preference-based refinement. Q-Reflector is added to the execution loop as a separate reflection module, with the action-generating model kept fixed. On the AndroidWorld transitions, Q-Reflector achieves 82.16% overall accuracy, exceeding GPT-5.2 by 11.83 percentage points given the same transition information. Its positive- and negative-class accuracies are 86.79% and 76.12%, respectively. These results show more balanced performance across positive and negative transitions than GPT-5.2 and GUI-Owl-1.5-8B. We further evaluate Q-Reflector on 117 MobileWorld tasks ( Kong et al., 2026 ) using GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct ( Xu et al., 2026 ) and MAI-UI-8B ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) as action-generating models. Q-Reflector achieves task success rates of 46.15% and 35.90%, improving over no external reflection by 17.09 and 14.53 percentage points, respectively. Under the same condition-generation setting, it outperforms the strongest reflection baselines, GPT-5.2 and Claude Haiku 4.5, by 3.42 and 5.98 percentage points, respectively. Compared with a separately trained variant using the same data and training pipeline but omitting condition generation, Q-Reflector completes five additional tasks with GUI-Owl-1.5-8B-Instruct and seven with MAI-UI-8B.
