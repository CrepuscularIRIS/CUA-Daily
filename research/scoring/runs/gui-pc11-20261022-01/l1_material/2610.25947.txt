arxiv: 2610.25947 (2610.25947v1)
title: Claim Laundering: When Agent Decision Models Trust Records Over Reality
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25947

§Abstract
Agent decision models select actions in a single forward pass from inputs that combine environmental observations with the agent's own record of previous actions. When an action fails silently, these sources can conflict: the record reports success, but the environment shows that the step did not take effect. We ask which source determines the next decision. Across Android screen traces, ALFWorld, and WebShop, we construct paired states that independently vary the observation, the logged action, and an explicit success claim. In all 12 configurations of four decision models and three domains, records have a larger average effect on commitment decisions than observations; after claimed-but-failed steps, the models still stop, advance, or purchase in 53-100% of cases. Yet the relevant evidence is often represented internally: linear probes on intermediate hidden states distinguish successful from failed steps on held-out apps, tasks, and goals (AUROC 0.77-1.00), and activation patching reveals increasing influence from records in later layers. We use this signal in EVIGATE, an intermediate-layer veto that requires no additional forward pass and adds 0.04-2.0% serving latency. Under injected silent failures, EVIGATE improves task success by 18.7 points in live ALFWorld agents, 5.0 in WebShop, and 5.1 in a phone simulator, and in WebShop and the simulator it also outperforms random vetoes at the same rate. Its effectiveness depends on representative calibration states: gates calibrated on offline counterfactual states do not transfer reliably to live agent trajectories.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Agents spend most of their inference on small, repeated decisions: stop or continue, buy or keep looking, move on or retry. A new class of System-One decision models is built for exactly this workload TypeSafe AI (2026) ; Palmer (2026) ; Marosi (2026) ; Convai Innovations (2026) . Given a state and a typed question, such a model returns a probability for every predefined option in a single forward pass, at a fraction of the latency and cost of LLM generation. Jev is already served through OpenRouter OpenRouter (2026) and Cloudflare Workers AI and integrated with LangChain and Pydantic AI. The problem: records can disagree with the world. A decision model does not watch the environment. It reads a state that the agent assembles: the goal, a record of what the agent did (LLM-written step summaries in T3A, ReAct thoughts in ALFWorld, logged clicks in WebShop) and the current observation. The record and the world can diverge. Actions fail silently: a tap misses, a click does not register, a command returns “Nothing happens.”. And records are written from the action’s intent , often before its effect can be checked. A 9B summarizer given T3A’s own prompt, which asks it to “be critical”, still reports success after 39% of actions that had no effect (§ 4 ). When the record and the observation disagree, the observation is right. A reliable decision layer must notice the mismatch before it takes an irreversible step. One that follows the record instead turns the agent’s claim into the decision; we call this claim laundering (Figure 1 ). This matters in practice. Replacing an LLM action selector with Jev cut the cost of every decision but lowered end-to-end success on AndroidWorld by 13–18 points, with premature completion among the leading causes Ling et al. (2026b) . Across LLM agents, 45–76% of failures on \tau^{2} -bench and AppWorld are false successes , where the agent reports completion the environment does not show Advani (2026) . These studies describe the symptom. We ask where it comes from in the component that makes the decision, and how to detect it. What we do. We answer four questions, each in its own section. 1. Do decision models detect record–state mismatches? (§ 4 ) We build paired states that change only the record or only the observation, in three domains, and test seven open decision models, hosted Jev and their base LLMs. They do not: the record moves the decision more than the observation for all four models tested in every domain, and for six of the seven open models on phones. 2. Does the model know better than it says? (§ 5 ) Yes. A linear probe on the hidden state detects whether the step worked, often where the model’s own output is near chance. 3. Why does the output ignore it? (§ 6 ) Activation patching shows the observation reaching the decision position by mid-depth, while record-dependent influence becomes dominant in the last layers. 4. Can we use this to detect mismatches? (§ 7 –§ 8 ) EviGate reads the middle layer and vetoes unsupported stop, proceed or buy decisions. We test it offline and in three live agents, against random vetoing at the same rate. We then ask whether simply fine-tuning the model would be enough (§ 9 ). Contributions. • A failure and how to measure it. We define record–state mismatch for agent decision layers and measure it with counterfactual pairs in three domains. After a failed step whose record reports success, the models still stop, proceed or buy in 53–100% of cases. The bias is already present in the base LLM, and decision tuning does not remove it. • Where it happens. The mismatch is encoded in the hidden state (probe AUROC 0.77–1.00 on held-out groups) and outweighed by record-dependent influence in the last layers. • A detector that works in live agents. EviGate costs one dot product and raises task success in all three domains, provided it is calibrated on states from the deployed agent itself. Fine-tuning removes the bias too, but only in the domains it is trained on.
