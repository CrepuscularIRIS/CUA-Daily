title: MedImageOSWorld: Benchmarking GUI Agents for Medical Image Consoles
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04800

§Abstract
Graphical consoles offer a practical interface for medical acquisition assistance, allowing agents to work through the controls and visual feedback used by human operators. Reliable assistance requires linking on-screen anatomy to acquisition decisions that determine what image evidence becomes available next. We introduce MedImageOSWorld, a benchmark for evaluating this capability in simulated CT, MR, and ultrasound consoles. Using screenshots and mouse-and-keyboard actions, agents configure protocols, plan acquisitions, inspect the resulting images, and make corrective adjustments across seven capability levels, from console operation to feedback-driven control. Evaluation combines task-specific workflow checks with hidden anatomical ground truth to assess procedural completion and acquisition outcomes separately. A common evaluation protocol specifies episode conditions and interaction budgets, while recorded trajectories support analysis of how agents observe, act, and respond to acquisition feedback. Across eleven open-weight agents, success rates range from 3.0 to 25.0 on a 0-100 scale while workflow-progress rates reach 21.5-74.5: agents complete much of the console workflow but rarely acquire the intended anatomy. Success collapses between perception and planning, from 79-90% at the lowest three levels for the best agent to at most 12% for millimetre-level planning and 0% for closed-loop control among open-weight agents. Two proprietary agents reach success rates of about 34 and exceed the best open-weight agent mainly in acquisition quality (61 versus 42). MedImageOSWorld provides a controlled setting for studying whether general-purpose GUI agents can translate visual observations into effective medical acquisition decisions.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Medical image acquisition depends on operators translating an examination request into console actions. They configure protocols, plan anatomical coverage or imaging planes, and assess the resulting images. The console is the interface where an operator turns an examination goal into acquired image evidence. An inadequate acquisition may require adjustment, repetition, or escalation. The graphical console is a natural interface for acquisition assistance: it brings protocol settings, spatial planning, and image assessment into the operator’s existing workflow. Agents that use screenshots and standard input actions can interact through this interface without requiring a dedicated scanner-control API or direct access to internal acquisition state. This offers a route to automation across heterogeneous console software while keeping actions and visible outcomes available for operator review. It also creates a demanding control problem: the agent must connect what is shown on screen to acquisition decisions whose consequences appear in subsequent images. Recent advances in general-purpose GUI agents make this interaction setting increasingly relevant. Computer-use benchmarks evaluate multimodal agents on stateful workflows over desktop applications and web services ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) . Medical acquisition poses a further question: can such agents combine visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and feedback-driven control to acquire the intended anatomy without human correction during an episode? We ask not only whether an agent can operate the machine, but whether its actions produce an acquisition that meets the intended goal. The distinction arises because acquisition actions determine what evidence becomes available for subsequent decisions. We call this property action-conditioned acquisition : an agent action alters the acquisition process and thereby changes the medical-image evidence produced at subsequent steps. The agent must identify the anatomy on screen, relate image coordinates to acquisition geometry, and use the resulting evidence to decide whether to proceed or adjust. Changes to geometry, timing, or probe pose affect acquisition; display-only adjustments do not. An agent can complete the prescribed workflow and still acquire the wrong anatomy. Workflow completion alone does not establish that the intended anatomy was acquired. Existing environments address parts of this evaluation problem. Clinical benchmarks cover electronic health records, simulated encounters, and administrative workflows ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Schmidgall et al., 2026 ; Bedi et al., 2026 ) , while MedCUA-Bench evaluates clinical GUI interaction with safety-critical errors ( Yu et al., 2026 ) . These settings primarily concern records, software state, or predefined observations rather than imaging acquisition. ABRA, RadABench, and MedSPOT study post-acquisition viewing, radiology tool use, and interface grounding ( Maksudov et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2024 ; Shakeel et al., 2026 ) ; they do not let the agent control the acquisition that generates the underlying study. Autonomous MRI and MaRGE/MaRCoS address acquisition and scanner control ( Ravi & Geethanath, 2020 ; Algarín et al., 2024 ) , but are MRI-specific systems rather than standardized benchmarks for general-purpose GUI agents. The remaining need is an evaluation setting that connects executable console workflows to action-conditioned acquisition and independent anatomical ground truth. To study this question, an environment must preserve the dependencies between planning, acquisition, and assessment while retaining ground truth for the acquired result. We introduce MedImageOSWorld , a benchmark with three executable consoles: computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance (MR), and ultrasound. All three derive observations from XCAT-based digital human anatomy phantoms ( Segars et al., 2010 ) . The environment couples an order, a screening record, persistent patient and device state, a protocol library, and an action-conditioned acquisition backend. Agents select and configure protocols, plan anatomy-dependent slices, volumes, or views, acquire images, and assess their adequacy. They can re-plan, re-acquire, repeat a view, or escalate. MedImageOSWorld provides a setting for studying how general-purpose agents combine visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and feedback-driven control in tasks with simulated physical consequences. A shared capability ladder organizes these requirements and helps locate failure points.
