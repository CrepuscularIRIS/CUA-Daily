arxiv: 2610.13268 (2610.13268v1)
title: ConClick: Element-Wise Contrastive Learning Improves GUI Grounding
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13268

§Abstract
GUI grounding, the task of locating the interface element specified by a user instruction, is a fundamental capability of GUI agents. However, existing grounding models remain error-prone, often causing downstream task failures. We systematically evaluate 10 grounding models across five datasets and identify a major error pattern: most incorrect clicks hit non-target interface elements rather than the background, with different models repeatedly selecting the same distractors. Motivated by these findings, we propose ConClick, a contrastive learning framework that teaches models to distinguish the target element from plausible distractors. We construct ConClick-Data, a dataset containing 4,292 challenging target-distractor pairs, and train the model using complementary listwise ranking and pairwise margin objectives. ConClick improves the grounding accuracy of Qwen3-VL-8B from 54.71% to 66.03% on ScreenSpot-Pro, from 83.72% to 86.28% on MMBench-GUI L2, and from 63.33% to 70.20% on OSWorld-G, outperforming prior methods built on the same or similarly sized backbones. We further introduce ReClick, an inference-time strategy that revisits promising regions and compares predictions across competing candidates, yielding an additional 2.96 percentage-point improvement in average accuracy across the three benchmarks. Together, our analysis, dataset, and methods provide both empirical insights and practical tools for building more reliable GUI agents. Data and code are available at https://github.com/zhangruotian/ConClick.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
GUI grounding maps instructions and screenshots to click locations , providing a core capability for GUI agents. Substantial effort has been devoted to improving GUI grounding through stronger multimodal backbones ( Bai et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025a ) , larger datasets ( Gou et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Xie et al., 2026 ; Qin et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026b ) , and reinforcement learning ( Zhou et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ; Tang et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Gu et al., 2025 ) . Inference methods yield further improvements by searching regions, enlarging crops, repeating predictions, or re-encoding selected regions ( Luo et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ; Jiang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2026 ; Du et al., 2026 ) . All these methods are evaluated using click-point accuracy, the percentage of correct clicks, defined as those falling within the annotated target region (Fig. 1 (a)). However, this criterion treats all off-target clicks equally and does not reveal where incorrect clicks land. For example, when instructed to review the invoice details in Fig. 1 , clicking “Pay now” instead of “Review details” can initiate an unintended payment, whereas clicking the background may have no effect. Wrong-button clicks may also delete files or expose confidential information, potentially causing irreversible harm that accuracy alone does not capture. Distinguishing error types is therefore important for assessing the potential consequences of grounding failures. To bridge this gap, we examine incorrect clicks along two dimensions: element selection and spatial proximity. The element-level dimension distinguishes clicks on a non-target GUI element from those on the background. The spatial dimension distinguishes clicks near or far from the target element according to whether they fall inside or outside a target-centered neighborhood. Combining these dimensions yields four error categories: near-element , near-background , far-element , and far-background (Fig. 1 (b)). We use this categorization to characterize incorrect clicks, with a particular focus on element selection errors . We evaluate 10 models across five datasets: a validation set of Click100K ( Awadalla et al., 2025 ) and four test sets, ScreenSpot-Pro ( Li et al., 2025 ) , MMBench-GUI L2 ( Wang et al., 2025b ) , UI-Vision ( Nayak et al., 2025 ) , and OSWorld-G ( Xie et al., 2026 ) . Fig. 1 (c) shows accuracy and the four error rates over all predictions, averaged across 10 models per dataset. These distributions reveal consistent error patterns across datasets. Specifically, near-element and far-element errors, i.e., clicks on non-target GUI elements, together account for approximately 74%–88% of all incorrect predictions across the five datasets. The predominance of element errors also holds when the 10 models are analyzed separately (Fig. 7 ). We conduct further analysis of incorrect element selection. Results show that element coverage and uniform random clicking cannot explain the predominance of element errors. We refer to non-target GUI elements selected by incorrect predictions as distractor elements and observe that different models can select the same distractor for the same instruction and screenshot. Our spatial analysis also shows that errors are denser inside the target neighborhood despite being more numerous outside it (Fig. 1 (d)). These results on element selection suggest that GUI models still struggle to distinguish the target from distractor elements, motivating us to explore supervision that explicitly accounts for distractors. Traditional coordinate supervision is effective for learning to predict target coordinates, but does not explicitly contrast the target with the distractor it is likely to select. Existing training examples with target-only grounding annotations do not provide the corresponding distractor elements needed for this supervision. To support it, we first construct ConClick-Data , a small dataset of about 8k examples from Click100K: half contain target–distractor annotations, and half are ordinary grounding examples sampled across source categories to maintain data diversity. We use two GUI models, GTA1-7B ( Yang et al., 2026b ) and GUI-Libra-8B ( Yang et al., 2026a ) , to identify observed distractors by matching their incorrect predictions to non-target GUI elements. For training, we introduce ConClick , an element-wise contrastive training strategy that learns to prefer the target over observed distractors for the same instruction. Specifically, a listwise ranking loss encourages the model to rank the target above candidate distractors, while a pairwise margin loss encourages a stronger preference for the target over the annotated distractor. We train the model with a coordinate loss on all examples and an additional selection loss combining these two terms on target–distractor examples, directly addressing the observed element selection errors.
