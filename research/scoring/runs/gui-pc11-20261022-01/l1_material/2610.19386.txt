title: STRATA: Self-Maintaining Structured Experience Graphs for Self-Evolving Language Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19386

§Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents can self-evolve at test time by accumulating experience: retrieving relevant memories before acting and writing new lessons back afterwards. Yet most are single-level: trajectories, distilled workflows and reasoning items all land in one undifferentiated pool indexed by embedding similarity, with no typed abstraction hierarchy. They never forget or deduplicate, and are prone to negative transfer, where a superficially similar but directionally opposite experience is recalled and its concrete steps are copied verbatim, actively harming the agent. We introduce STRATA, a structured, outcome-grounded, self-maintaining memory that organizes experience into a three-layer graph of atomic skills and code snippets, task workflows and generalized meta-frameworks, connected by typed edges. STRATA maintains itself through five mechanisms sharing one similarity geometry: a multi-dimensional similarity that scores retrieval by operation and target; a typed reflector crystallizing procedures from successes and pitfalls from failures; community detection that induces meta-frameworks; pressure-triggered garbage collection with articulation-point protection; and contrast edges that flag confusable, direction-opposite experiences. Across four interactive benchmarks (AppWorld, WebArena, ALFWorld, ScienceWorld), two frozen backbones and seven baselines, STRATA attains the best average score while simultaneously using the lowest latency and fewest environment steps of any memory-augmented method, and 44% fewer lines of injected memory than the most context-hungry baseline. We isolate two failure modes of prior memories: single-level memory degrades sharply as the task set grows, and single-level concrete memories suffer catastrophic negative transfer, which contrast edges repair, lifting ScienceWorld from 78.7 to 96.7 within the same system.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Language-model agents increasingly solve long-horizon interactive tasks such as navigating websites, orchestrating APIs across applications, and acting in simulated environments ( Yao et al. 2023b ; Trivedi et al. 2024 ; Zhou et al. 2024 ; Shridhar et al. 2020 ; Wang et al. 2022 ) , building on prompting and tool-use techniques that structure a single episode ( Wei et al. 2022 ; Yao et al. 2023a ; Schick et al. 2023 ; Wang et al. 2024a ) . A promising route to improving such agents without weight updates is experience-driven self-evolution : the agent keeps a memory of its past episodes, retrieves relevant experience before each new task, and folds fresh lessons back into memory afterwards, becoming more capable over time ( Ouyang et al. 2025 ; Xu et al. 2026 ; Shinn et al. 2023 ) . Because this loop runs purely at test time, its effectiveness hinges almost entirely on how experience is represented, retrieved and maintained ( Snell et al. 2024 ) . Despite rapid progress, prevailing agent memories share three limitations. (1) They lack a typed abstraction hierarchy. Several do organize what they store (AWM distils workflows from trajectories ( Wang et al. 2024b ) , A-Mem links notes into a network ( Xu et al. 2026 ) ), but within one undifferentiated level: nothing separates a one-off tool call from a reusable procedure from a cross-task principle, and no operation folds procedures into a principle. Retrieval stays a single similarity search dominated by surface overlap, merging same-material, different-operation pairs like “create a playlist” and “add months to a note”. (2) They are not self-maintaining. Memory grows monotonically, with no forgetting, no deduplication and no consolidation, so stale entries crowd out useful ones. We measure this: with forgetting disabled the workflow store grows to 269 nodes injecting 48.7 lines of memory per task, yet scores lower than a bounded 201 -node memory injecting less than half as much. (3) They are vulnerable to negative transfer. When two experiences are superficially similar but directionally opposite, as in “heat the substance” versus “cool the substance”, similarity recall surfaces the wrong one, and if the memory stores concrete executable steps the agent copies them and acts catastrophically. This is not hypothetical: on ScienceWorld a production agent’s native flat memory scores -100 on a task family that a memoryless agent solves outright. We address these limitations with STRATA, a memory that is structured, outcome-grounded and self-maintaining. STRATA organizes experience into a three-layer directed graph: atomic nodes (reusable skills, code snippets, or specialized sub-roles), workflow nodes (generalized task procedures), and meta-framework nodes (task families abstracted from recurring workflows), linked by typed call, generalization, merge and contrast edges. The graph is grown and pruned by five mechanisms that all operate in one similarity geometry: (i) a multi-dimensional similarity combining content and goal embeddings with an explicit operation/target tag term; (ii) a typed reflector that crystallizes a reusable procedure only from solved episodes and attaches a pitfall bullet on failure, with a per-group adaptive threshold deciding whether to create, extend, or skip a workflow; (iii) community detection that induces a meta-framework whenever a community is large, tight, and novel; (iv) pressure-triggered garbage collection that ranks workflows by a competition-aware quality score and forgets the lowest while protecting articulation points of the similarity graph; and (v) contrast edges that connect confusable, direction-opposite experiences so that retrieving one injects an explicit “do not confuse” warning about the other. Our contributions are as follows. • We propose STRATA, a three-layer structured experience graph for self-evolving LLM agents, together with five maintenance mechanisms that grow, generalize, and forget it, formulated precisely and reproducibly. • We evaluate STRATA on four interactive benchmarks across two frozen backbones, seven baselines and three seeds, with a component-wise ablation of every mechanism. STRATA obtains the best average score at the lowest latency and fewest environment steps of any memory baseline. • We identify two failure modes of prior memories: a scale failure of single-level memory that neither abstracts nor forgets (on Flash the strongest baseline ranks first at 168 tasks and seventh at 812 ), and negative transfer in single-level concrete memories, which contrast edges repair, exposing a structure-versus-abstraction trade-off.

## Method
The Three-Layer Experience Graph STRATA maintains a graph G=(V,E) whose nodes carry a type \tau(n)\in\{\textsc{atomic},\textsc{workflow},\textsc{meta}\} : atomic nodes are the smallest reusable units (one tool/API, a parameterized code snippet, or a specialized sub-role); workflow nodes are generalized procedures for a whole task; meta nodes are paradigms abstracted from a family of similar workflows. The stored edge set is typed, E=E_{\text{call}}\cup E_{\text{gen}}\cup E_{\text{merge}}\cup E_{\text{con}} : call edges ( \textsc{workflow}\to\textsc{atomic} , ordered) and three evolution relations ( \textit{sources}\to\textit{target} ) labelled generalizes , merged_from and contrasts . Maintenance uses a derived graph W=(V_{\textsc{workflow}},E_{\text{sim}}) : an undirected union-kNN graph over workflows weighted by s_{\text{nn}} , recomputed from G every round and never stored. Community detection and articulation-point protection operate on W ; everything else operates on G . Each node exposes several textual views used by different similarity dimensions: a content view (what it does), an index view (applicability and goal), and a set of structured tags T(n)\subseteq\{\texttt{app:}\ast,\texttt{op:}\ast,\texttt{obj:}\ast\} , a node attribute rather than a node, naming the application, operation and target object. Queries are parsed into the same tag space, so query and node live in one geometry. Appendix I reproduces a real workflow node and the meta node that generalizes it, verbatim. Multi-Dimensional Similarity and Retrieval A single content-embedding cosine conflates same material, different operation experiences. STRATA instead scores a query q against a node n with four complementary signals, \begin{split}s(q,n)={}&W_{\text{c}}\cos\!\big(\mathbf{e}(q),\mathbf{e}_{\text{c}}(n)\big)+W_{\text{i}}\cos\!\big(\mathbf{e}(q),\mathbf{e}_{\text{i}}(n)\big)\\ &+W_{\text{t}}\,J\!\big(T(q),T(n)\big)+W_{\text{l}}\,\ell(q,n),\end{split} (1) where \mathbf{e}_{\text{c}} and \mathbf{e}_{\text{i}} embed the content and index views, J is Jaccard overlap of the structured tags, and \ell is a lexical token overlap in the spirit of classical term matching ( Robertson and Zaragoza 2009 ) . We use W_{\text{c}}{=}0.40 , W_{\text{i}}{=}0.25 , W_{\text{t}}{=}0.30 , W_{\text{l}}{=}0.05 . The tag term is an additive weight, not a multiplicative gate; at W_{\text{t}}{=}0.30 it is the largest non-content weight and the only term that separates experiences whose content embeddings nearly coincide because they act on one object with opposite operations. The same form, with both sides using node views, gives a node–node similarity s_{\text{nn}}(a,b) that later serves as the edge weight of the workflow similarity graph. Retrieval returns a subgraph , not isolated items. Nodes are ranked by \tilde{s}(n)=s(q,n)+\beta_{\text{layer}}\frac{2-\mathrm{ord}(\tau(n))}{2} with \beta_{\text{layer}}{=}0.15 and \mathrm{ord}(\textsc{meta}){=}0<\mathrm{ord}(\textsc{workflow}){=}1<\mathrm{ord}(\textsc{atomic}){=}2 , biasing towards general nodes. Only seeds with raw score s(q,n)\geq\theta_{\min}{=}0.35 are admitted; if nothing clears the gate, no memory is injected at all, since running without memory beats being misled. Around the top- k seeds we pull in their meta parents (which must themselves clear \theta_{\min} , so catch-all hubs cannot leak into every task), the atomic nodes they call, and their contrast neighbours, rendered meta \to workflow \to atomic so the agent reads principles first and callable skills last. Typed Reflection with Adaptive Thresholds After each episode a master router inspects the task, outcome and trajectory and dispatches to typed sub-reflectors for skill, code, role and workflow. Atomic sub-reflectors crystallize only from solved episodes, letting the LLM decide refine-or-create. The workflow sub-reflector is threshold-driven, and the thresholds must adapt: equivalent task pairs score \approx 0.4 – 0.5 in one application and \approx 0.8 in another, so one global band either over-merges or over-fragments. We therefore group nodes by their leading tag and shrink a per-group quantile towards a global prior in empirical-Bayes fashion ( Efron and Morris 1973 ) : \tau_{\bullet}=\lambda\,Q_{p_{\bullet}}(S_{g})+(1-\lambda)\,G_{\bullet},\qquad\lambda=\frac{n_{g}}{n_{g}+\kappa}, (2) with \kappa{=}30 , p_{\text{lo}}{=}0.25 , p_{\text{hi}}{=}0.92 and global priors G_{\text{low}}{=}0.33 , G_{\text{high}}{=}0.62 . Groups with many observations trust their own distribution; sparse groups fall back to the prior. Letting s be the similarity to the nearest existing workflow, the decision is noop if s>\tau_{\text{high}} (a near-duplicate), add_bullet if \tau_{\text{low}}\leq s\leq\tau_{\text{high}} (record only the difference as one bullet), and create otherwise. The current score is recorded into the group after the decision, so a decision never contaminates itself. Crucially, failed episodes never crystallize a “do this” procedure; at most they attach a pitfall bullet to the nearest workflow, rendered to the agent as something to avoid. Pressure-Triggered Forgetting with Bridge Protection Forgetting is applied only at the workflow layer. Each workflow gets a quality score combining an activation term A_{i} (its similarity-weighted degree among surviving neighbours, so central workflows are kept) and a competitive age G_{i} : Q_{i}=w_{A}\,z\big(\mathrm{rank}(A_{i})\big)-w_{G}\,z\big(\mathrm{rank}(G_{i})\big), (3) with w_{A}{=}0.35 , w_{G}{=}0.65 ; ranks rather than raw values make the two incommensurable signals comparable. Age updates online at every retrieval: all candidates age by \alpha{=}0.02 , a retrieved-but-unselected candidate is penalised by \beta\,w_{i}(1-\mathrm{margin}) so close-but-losing competitors age fastest, and the winner is rejuvenated by G_{u}\leftarrow\gamma G_{u} , \gamma{=}0.5 . Once live workflows exceed a cap, lowest- Q nodes are deleted down to \mathrm{cap}\cdot(1-0.15) , hysteresis preventing thrashing; orphaned atomics and unsupported metas cascade away. This has a failure mode: if the lowest- Q node is an articulation point of W , removing it splits a cluster that should stay connected, and the next round of community detection fractures one task family into two metas. STRATA therefore deletes the lowest- Q non-cut vertex, j^{\ast}=\arg\min_{i\in\text{alive}}\{\,Q_{i}\mid i\notin\mathrm{Cut}(W)\,\}, (4) recomputing \mathrm{Cut}(W) each round with Tarjan’s algorithm in O(|V|+|E|) ( Tarjan 1972 ) , and falling back to the unconstrained minimum only if every candidate is a cut vertex, so collection always makes progress. Inducing Meta-Frameworks by Community Detection Meta nodes are triggered not by counting children but by community structure . We build W by joining a workflow pair when s_{\text{nn}} exceeds a per-group adaptive threshold (shrunk as above, \kappa_{E}{=}6 , intra-group default 0.40 ) or a fixed cross-group threshold 0.55 , then keeping each node’s 6 strongest edges (an edge survives if either endpoint ranks it that high). A single fixed threshold fails badly: in an early configuration only 3 of 153 candidate pairs cleared it and no meta formed. Louvain ( Blondel et al. 2008 ) at resolution 1.5 yields communities (the Leiden refinement ( Traag et al. 2019 ) would also serve), and a community is promoted only if it is large ( |C|\geq N_{\min} ), tight (conductance \phi(C)<0.6 : dense inside, sparse outside ( Shi 2000 ) ), and novel . Letting \mathbf{c}(C) be its centroid and d^{\ast} its distance to the nearest existing meta, we create if d^{\ast}\geq R_{\text{meta}}{=}0.6 and otherwise update the matched meta by absorbing its unlinked members. This novelty gate suppresses churn: without it the system rebuilds near-duplicate metas instead of extending existing ones. Contrast Edges against Negative Transfer The content dimension rates same object, opposite direction experiences as highly similar, such as “heat tin until it melts” versus “cool tin until it solidifies”. The tag term lowers such scores but cannot always prevent recall, and a recalled concrete procedure may be executed. A contrast edge is a bidirectional evolution edge linking two such nodes, \mathrm{contrast}(a,b)\iff\mathrm{op}(a)\ \text{opposes}\ \mathrm{op}(b), (5) instantiated by rule over a fixed lexicon of opposite operator pairs, \{\textit{heat},\textit{cool}\} and \{\textit{create},\textit{delete}\} . We do not require \mathrm{obj}(a)=\mathrm{obj}(b) , because negative transfer is often cross-object, as when a melt-lead procedure misleads a freeze-tin task. A retrieved seed pulls its contrast neighbours into the subgraph, rendered with a prominent do not confuse warning rather than as a flat procedure. Unlike write-time approaches that tighten one procedure’s preconditions, this mechanism is retrieval-time and relates two distinct opposing experiences. The Full Loop An episode retrieves a subgraph, renders it into the prompt, acts until done, then reflects: typed sub-reflectors write back, community detection updates the meta layer, and pressure-triggered collection prunes. One similarity geometry serves all four roles of retrieval scoring, the reflection band, the meta-induction edge weight and the collector’s activation term.
