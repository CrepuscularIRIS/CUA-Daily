title: LEASH: Uncertainty-Aware Weak-to-Strong Escalation and Oversight for Computer-Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22280

§Abstract
Computer-use agents increasingly tackle long-horizon tasks, where running a frontier model at every step is expensive and a small open-weight model alone often fails. Weak-to-strong escalation offers a middle ground where a weak agent acts by default and hands control to a strong one only when needed. The core challenge is deciding when to escalate, which requires an uncertainty signal cheap enough to check at every step. However, even after handoff, the strong agent can still get lost, but a black-box API that the strong agent is usually served exposes no internals to monitor. To this end, we propose LEASH, a training-free framework that unleashes the strong agent only when the weak agent's latent uncertainty signals it is needed, and keeps it on a leash afterward. LEASH has three layers: (1) initial task uncertainty for early routing, (2) step-level uncertainty for runtime escalation, and, after handoff, (3) a shadow observer for continued oversight of the strong black-box agent. Here, we use latent uncertainty as a fuel for all three layers. Specifically, we compare the weak model's hidden states with those from its past successful and failed trajectories, and a state that looks more like the failures signals higher uncertainty. During weak-agent execution, our uncertainty estimator reuses activations already produced during action generation, so it needs no extra forward pass or dedicated monitor training. Experiments on OSWorld across four contemporary model backbones show that LEASH consistently improves over standalone weak agents and outperforms random escalation by 6.55--11.94 points at matched escalation rates. With Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, LEASH achieves 73.66% task success at $0.4117 per task, matching always-on Claude Opus 5 (72.90%) while reducing inference cost by 19.2%.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Computer-use agents (CUAs) automate tasks of graphical interfaces by translating natural-language instructions and visual observations into executable actions ( Xie et al., 2024 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ) , and are increasingly deployed for complex, long-horizon automation tasks that can take more than an hour on average ( Abhyankar et al., 2026 ) . While frontier proprietary models have been shown to achieve impressive performance leaps ( OpenAI, 2026 ; Anthropic, 2026a ) , running every single task spanning hundreds of steps with these models raises cost concerns, whereas a light open-weight model alone performs poorly on even much simpler tasks ( Xie et al., 2024 ) . This creates a fundamental trade-off between accuracy and cost efficiency, motivating weak-to-strong escalation (W2SE), in which a weak agent serves as the primary executor and hands control to a strong agent only when needed ( Wei et al., 2026 ; Lei and Huang, 2026 ) . W2SE raises two important questions: (1) When should the weak agent escalate, and (2) who oversees the strong agent after handing over? Existing work on W2SE primarily focuses on the first question, estimating the weak agent’s failure risk to decide when escalation is needed. Some of them adopt another monitoring agent to verify each step ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Yi et al., 2026 ) , others train an extra monitoring module on the collection of annotated trajectories ( Wei et al., 2026 ; Lei and Huang, 2026 ) . However, the former inevitably adds nontrivial cost to run another heavy agent step-by-step, whereas the latter creates another phase of data annotation and training, hindering direct adoption in real-world applications under varying deployment constraints. While training-free uncertainty estimation-based approaches might avoid the aforementioned bottlenecks ( Oh et al., 2026b ) , prior works still rely on external knowledge base ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , another reference model ( Oh et al., 2026a ) , or multiple sampling for each step ( Wang et al., 2026 ) quickly becomes infeasible or impractical in long-horizon tasks in the wild. Besides, escalation alone does not eliminate execution uncertainty, resulting in the second important question in W2SE which is commonly overlooked. That is, even after a stronger agent takes control, subsequent actions may deviate from the intended task or fail to achieve the desired outcome. However, monitoring a black-box strong agent is challenging because its internal states are inaccessible and verbalized confidence can be unreliable on evolving context of long-horizon task ( Han et al., 2024 ; Kirchhof et al., 2026 ) . To answer both questions, we formulate W2SE as a budget-constrained sequential stopping problem, and use the weak agent’s failure risk as a proxy for the expected benefit of escalation. We then propose L atent uncertainty for E scalation A nd SH adow oversight ( LEASH ), a training-free framework driven by the uncertainty signal in the weak agent’s hidden states. First, we record its hidden states of prompt and action tokens on a small set of held-out tasks and label them with task-level and subgoal-level outcomes to construct a representation bank. This representation bank then serves as a semantic anchor to score the newly faced prompts and actions during test-time by measuring, under a Mahalanobis metric in latent space, how much closer each one lies to past failures than to past successes. LEASH puts this signal on two jobs (Figure 2 ): For escalation , an initial gate scores uncertainty from the task prompt query, while a runtime gate monitors subsequent execution trace. For oversight , after the weak model hands the control to the strong agent, it switches its role from actor to observer that monitors the strong agent’s trajectory every three steps; when its uncertainty is high, the pending action is rolled back and re-decided once with higher reasoning effort. Importantly, our uncertainty-driven W2SE formulation draws on an easily accessible resource, the weak agent’s own past experience ( Silver and Sutton, 2025 ) , removing reliance on an external dataset or additional training without hosting another expensive agent during runtime, while offering extensibility for recursive self-improvement ( Schmidhuber, 2007 ; Zelikman et al., 2022 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) . We evaluate LEASH on OSWorld ( Xie et al., 2024 ) with four open-weight weak agents and Claude Opus 5 ( Anthropic, 2026b ) as the strong agent. At matched escalation rates, LEASH outperforms random escalation by 6.55–11.94 points. We further measure how much of the performance gap between the weak agent alone and always-on Opus is recovered by escalation, following the performance-gap metric of ( Burns et al., 2023 ) . LEASH recovers 75–102% of this gap, against 37–69% for random escalation, while costing 69–81% as much as always-on Opus.
