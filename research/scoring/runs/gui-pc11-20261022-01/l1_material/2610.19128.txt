title: CLASHarness: Benchmarking Multi-Agent Conflict in Computer Use via a Controllable and Verifiable Generation Pipeline
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19128

§Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems to automate complex computer-use workflows, yet existing benchmarks mostly assume consistent, cooperative environments. In contrast, real-world collaboration rarely runs on consistent information: peer or sub-agents frequently report outdated states, issue contradictory instructions, or compete over shared permissions and files, forcing agents to check others' outputs and verify ground truth in the environment. Evaluating such conflict resolution remains challenging: manual authoring cannot scale across complex systems, while unconstrained LLM generation produces narrative drift, answer leakage, and unsatisfiable rubrics. We introduce CLASHarness (Conflict-Loaded Agent Scenario Harness), a controllable framework for generating verifiable multi-agent conflicts in computer use, with a benchmark of 600 multi-round tasks. By combining hierarchical scenario planning with an eight-step verified micro-pipeline, CLASHarness pre-commits decisive visual facts before rendering and compiles hybrid code and semantic graders directly from executed reference trajectories, guaranteeing every task is solvable by construction. Evaluating 15 frontier models on CLASHarness reveals three core bottlenecks. First, when sub-agent outputs conflict with the environment, weaker models blindly follow misleading information, whereas stronger models inspect visual evidence to verify ground truth. Second, resolving conflicts is much harder than finding them: while agents can spot inconsistencies, actively fixing system state drops task success significantly. Third, coordination protocols remain a universal bottleneck: even frontier models fail over 58% of closing actions, often forgetting to release shared locks or hand off tasks. CLASHarness provides a scalable, verifiable foundation for building reliable multi-agent systems.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
As vision-language models (VLMs) transition to tool-augmented computer-use scenarios, multi-agent systems are increasingly deployed to automate complex collaborative workflows ( Li et al., 2023 ; Wu et al., 2023 ; Du et al., 2023 ) , prompting numerous benchmarks to evaluate and advance agent capabilities ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024b ; Xu et al., 2026 ) . However, existing computer-use benchmarks mostly assume consistent, cooperative single-agent environments ( Xie et al., 2024b ; Zhou et al., 2024 ) , while multi-agent evaluations remain largely confined to pure-text dialogue ( Cemri et al., 2026 ; Pappu et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, real-world collaboration rarely runs on consistent information: peer or sub-agents frequently report outdated states, issue contradictory instructions, or compete over shared permissions and files, triggering severe coordination failures ( Xie et al., 2024a ; Xu et al., 2024 ; Su et al., 2024 ; Khatua et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . For example, when a sub-agent relies on a stale cache to report a deployment as successful and urges routing production traffic, while live telemetry shows failing health checks, an agent resolving the conflict cannot blindly trust the peer report. Instead, it must check others’ outputs, inspect visual dashboards to verify ground truth in the operating environment, and execute a configuration rollback. Evaluating such conflict resolution faces a fundamental challenge in benchmark construction: manual task curation is labor-intensive and cannot scale across complex operating environments ( Mialon et al., 2024 ; Deng et al., 2023 ) , while generating tasks via unconstrained LLM prompting produces uncontrollable scenarios suffering from narrative drift, answer leakage, ungrounded visual assets, and unsatisfiable grading rubrics that leave tasks unsolvable ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Lù et al., 2025 ; Peng et al., 2026b ) . To address these challenges, we introduce CLASHarness ( C onflict- L oaded A gent S cenario H arness), a controllable generation framework and benchmark for synthesizing verifiable multi-agent conflict tasks in computer use. Instead of relying on unconstrained prompting, CLASHarness follows three core design principles to guarantee scenario validity by design: (1) Hierarchical Phased Synthesis: scenario generation is decomposed into macro scenario planning and verified micro-execution, preventing context overflow and drift across rounds; (2) Pre-Committed Multimodal Grounding: decisive ground-truth values are committed prior to environment rendering, ensuring visual evidence is truly necessary rather than decorative; and (3) Execution-Compiled Verification: hybrid evaluation checkers, combining exact code checks and scoped semantic criteria, are compiled directly from executed reference trajectories, guaranteeing that every task is solvable. We summarize our principal contributions as follows: • Controllable Generation Framework (CLASHarness): A hierarchical synthesis pipeline combining declarative outline planning with verified micro-execution, compiling hybrid checkers from executed reference trajectories to guarantee every task is solvable without manual scripting (§ 3 ). • 600-Round Multimodal Conflict Benchmark: An extensive benchmark spanning 20 operational domains, 600 executable conflict rounds, 7,970 pre-committed visual assets, and 3,760 hybrid checkers enforcing grounded visual reasoning (§ 4 ). • Comprehensive Diagnostic Findings: Across 15 evaluated models, we uncover three systemic bottlenecks: (1) Visual Grounding vs. Blind Trust: weaker models blindly follow misleading information, whereas stronger models inspect visual evidence to verify ground truth (§ 5.2 ); (2) Resolution vs. Detection: while agents can spot inconsistencies, actively fixing system state drops task success significantly (§ 5.4 ); and (3) Coordination Breakdown: even frontier models fail over 58% of closing actions, often forgetting to release shared locks or hand off tasks to sub-agents (§ 5.2 ).
