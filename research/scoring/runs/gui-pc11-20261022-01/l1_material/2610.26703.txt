title: Comparing Computer Use Behavior Across Multiple Levels of Abstraction
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26703

§Abstract
Interaction traces record how people and agents use computers, and comparing them can reveal usability barriers, differences in expertise, and human--agent similarity. Yet traces are recorded as low-level, interface-specific actions, making comparisons difficult across tasks, interfaces, and input modalities. Existing approaches either compare these actions directly, reduce traces to aggregate outcomes, or summarize them at higher-level goals, obscuring where behavior differs. We present MAP (Multi-level Action Parsing), a hierarchical semantic representation and annotation pipeline that maps traces into a common vocabulary across linked levels of abstraction. MAP compares traces by sequence, recurring patterns, and effort, localizing differences by type and abstraction level. A technical evaluation across three computer use datasets shows high agreement with manual annotations and broad coverage of behavior. Applying MAP shows that sighted and blind users increasingly converge at higher levels despite navigation differences, while agents omit corrective, help-seeking, and recovery behaviors present in human traces.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Understanding how people use computers has long been a core goal of HCI ( Hilbert and Redmiles, 2000 ) . A rich source of evidence is the interaction trace : a logged sequence of computer use actions ( e.g. , clicks and keypresses) together with their context ( e.g. , applications and interface elements) ( Hilbert and Redmiles, 2000 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ) . Researchers use such traces to understand how an individual performed a task and to compare how people use computers — e.g. , to reveal usability issues by identifying deviations from an ideal trace ( Waterson et al., 2002 ; Siochi and Ehrich, 1991 ) , characterize interaction patterns of experts and novices ( Grossman and Fitzmaurice, 2015 ) , and surface web browsing strategies ( Wang et al., 2016 ; Clark et al., 2006 ) . Prior work makes such comparisons by holding the task, interface, and input modality constant to compare low-level actions directly ( Waterson et al., 2002 ; Siochi and Ehrich, 1991 ; Wang et al., 2016 ) , or by reducing the trace to aggregate measures such as completion time, action count, or success ( Grossman and Fitzmaurice, 2015 ; Abhyankar et al., 2026 ; Gubbi Mohanbabu et al., 2026 ) . But, such work does not yet compare computer use behaviors that cut across tasks, interfaces, and input modalities – e.g. to compare across tasks to surface usability barriers, across modalities to locate accessibility gaps, or across interfaces to characterize general computer use expertise. Recent datasets of interaction traces record this kind of variation ( Deng et al., 2023 ; Rawles et al., 2023 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Gubbi Mohanbabu et al., 2026 ) , creating the opportunity to make such comparisons at scale. But, it remains challenging to compare traces as they are recorded in a low-level input- and interface-specific vocabulary. For example, a screen reader user may press Tab six times while a sighted user points to reach the same button, but their traces differ. Recent work thus describes traces at a higher level of abstraction by using LLMs to summarize low-level actions into goals ( e.g. , search for a product, fill in the shipping form) ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) , then aligns those descriptions to report where two traces cover the same steps ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . However, as each trace is labeled independently the same goal may be described in different ways, such that it remains challenging to count or match goals across a corpus of traces ( e.g. , to identify where a group of users got stuck). Further, this level of abstraction is useful for comparing higher-level workflows but obscures differences we care about when comparing how people use computers: people can reach the same goal by different methods ( e.g. , search vs. faceted browsing), or follow the same method with different costs ( e.g. , navigating facets linearly vs. by heading). To meaningfully compare computer use behavior across heterogeneous interaction traces thus requires a representation with a shared vocabulary to aggregate labels across traces and multiple linked levels of abstraction so that we can recover how and at what level interaction traces differ. We introduce MAP (Multi-level Action Parsing), a framework for comparing computer use behavior across multiple levels of abstraction and dimensions of similarity. MAP provides a hierarchical semantic representation of interaction traces across four connected levels, from input actions as Operations (L1) , to their Functions & Consequences (L2) , Methods (L3) , and Goals (L4) . We construct these levels through the following procedure: Operations (L1) are low-level actions common to interaction traces ( e.g. , pointing, clicking), Functions and Consequences (L2) are derived by unifying existing standards that describe how interfaces are implemented ( , 2023 ; Karl-Bridge-Microsoft, 2025 ) ( e.g. , “focus \langle search box \rangle ”), Methods (L3) are derived by open-coding interaction traces and consolidating equivalent labels ( e.g. , “edit”, “search”), and Goals (L4) are task-dependent and thus open-vocabulary as in prior work ( e.g. , “Edit the file named final.txt”) ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) . MAP’s vocabulary instantiates this procedure for desktop GUI computer use. To assign this vocabulary to interaction traces at scale, MAP applies labels deterministically when possible ( e.g. , L1 operations and L2 functions) and otherwise with an LLM with a fixed vocabulary ( e.g. , L3 methods), such that only task-dependent fields remain open-vocabulary. MAP’s shared representation thus makes it possible to identify where traces diverge. To capture different ways in which behavior can be similar, MAP applies three complementary metrics: sequence similarity , which measures alignment in the order of interactions; pattern similarity , which captures recurring local interaction patterns; and effort similarity , which captures how actions and time are allocated across a workflow.
