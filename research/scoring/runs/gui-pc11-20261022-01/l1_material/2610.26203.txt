title: Focused Omni-Modal GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26203

§Abstract
Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents increasingly act over streaming omni-modal observations spanning screenshots, audio, video, interaction history, and device states. However, exposing this complete context uniformly at every step ignores that relevance is transient and sparse: the signal determining one action can become clutter at the next. Because executed actions feed back into the context, these errors compound into Sequential Distraction, driving the agent into irreversible trajectory drift. We formulate the underlying requirement as Focused Perception: a representation that is step-adaptive, action-sufficient, and non-intrusive. We realize this in FocusGUI, a lightweight adapter that dynamically prioritizes multimodal channels against a step-specific decision reference. Instead of uniformly processing all inputs, it compresses the most relevant evidence into a compact latent and subtly injects it into the frozen decoder's embeddings, guiding the model's attention without altering its native parameter space. FocusGUI trains from action supervision alone, requiring neither modality-relevance labels nor backbone fine-tuning. Across four omni-modal backbones, it raises overall Type Match by +33.2 and Exact Match by 3.0 times over the frozen backbone on average, and Type Match by +30.1 over the strongest existing enhancement method.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents are shifting from parsing static screenshots to acting on dynamic, streaming observations across multiple modalities ( Henry et al., 2026 ; Jang et al., 2025 ; Yao et al., 2026 ) . In realistic computing environments, the evidence needed for the next action need not be confined to the current visual frame. An unexpected notification chime might demand an immediate context switch, a specific transition in a live video can dictate when to click, and spoken instructions must be continuously mapped to visual changes on the screen ( ByteDance Seed, 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) . Contemporary omni-modal models can ingest screenshots, video streams, audio, interaction history, and device states within a single network ( Xu et al., 2025 ; Fu et al., 2025 ; Cui et al., 2026 ) . This has naturally led to a default paradigm: exposing the complete multimodal observation to the model at every interaction step and leaving it to implicitly determine what matters while generating the action. Leaving this selection entirely to the decoder assumes that it can reliably rediscover what matters from the complete observation at every interaction step. Our analysis reveals why this is difficult (Fig. 1 ). When individual modalities are removed during multi-step interaction, the dominant modality switches across nearly half of adjacent steps; even within a single step, only a small fraction of the observation may support the decision, while the rest can become distracting. Relevance is therefore fundamentally a property of the step , not a static property of the observation. Yet the decoder is forced to rediscover this relevance at every step, without any explicit signal of what has changed. In a closed-loop agent, such misreads do not end with the current prediction: the executed action changes the environment and feeds back into the observation and history of the next step, allowing errors to compound over time. We refer to this compounding failure as Sequential Distraction . The most intuitive remedy is to perform subtraction on the inputs. This is exactly what existing methods attempt: cropping parts of the current screen to retain only candidate regions of interest ( Lu et al., 2024 ; Luo et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026b ; Cheng et al., 2024 ) . However, this strategy of giving less fundamentally misaligns with the nature of streaming interaction in two ways. First, the decisive signal could emerge from audio, video, or device status at any moment, channels entirely outside the scope of spatial cropping. Second, even when the signal resides on the screen, the crudely cropped background often contains the essential contextual evidence needed to ground accurate coordinates and text parameters. The problem is not that the decoder is given too much, but that it lacks guidance on how to prioritize it. A proper focus mechanism should change how the decoder reads the observation, rather than altering what it is given . We realize this principle in FocusGUI (Fig. 2 ). Since the decoder can already read the full observation and what it lacks is a signal of what matters at the current step, FocusGUI leaves both the backbone and its input untouched and supplies that signal alongside them. At every step, it exposes the observation as separable perceptual channels, scores each against a reference derived from the current observation as a whole, and compresses the prioritized channels into a compact focus latent. The latent enters as a zero-initialized residual at the boundary where the decoder begins to generate the action. This residual reallocates attention over the entire observation without altering it, guiding both the choice of action and the grounding of its arguments. Training uses next-action supervision alone, with no labels indicating which modality matters. On average across four omni-modal backbones, FocusGUI raises Type Match from 31.0% to 64.2% and Exact Match from 11.2% to 33.5% over the frozen backbone, and reaches 1.9 \times the Type Match of the strongest enhancement method. The gains grow with interaction depth, as Sequential Distraction predicts: from the first step to the fourth, the frozen Qwen2.5-Omni loses half of its Type Match while FocusGUI retains 86.8%.
