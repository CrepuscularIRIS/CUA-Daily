arxiv: 2610.01491 (2610.01491v1)
title: Auditing Web Agent Evaluation on WebArena-Lite: Human Review of Outcomes and Trajectories
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01491

§Abstract
Web agents are an important application of large language models, yet their evaluation often depends on rule based or language model evaluators that inspect only the final outcome. Human verification of task completion and detailed analysis of failed trajectories remain limited. We audit all 165 WebArena Lite tasks under six evaluation conditions built from GPT 5.5 and an untrained Qwen3.5 9B model. The audit retains the original score, corrects false negatives from the automatic evaluator, identifies the first consequential error, and examines progress across the trajectory. We also study a Memory and Analysis Support Mechanism (MASM), which maintains explicit execution state, and Guide Text, which provides task relevant procedural guidance. Across four GPT 5.5 settings, human review recovers 5.45 to 8.49 percentage points of success missed by the evaluator. With a 25 step budget, Guide Text raises corrected success with MASM from 34.55% to 38.18%. On the untrained Qwen3.5 9B model, MASM raises the evaluator score from 13.90% to 18.80%. Review of 102 failed GPT 5.5 trajectories reveals frequent scrolling loops, unfinished exploration, premature answers, invalid actions, and incomplete form workflows. Step level evidence further shows that substantial early progress can coexist with a final failure. These results show why final scores alone provide an incomplete account of web agent behavior and motivate human grounded, trajectory aware verification.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language model agents are increasingly expected to interpret goals, gather information, choose actions, and respond to changes in an external environment. Web agents provide a concrete setting for studying these abilities because many everyday and professional activities are mediated through browser interfaces. Early work explored web interaction from demonstrations and language instructions, while later systems combined reasoning and acting to support longer sequences of decisions ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Deng et al., 2023 ) . WebArena has become a central interactive benchmark: it provides reproducible websites for shopping, social discussion, software development, content management, and map based search, together with tasks whose completion can be checked against the environment state ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) . WebArena Lite, introduced as part of VisualAgentBench ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , retains 165 representative tasks and makes repeated evaluation more practical. Progress is usually summarized by the proportion of tasks accepted by an automatic evaluator. This number is useful, but it combines two distinct capabilities. One is whether the agent actually completes the task. The other is whether the evaluator recognizes that completion. Exact string matching, output formatting, partial state checks, and differences in stopping behavior can all change the recorded score without changing what the agent achieved. Unified environments such as BrowserGym improve execution consistency ( Le Sellier De Chezelles et al., 2024 ) , but they do not guarantee that every final judgment is correct. This distinction raises three questions. First, how often does the evaluator reject a run that satisfies the user request? Second, when a task truly fails, where does the failure begin and what behavior follows from it? Third, how much verified progress is made before the final outcome is assigned? A binary score cannot distinguish an agent that never reaches the relevant page from one that completes most requirements, reaches a correct state and then continues acting, or returns a semantically correct answer in an incompatible format. We study these questions through a human grounded audit of all 165 WebArena Lite tasks. We retain the original evaluator result and review failed runs against the task instruction, environment state, screenshots, execution records, and final response. We identify the first consequential error and assign a fine grained failure category. We also decompose tasks into verifiable subgoals and inspect progress after each action. The retained experiments are evaluation only: they compare GPT 5.5 under matched MASM and Guide Text conditions at 15 and 25 steps, together with an untrained Qwen3.5 9B ablation for MASM. No GPT generated trajectory is used to train or improve another model in the experiments reported here. The audit reveals a consistent gap between measured and observed performance. Human review recovers 5.45 to 8.49 percentage points across the four GPT 5.5 settings. Under a matched 25 step budget, Guide Text raises corrected success with MASM from 57 of 165 tasks (34.55%) to 63 of 165 (38.18%), although paired task flips show both gains and regressions. Among 102 failed GPT 5.5 runs in the strongest audited setting, scrolling loops, unfinished exploration, premature answers, invalid action formats, and incomplete workflows dominate. Progress records show that the first actions often produce the largest gains, whereas later steps are more often spent on verification, recovery, and repeated exploration. These findings are intended to improve how web agents are evaluated and debugged, rather than to make a leaderboard claim. Our contributions are: • We conduct a human grounded audit of all 165 WebArena Lite tasks under six evaluation conditions and quantify false negatives in four matched GPT 5.5 settings. • We identify the first consequential error in failed runs and provide a fine grained taxonomy that separates navigation, information gathering, action execution, stopping, and answer generation failures. • We assess task progress at the step level and show that the same final binary score can hide markedly different trajectories. • We use paired task evidence to analyze MASM, Guide Text, and action budget, and derive practical recommendations for evaluator design, stopping criteria, and deployment monitoring.
