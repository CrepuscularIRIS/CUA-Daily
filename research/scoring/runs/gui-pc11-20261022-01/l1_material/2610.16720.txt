title: Learn from Move: the Next Step for GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16720

§Abstract
Existing benchmarks for GUI agents primarily focus on increasing task difficulty by extending task horizons. Besides, enabling agents to explore unfamiliar environments and successfully complete tasks within them is also a critical yet challenging problem. We introduce LatentGUIWorld, a benchmark of six tasks in three matched pairs organized by the evidence needed for control: $I_0$ uses prior knowledge and available observations, $I_1$ requires interaction that directly reveals hidden semantics, and $I_2$ identifies dynamics from action-response relationships. Frontier models struggle especially on $I_2$ tasks. We introduce MOVE, which separates movement from button events and exposes intermediate visual feedback, together with demonstrations of iterative exploration and control. Ablations show that both action granularity and multi-step demonstrations improve performance. After SFT, LatentLearner-9B achieves 50.0% overall success on LatentGUIWorld, comparable to several frontier models. Joint RL further raises egocentric Drag and Ten-Choice success to 92.0% and 75.3%. Behavioral analysis reveals multi-step exploration. In egocentric Drag, RL increases Pearson correlation between error-normalized commands and ideal gain compensation from 0.610 to 0.803. Together, these behaviors show that models learn to explore through interaction and adapt their control to environment dynamics. The same format improves GUI grounding after RL, reaching 67.37% mean accuracy across five benchmarks versus 66.72% for conventional RL.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Multimodal agents combine visual perception, language understanding, and action to assist with a broad range of tasks. Computer use is a practical setting for these capabilities, spanning web browsing, document editing, and workflows across applications [ xie2024osworld ] . GUI agents carry out such tasks by interpreting screenshots and operating graphical user interfaces through mouse and keyboard actions, using the same interfaces available to human users. As GUI agents tackle more complex tasks, benchmarks increasingly emphasize longer workflows and sustained execution across applications [ xie2024osworld , yuan2026osworld2 ] . Yet completing long sequences of screen-based actions remains costly and unreliable [ song2025coact1 , zhou2026guivscli ] . These difficulties have motivated hybrid agents that combine GUI interaction with command-line execution, code, or application tools. CoAct-1 and OSWorld-MCP show how programmatic actions can reduce reliance on lengthy GUI sequences and improve task completion [ song2025coact1 , jia2025osworldmcp ] . A matched GUI–CLI study likewise finds that CLI agents with verifier-guided skill augmentation outperform screen-only GUI agents [ zhou2026guivscli ] . This shift motivates evaluating visual interaction beyond workflow length: what can an agent learn by acting on an unfamiliar interface and observing its response? Coding agents investigate unfamiliar repositories by reading code, running programs, and using test feedback to guide subsequent changes [ yang2024sweagent ] . GUI agents face an analogous need when the correct action depends on properties that are absent from the current screenshot. Consider a rotation task whose response direction and sensitivity vary across episodes. An agent may know the goal and possible control rules, yet still need to move, observe the resulting rotation, and infer how to complete the task. Existing benchmarks include information acquisition and adaptation, but seldom isolate the evidence required to identify such episode-specific properties. We therefore study whether GUI agents can identify these hidden properties from action-induced visual feedback and use what they learn to guide subsequent control. We introduce LatentGUIWorld, a multimodal benchmark of six short GUI tasks organized into three matched pairs. Its taxonomy classifies tasks by whether sufficient knowledge is available before a task-directed action, and by how missing knowledge must be acquired: I_{0} uses prior knowledge and observed context; I_{1} requires a probe outcome that directly reveals hidden semantics; and I_{2} requires interpreting a visual transition together with the action that caused it to identify hidden dynamics. The benchmark pairs exocentric Drag ( I_{0} ) with egocentric Drag ( I_{2} ), exocentric Ten-Choice ( I_{1} ) with its egocentric variant ( I_{2} ), and two Rotation geometries ( I_{2} – I_{2} ). These pairs preserve task objectives while varying the interaction mechanism, making environment identification an explicit difficulty dimension alongside task horizon. Our evaluations reveal large performance gaps when successful control requires identifying hidden environment properties. To expose the evidence needed for adaptation, we organize the interface around Move : cursor movement, mouse-down, and mouse-up are separately executable, and each primitive returns an observation before the next agent decision (Figure 1 ). We construct training trajectories that demonstrate iterative exploration and feedback-conditioned control, and train LatentLearner-9B on these trajectories. Ablations show that both action granularity and demonstration structure matter: overall success is 50.0% with the full interface and data, compared with 19.6% for bundled click/drag and 27.6% when each movement phase contains a single supervised move. Joint RL further improves control across the matched regimes (Section ). Egocentric Drag and Ten-Choice success rises from 41.3% and 40.7% after SFT to 92.0% and 75.3%, while exocentric success remains 96.7% and 100.0%. Paired Rotation training reaches 27.3% and 11.3% on the inner and outer rings, improving on the inner-ring specialist and matching the outer-ring specialist. Finally, applying the same interaction format to standard grounding yields 67.37% mean accuracy across five benchmarks after RL, compared with 66.72% for conventional grounding RL (Section ). Beyond success rates, the trained models exhibit feedback-conditioned multi-step exploration. In egocentric Drag, the Pearson correlation between error-normalized commands and ideal gain compensation 1/g increases from 0.610 after SFT to 0.803 after paired RL, indicating stronger compensation for episode-specific dynamics (Section ). Together, these results connect learning from visual interaction to both adaptation within the benchmark and broader GUI grounding.
