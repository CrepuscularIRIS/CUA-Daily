title: GUI-GenBench: Evaluating Image Generation Models as Interactive GUI Environments
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09007

§Abstract
An image generation model used as a graphical user interface (GUI) environment must produce not only a plausible screen but also the correct response to an action. Existing visual-generation benchmarks do not directly assess this combination of functional correctness and temporal consistency in GUIs. We introduce GUI-GenBench, a benchmark of 700 curated samples across five task suites: single-step transitions, multi-step planning, fictional-app generation, real-app trajectories, and coordinate-based grounding. We also introduce GUI-Score, a VLM-based metric that assesses Goal Achievement, Interaction Logic, Content Consistency, UI Plausibility, and Visual Quality. Evaluating 12 image generation models on Chinese and English subsets, we find that strong single-step performance does not consistently extend to coherent multi-step trajectories or accurate grounding. The highest aggregate GUI-Scores are 69.62 on the Chinese subset and 63.16 on the English subset. Qualitative analysis further identifies failures in text rendering, icon interpretation, and coordinate-conditioned transitions. Together, these results distinguish visual plausibility from functional reliability and identify limitations that must be addressed before generated GUIs can serve as dependable interaction environments.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Image generation models [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] can synthesize a new graphical user interface (GUI) state from a current screen and a user instruction. This capability motivates their use as generative GUI environments : models that produce visual responses to actions, with potential applications in agent training and evaluation [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . Conventional interaction environments depend on implemented dynamics or executable software [ 13 , 14 , 15 , 7 ] . Generating screens offers a complementary way to construct diverse interface examples and trajectories [ 16 , 17 ] , but raises a basic question: do the generated states faithfully reflect the requested interaction? Existing work on visual generation and evaluation examines fidelity, semantic alignment, composition, and temporal consistency [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ] . These properties alone do not establish the functional correctness of GUI transitions [ 26 , 8 ] . As Figure 1 illustrates, a GUI environment must model discrete, action-triggered changes [ 7 ] : a single click can replace an entire screen, while a small text or control-state error can change the meaning of the response. Correct transitions require coordinate grounding [ 16 , 27 ] , icon interpretation [ 17 , 26 ] , and readable text [ 28 ] , together with logical continuity across changing layouts [ 29 ] . Evaluation must therefore test both what a screen looks like and whether it represents the intended state. We introduce GUI-GenBench to evaluate image generation models along these functional and visual requirements. Its 700 curated samples span five task suites, from local state transitions to multi-step trajectories, fictional scenarios, and coordinate-based interactions. We pair the benchmark with GUI-Score , a rubric-based metric evaluated by vision-language models (VLMs) [ 30 , 1 , 31 ] . Five scoring dimensions separate goal achievement and interaction logic from content consistency, UI plausibility, and visual quality. This distinction makes it possible to identify outputs that look convincing but fail to realize the requested action. Our evaluation covers 12 image generation models [ 32 , 3 , 4 , 33 , 5 , 6 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 ] . Leading models score above 80 on single-step transitions, yet most models obtain lower scores on multi-step planning. Grounding remains difficult even when the generated screen is visually plausible. Qualitative examples reveal errors in Chinese text rendering, icon interpretation, and action-target localization. These findings motivate evaluating functional reliability separately from visual fidelity, rather than treating realistic appearance as evidence of correct interaction. Our primary contributions are as follows: 1. We introduce GUI-GenBench , with 700 samples across five task suites for evaluating generated GUI states and trajectories. 2. We introduce GUI-Score , a five-dimensional rubric-based metric that separates functional correctness, consistency, and visual fidelity. 3. We evaluate 12 models on Chinese and English subsets, identifying limitations in multi-step coherence and grounding, with qualitative diagnoses of text, icon, and coordinate-related errors.
