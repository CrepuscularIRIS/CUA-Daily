title: Player-1: Learning to Play from Synthetic Experience
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20584

§Abstract
Computer-use agents have demonstrated strong capabilities in interpreting screens and executing keyboard and mouse actions. However, games, with their visually rich worlds and diverse mechanics, remain challenging and offer a demanding next step toward general digital control. Current game-agent learning pipelines often rely on large-scale demonstrations with limited coverage of dynamic game states, while scalable environments for interactive training remain difficult to obtain. We introduce Player-1, a post-training framework for learning game control beyond demonstrations through synthetic experience. We extend the computer-use action space with relative pointer motion, sustained inputs, and composite actions. For supervised initialization, we fine-tune computer-use models on 6,044 curated gameplay trajectories that pair recorded actions with task instructions and visual reasoning. We construct Player-1-Gym, sixteen synthetic 2D and 3D environments designed around control demands shared across games. Programmatically verifiable rewards and randomized observations and control dynamics support multi-environment reinforcement learning with PPO. The resulting Player-1-9B and Player-1-35B-A3B improve MineDojo-300 success over their SFT policies by 27.8 and 17.7 percentage points, respectively. Player-1-9B reaches 38.33% success compared with 12.63% for the strongest evaluated baseline, with further gains over SFT on ViZDoom-10 and GameWorld. Further analyses show that broader synthetic environment coverage improves transfer, while the use of composite actions that combine multiple keyboard and mouse inputs increases during RL. We demonstrate that learning from synthetic experience substantially improves game control beyond supervised initialization, with gains that generalize beyond the RL training suite.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Foundation models have developed broad capabilities in visual understanding, reasoning, and instruction following ( OpenAI, 2024 ; Anthropic, 2025 ; Gemini Team, 2025 ) . Computer-use agents bring these capabilities to digital interfaces, interpreting screens and executing keyboard and mouse actions ( Wang et al., 2025a ; Bai et al., 2025 ; Lu et al., 2026 ) . Games offer a demanding next step toward general digital control: their visually complex environments and diverse mechanics require precise, sustained, and concurrent control under dynamic and rapidly changing visual feedback ( Luo et al., 2026a ) . Recent game agents learn from large-scale gameplay demonstrations and interactive experience ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Magne et al., 2026 ; SIMA Team, 2025 ; Shi et al., 2026 ) . Yet demonstrations cover only a limited portion of the states an agent may encounter in open-ended tasks, while scalable environments for interactive training remain difficult to obtain. Despite differences in appearance and rules, we find that many games share recurring control demands such as precise aiming, sustained movement, and coordinated inputs. This motivates our central question: Can synthetic experience advance game-control capabilities beyond supervised initialization, with gains that generalize to games outside the RL training suite? Three challenges shape this approach. First, gameplay demonstrations recorded with heterogeneous input conventions and temporal resolutions must be converted into a shared action space, and paired with task instructions and visual reasoning that reflect the player’s intent. Second, learning beyond demonstrations requires feedback on states reached by the policy’s own actions, but implementing programmatic rewards across diverse commercial games is difficult to scale. Third, experience from environments with different control demands, dynamics, and reward scales must be incorporated into a shared policy that generalizes beyond the training suite. These challenges motivate our supervised initialization from curated demonstrations, followed by learning in controllable synthetic environments with verifiable rewards. We present Player-1 , a post-training framework for learning game control beyond demonstrations through synthetic experience, as illustrated in Figure 1 . We extend the computer-use action space with relative pointer motion, sustained inputs, and composite actions. Curated gameplay demonstrations pair recorded actions with task instructions and visual reasoning to initialize game control. Player-1-Gym then provides sixteen synthetic 2D and 3D environments designed around recurring control demands across aiming, combat, driving, navigation, and UI interaction. Programmatic game-state rewards and randomization of observations and control dynamics support multi-environment reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), yielding Player-1-9B and Player-1-35B-A3B. Our results demonstrate that learning from synthetic experience improves game-control capabilities, with gains extending beyond the RL training suite. On MineDojo-300, Player-1-9B improves from 10.54% success after SFT to 38.33%, exceeding the strongest evaluated external baseline at 12.63% under our evaluation protocol. The same policy also improves on ViZDoom-10 and GameWorld, both outside the RL training suite.
