title: Seg2Click: Learning Pixel-Level Action Masks for GUI Visual Grounding
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16234

§Abstract
Visual grounding is a central challenge for GUI agents, requiring an instruction to be mapped to a target within a crowded screenshot. Coordinate generation with single-point supervision reduces a spatially extended target to one position, despite the presence of many valid click locations. Region-based patch scoring alleviates this ambiguity, but its spatial decisions remain tied to visual-token footprints that may span the target, neighboring controls, and background. To address these limitations, we propose Seg2Click, which reformulates GUI grounding as executable-region prediction: identifying where a click can validly execute the given instruction. Using bounding-box supervision, Seg2Click predicts an executable-region mask and computes the final click point through a deterministic rule. This formulation captures the spatial extent of valid click locations and enables finer localization beyond the visual-token grid, making it well suited to small, densely arranged controls without requiring a verifier or zoom-in pass. Experiments across five backbone configurations from two model families demonstrate the effectiveness of Seg2Click. Seg2Click-9B achieves 92.06\%, 94.10\%, and 55.22\% on ScreenSpot, ScreenSpot-v2, and ScreenSpot-Pro, respectively, while matched-backbone comparisons show improvements over both coordinate generation and patch-based grounding. Code will be available at https://github.com/jcwang0602/Seg2Click.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
GUI agents carry out user instructions across web, mobile, and desktop environments ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Rawles et al., 2025 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) . A key capability is visual grounding: locating the target specified by an instruction within a screenshot ( Cheng et al., 2024 ; Gou et al., 2025 ; Qin et al., 2025 ) . This task is particularly challenging in dense professional interfaces, where small controls are often placed close together ( Li et al., 2025 ) . Recognizing the relevant toolbar or control group is insufficient: successful interaction requires placing the click within the specific element named by the instruction. Existing approaches commonly address this localization problem through either direct coordinate generation or relevance prediction over visual tokens. Coordinate-generation methods represent screen positions as text and directly predict a click location ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025b ) . Under single-point supervision, these methods are trained to reproduce one annotated coordinate, even though many locations within the target may be equally valid. For example, different clicks inside a button can trigger the same action, whereas a nearby click just outside its boundary may activate another control. Single-point supervision does not explicitly capture this distinction, because it penalizes deviations from the annotated point regardless of whether they remain within the target. This mismatch motivates predicting the valid target region before selecting a specific click location. Visual-token-based methods move toward region prediction by assigning relevance scores to image patches. In particular, GUI-Actor supervises all visual patches that overlap the target, allowing multiple patches to receive positive supervision ( Wu et al., 2025a ) . Although this approach relaxes the commitment to a single annotated point, its spatial decisions remain tied to the visual-token grid. A single token footprint may cover part of the target, neighboring controls, and background, yet receive only one relevance score. Interpolating these scores increases output resolution but does not introduce independently learned predictions within each footprint. Thus, moving from point supervision to region supervision leaves a granularity bottleneck unresolved, especially for small, densely arranged controls. The remaining challenge is to preserve region-based supervision while learning spatial decisions finer than the visual tokens that represent the screenshot. To address this challenge, we propose Seg2Click, which reformulates GUI grounding as executable-region prediction: predicting the region in which a click can validly execute the instruction, then computing the click point using a deterministic rule. We instantiate this formulation as box-supervised sub-token action-field prediction, learning distinct scores within each visual-token footprint (Figure 2 ). A query-conditioned pixel-shuffle decoder ( Shi et al., 2016 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) combines aligned vision and language-model features to produce an executable-region mask at the processed screenshot resolution. Target bounding boxes provide supervision, and the centroid of the mask’s largest connected component determines the final click. This formulation addresses the two limitations above: region supervision accommodates multiple valid click locations, while sub-token prediction enables finer spatial distinctions within token footprints. It requires no additional contour annotations, verifier, or zoom-in pass. To assess whether these changes translate into more accurate grounding, we first compare Seg2Click with coordinate generation and patch scoring, then evaluate its applicability across backbones. Under matched backbone, training data, and optimization settings, Seg2Click-0.8B produces 24 more correct clicks than coordinate generation on ScreenSpot-Pro. On the same 0.8B backbone, it also outperforms reproduced GUI-Actor by 4.87 percentage points, corresponding to 77 additional correct clicks (Table 6 ). Beyond these comparisons, experiments cover five backbone configurations from two model families. Seg2Click achieves 47.00% on ScreenSpot-Pro with Qwen2.5-VL-3B and 55.22% with Qwen3.5-9B, supporting the effectiveness of executable-region prediction across model families and scales (Figure 1 ).
