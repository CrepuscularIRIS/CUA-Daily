title: EngiWorld: What Can Frontier Agents Deliver in Professional Engineering Environments?
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37686

§Abstract
Autonomous agents have made rapid progress in general-purpose computer use, yet reliable automation of professional industrial engineering remains out of reach. Engineering workflows require reasoning over geometric and physical constraints and keeping dependencies consistent across software and design stages. We present EngiWorld, a benchmark designed around the capabilities that real engineering projects require, from operating a single application to solving engineering problems end to end. It contains 1,301 expert-curated tasks spanning six engineering domains (CAD, CAE, CAM, BIM, EDA, and DCC) and 26 professional software applications, with both GUI and CLI interfaces. The tasks are organized into six types, corresponding to capabilities that real engineering projects require. We further introduce an artifact-centric evaluation methodology built on a unified suite of domain verifiers. The verifiers programmatically check whether final and intermediate artifacts are geometrically valid, physically feasible, and compliant with design rules. Beyond binary success, they also score how well optimized designs meet their specifications. Evaluation of seven frontier models reveals a substantial capability gap: the strongest model scores only 44.7/100, and just 3.6% of runs on tasks that require cross-software coordination succeed. EngiWorld provides a rigorous foundation for measuring progress toward agents that operate professional engineering software end to end.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language models (LLMs), multimodal large language models (MLLMs), and agents built on them have made transformative progress on general computer-use tasks, and can now plan, perceive, and act across digital environments spanning web navigation, office productivity, and software engineering [ 5 , 45 , 41 , 34 , 15 , 13 , 42 , 11 , 19 ] . However, their potential in professional industrial design software, a domain of enormous economic and societal value, remains largely unexplored [ 35 , 7 , 25 ] . Although existing benchmarks evaluate general digital workflows well, they show three major limitations in industrial domains. First , lack of artifact verification. Benchmarks such as OSWorld [ 41 ] , Spider2-V [ 2 ] , and ScienceBoard [ 37 ] check output files and application states against reference files or expected values. Such checks suit office and data workflows but cannot establish whether an engineering artifact is valid: an engineering model that looks correct may still be unusable because of non-manifold geometry or improper topological constraints [ 40 , 12 , 21 ] . FEABench [ 29 ] , BIM-Edit [ 30 ] , and CADTestBench [ 27 ] have begun to validate artifact quality, but cover only a single software application and a single task format. Second , lack of long-horizon interaction. Text2CAD [ 16 ] focuses on single-turn generation, and although CADWorld [ 6 ] introduces GUI interaction, its workflows stay within a single application (FreeCAD). But industrial tasks require maintaining parametric logic over long operation sequences and across applications, where an early error can invalidate all subsequent operations [ 28 , 8 ] . Third , lack of native software feedback. Tool-using engineering agents such as ChatEDA [ 39 ] and Foam-Agent [ 44 ] observe only textual signals such as execution logs and error messages, and GUI-EDA [ 22 ] even resorts to offline single-step prediction without executing any action. In contrast, engineers rely on native feedback from domain software, such as rendered outputs, simulation convergence curves, and design-rule violation reports, to iteratively refine their designs. More fundamentally, a real engineering project requires a range of capabilities from an engineer. The basic capability is to operate professional software to build, modify, and simulate designs. On this basis, the engineer must also choose suitable software for a requirement, read design intent from engineering drawings, improve a design against quantitative objectives under engineering constraints, coordinate work across several applications when one application cannot meet the requirement, and solve open-ended problems on a computer without preinstalled engineering software. Yet existing benchmarks cover only part of these capabilities. To bridge this gap, we introduce EngiWorld , a benchmark designed around these capabilities for evaluating agents in professional industrial design environments. As shown in Figure 1 , EngiWorld spans six engineering domains, namely computer-aided design (CAD), computer-aided engineering (CAE), computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), building information modeling (BIM), electronic design automation (EDA), and digital content creation (DCC), and contains 1,301 expert-curated tasks across 26 software applications. The tasks are grounded in real engineering processes [ 7 ] and require agents to interact with real professional software over multiple turns through GUI or CLI interfaces. The tasks in EngiWorld are organized into six types. Single-Software Execution tests the basic capability of operating one application. The other five types go beyond it: Software Selection tests choosing suitable software, Vision-Guided Modeling tests working from engineering drawings, Design Optimization tests optimizing a design under constraints, Cross-Software Coordination tests coordinating several applications, and Open-Environment Engineering tests solving problems on a computer without preinstalled engineering software (Section 3.1 ). Furthermore, we propose an artifact-centric evaluation methodology . Because industrial artifacts rarely have a unique correct answer, we do not compare them with a single reference. Instead, we extend artifact-level programmatic verification from single-domain benchmarks [ 29 , 30 , 27 , 36 ] into a unified suite of domain verifiers spanning all six domains, which check the geometric accuracy, physical feasibility, and rule compliance of both final and intermediate artifacts (e.g., STEP files, netlists, and G-code). Unlike most interactive benchmarks, which judge only success or failure, EngiWorld assigns continuous scores to Design Optimization tasks: designs first pass feasibility checks and then receive quality scores according to how well they meet their specifications.
