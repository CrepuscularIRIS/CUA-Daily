title: AOS-Bench: Can LLM Agents Manage Dynamic Workloads?
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33178

§Abstract
Coding agents are moving from handling one request at a time to serving as persistent assistants to which users hand a stream of work: a build, a data-processing job and a bug fix may all be pending on the same machine at once. Such an agent must decide when to take on new tasks, how to delegate and overlap them, and how to stay within the machine's CPU and memory. Existing benchmarks cannot measure this, because they evaluate each task in isolation. We introduce AOS-Bench, which evaluates an agent system as a workload manager: verified tasks arrive over time and must be delivered under shared CPU and memory limits, and the same tasks run one at a time serve as a per-system reference. Across five model--harness pairs, isolated task success does not carry over evenly. On heavy workloads every system loses 13 to 23 points. On medium workloads, systems span 11 points in isolated success but 26 points in stream success, and two systems 0.6 points apart in isolation deliver 54\% and 68\% of the same tasks. Execution-policy interventions trace much of this gap to task intake: serial dispatch leaves 73\% of heavy-workload tasks unclaimed. Yet more elaborate management does not necessarily help. A fixed concurrency of two comes within four points of the best agent-managed policy on both medium and heavy workloads, and under every policy median CPU use stays below 30\% of the provisioned cores while tasks wait. These results identify workload management as a distinct capability for agents, and AOS-Bench as a way to measure it.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Advances in coding and computer-use agents let users delegate a growing range of computer tasks through natural language. Benchmarks such as Terminal-Bench and OSWorld typically use individual tasks as the unit of evaluation ( Merrill et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) . New requests may arrive before earlier work is finished, such as file processing requested during a software build. We call the collection of requests handled in a shared computing environment over time a workload . In a task-by-task workflow, users launch separate agent sessions and remain responsible for coordinating their work. We envision delegating this responsibility to a persistent agent system that manages an evolving workload on the user’s computer. The agent must construct each task’s execution plan while deciding when to accept more work, how to delegate it, and which activities to overlap. Task solving and workload management are therefore coupled. Overlapping execution can use resources left idle during model calls or tool waits, but can also introduce contention and coordination overhead under shared CPU and memory constraints. Performance on isolated tasks does not establish how well an agent maintains progress across requests, or whether its execution decisions improve workload delivery and efficiency. This motivates our central question: Can current frontier agent systems effectively manage dynamic workloads under shared resource constraints? We call this role Agent as a Workload Manager . Figure 1 illustrates the role through a limited analogy with an operating system organizing applications. We introduce AOS-Bench , which makes a complete workload the unit of evaluation. A persistent agent system handles tasks from existing CLI benchmarks in a shared environment under fixed CPU and memory limits. To distinguish workload management from task execution, we evaluate each system in two settings: individual (each task runs alone, providing an isolated capability baseline) and stream (the same tasks arrive over time in a shared environment). We compare verified delivery and execution efficiency across the two settings. Our evaluation of five model–harness pairs shows that how much isolated task performance carries over to a shared workload differs widely across systems: every system loses 13.0 to 22.7 points on the heavy tier, and on the medium tier systems span only 11 points in individual SR but 26 points in stream SR, with no system holding the same rank in both orderings. The sharpest case is a pair of systems whose individual baselines differ by 0.6 points on medium workloads (74.9% and 74.3%) yet deliver 53.9% and 68.1% on the same task set. Execution-policy interventions identify task intake as a primary factor limiting delivery: serial dispatch leaves 73% of heavy-tier tasks unclaimed while native management claims three quarters of them, and trial-level attribution finds task-solving failures in at most one scored trial per system, so most of the delivery gap is carried by orchestration outcomes. Managing a workload well, however, is not the same as managing it cleverly: a fixed concurrency of two — a constant with no workload reasoning — comes within four points of the best agent-managed policy on both tiers, guidance raises the share of tasks claimed on heavy workloads while lowering delivery, and no policy exceeds 30% median utilization of the four provisioned cores while tasks wait. A thirty-task case study shows a single system spanning the full outcome range across identical repeats, suggesting that single-task-at-a-time execution can be a stochastic failure mode. Our contributions are: • A formulation of agent workload management and the AOS-Bench benchmark with construction pipeline, execution protocol, and metrics. • A comparative evaluation of five model–harness pairs showing that the share of isolated task performance a system retains under a shared workload differs widely across systems and reorders them on medium workloads. • Execution-policy interventions identifying task intake as a primary driver of delivery gaps, and showing that avoiding serial execution captures most of the available gain while dynamic management yields no measurable advantage over a fixed concurrency constant.
