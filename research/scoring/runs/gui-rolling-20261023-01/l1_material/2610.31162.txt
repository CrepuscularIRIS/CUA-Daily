title: HiSTEVE 1.0: Harnessing Human-Interface VLA Agents for Open-World Games
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31162

§Abstract
Building virtual embodied agents that operate in open-ended games like human players is a long-standing goal requiring both long-horizon autonomy and reliable native-interface control. As a procedurally generated open world, Minecraft provides a natural testbed for studying the integration of these capabilities. Existing agents typically provide either native visuomotor control without long-horizon autonomy or high-level planning executed through environment APIs or predefined skills. To bridge this gap, we propose HiSTEVE, a hierarchical VLA agent with a capability-aware agent harness. At the orchestration level, we equip the harness with structured memory for tracking progress and a VLM planner for selecting subtasks within the VLA's capabilities. To reliably determine subtask completion, we further introduce a policy-coupled task completion state detector that conditions on VLA policy context to signal candidate completion. At the control level, a single VLA executes all subtasks with vision-only perception and native keyboard-and-mouse actions. To mitigate interference between heterogeneous GUI and non-GUI interactions, we introduce regime-specific RMSNorm branches and separate mouse-movement token embeddings. We also train the VLA through world knowledge injection, imitation learning, and reinforcement learning with environment feedback to improve reliability and recovery. HiSTEVE achieves state-of-the-art atomic-task performance, scoring 85.0% on MineStudio Bench and a 37.4% average success rate on OpenHA, while also completing long-horizon tasks such as crafting an iron pickaxe from scratch like human players.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
A long-standing goal in embodied intelligence is to build virtual embodied agents ( Fung et al., 2025 ) that can operate in open-ended games under the same interaction constraints as human players. Achieving this goal requires two complementary capabilities: long-horizon autonomy to maintain goals and adapt plans; and reliable native-interface control to translate first-person visual observations into keyboard-and-mouse actions. As a procedurally generated open world, Minecraft provides a natural testbed for studying these capabilities by combining partially observable, compositional tasks with diverse visuomotor interactions ( Fan et al., 2022 ; Zheng et al., 2023 ) . A generally capable Minecraft agent must therefore integrate long-horizon planning with native-interface execution in a closed loop, continually updating its decisions from visual feedback. Existing Minecraft agents have primarily emphasized one of these two capabilities. On the one hand, visuomotor policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models address native-interface control by directly mapping raw first-person observations to keyboard-and-mouse actions ( Baker et al., 2022 ; Lifshitz et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2025 ) . However, these models typically lack mechanisms for persistently tracking task state and explicitly reasoning over task dependencies, limiting their long-horizon autonomy. On the other hand, agents driven by large language models (LLMs) or vision-language models (VLMs) support long-horizon reasoning through task decomposition planning ( Wang et al., 2023a ; Zhu et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024b ; Li et al., 2024 ) . However, many of these methods still rely on environment APIs, predefined skills, or functional actions for low-level execution, which may be unavailable in most games. Although recent work has begun to combine high-level planning with learned policies ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Park et al., 2025 ) , integrating task-state tracking, completion verification, and failure recovery remains an open challenge. Satisfying both requirements introduces coupled challenges at the planning and action timescales. At the planning timescale, long-horizon autonomy requires tracking the objective, progress, and execution history across subtasks, while ensuring that plans are consistent with Minecraft mechanics and remain within the lower-level policy’s capabilities ( Wang et al., 2023b ) . The agent must also determine when to terminate the current subtask: terminating too early leaves the subtask unfinished, whereas terminating too late wastes actions and may even undo existing progress. At the action timescale, native-interface control requires a single VLA policy to operate reliably across heterogeneous task distributions. In particular, non-GUI control and GUI interaction differ in both visual distributions and mouse-action semantics: mouse movement rotates the camera in the former but displaces the cursor in the latter, making naive joint training susceptible to negative transfer ( He et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026 ) . Each VLA invocation must also be reliable, since even small per-subtask failure risks accumulate over repeated invocations in long-horizon tasks. To address these challenges, we propose HiSTEVE, a hierarchical VLA agent that combines a capability-aware harness for long-horizon autonomy and reliable native-interface control. At the slower planning timescale, we design the harness with a structured memory of task progress and a VLM planner that selects the next atomic subtask. To close the planning–execution loop, we introduce a policy-coupled task completion state detector that conditions on VLA policy context to signal candidate completion. The harness then verifies the task state and decides whether to advance, recover, or replan. At the faster action timescale, the VLA predicts native keyboard-and-mouse actions from the subtask instruction and first-person visual observation. To mitigate negative transfer across heterogeneous interaction regimes, we introduce regime-specific RMSNorm branches and separate mouse-movement token embeddings for non-GUI and GUI tasks. Following world knowledge injection and imitation learning, we further finetune the VLA through reinforcement learning (RL) to improve recovery when execution deviates from demonstration trajectories. We comprehensively evaluate HiSTEVE on atomic and long-horizon benchmarks. Compared with the strongest baseline in our main comparison, HiSTEVE improves average success by 20.0 percentage points on MineStudio Bench and 0.6 points on OpenHA, with family-level gains of up to 28.6 points on LongBench. The largest long-horizon improvements occur in tasks requiring multi-stage resource production or the reuse of information gathered during exploration. Ablations reveal that stronger atomic execution alone does not yield comparable gains in long-horizon performance.
