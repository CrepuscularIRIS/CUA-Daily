title: Rational Use of Time for Social Coordination in Humans and AI
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14766

§Abstract
All social interactions are situated in time. Previous studies have shown that humans are able to use implicit temporal cues, such as the duration of a pause, to infer others' mental states. But how do humans use temporal cues for dynamic, fine-grained social coordination? And can AI systems do the same, without ``experiencing'' continuous real-time as humans do? We study these questions using a cooperative card game task called The Mind, which requires players to achieve a shared goal across repeated interactions but forbids explicit communication: the only available signal is the passage of time. We evaluate 150 humans, and two classes of AI models which reflect different ways of operationalizing time in artificial systems: computer-use agents (CUAs), and standard text-prompted LLMs. We find that human behavior is best captured by a Bayesian strategy that infers a partner's belief from their timing---and similarly for frontier models. However, unlike humans, none of the tested models seem to efficiently adapt their behaviors to their partners' over the course of the game. From a cognitive perspective, our findings suggest that humans perform implicit probabilistic reasoning about continuous-time signals in dynamic social settings, and that such reasoning mechanisms may emerge without real-time experience. Practically, our study highlights new challenges for AI systems to coordinate efficiently with other agents by learning to interpret and adapt to their implicit signals in dynamic settings.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Humans draw rich social inferences through all kinds of signals, from linguistic expressions to gestures to the placement of objects. But humans also infer complex information about other agents’ mental states in the absence of explicit communicative signal: that is, based on the passage of time . For example, the duration of silence before an utterance in a conversation can provide cues into a speaker’s trustworthiness or cognitive state ( Christenfeld, 1995 ; Kohtz and Niebuhr, 2017 ; Ziano and Wang, 2021 , e.g.,) , and solving a complex task in a short amount of time might suggest that the agent had prior knowledge about the task ( Berke et al., 2026 , e.g.,) . These findings provide evidence that humans use temporal information to draw one-shot social inferences, but we still lack a systematic account of how humans use time as a social signal to dynamically coordinate with other participants. It also remains unclear whether AI systems can similarly use temporal information for social coordination. As AI agents are increasingly used in real-time interactions ( Nakano et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2026 , e.g.,) , it seems desirable for AI agents to leverage time as an information source about human users or other agents. However, while large language models (LLMs) have been evaluated in diverse social tasks ( Tran et al., 2025 ; Sidji et al., 2024 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Yim et al., 2024 ) , most studies have investigated communication based on explicit signals, such as words or non-linguistic symbols. And studies of temporal reasoning typically represent time using explicit timestamps or durations ( Fatemi et al., 2025 ; Gupta et al., 2023 ; Wang and Zhao, 2024 ) , leaving underexplored how AI systems perform in settings where time unfolds continuously and must be inferred through interaction. Moreover, investigating time-based social coordination in AI models is not just motivated on practical grounds. It is not even clear whether current AI models are the right kind of thing to be able to use time like humans do. LLMs can only iteratively receive discrete prompts, instead of existing in the continuous flow of real-time like biological organisms. Even Computer-Use Agents (CUAs), which can dynamically interact with graphical user interfaces, still perceive the environment through discrete screenshots rather than continuous visual input. It is therefore worth investigating whether such AI systems can perform complex collaborative tasks that require time-based inferences. Building upon prior work using games to investigate cognition in humans ( Newell and Simon, 1972 ; Dubey et al., 2018 ; Allen et al., 2024 ) and AI models ( Silver et al., 2016 ; Vinyals et al., 2019 ; Bakhtin et al., 2022 ; Liang et al., 2025 ) , we evaluate humans and models on a cooperative task based on a card game called The Mind . In our simplified version of the game, there are two players. On level n , each player is randomly dealt n cards from a deck of cards numbered 1 through 100. The players share a cooperative goal: play all dealt cards in ascending order without any communication (no talking, gesturing, winking, etc.). Beyond the cards in their own hands and cards that have been played, the only information players have is the passage of time. This form of coordination lacks any kind of explicit signaling or grounding of symbols into meanings---and yet, people successfully play the game, with minimal training. They even find it fun. Using The Mind , we focus on one underexplored aspect of social interaction: coordination in the absence of explicit signals. We investigate two main questions: How do humans use temporal cues for dynamic social coordination? And can AI systems also coordinate using such cues, despite not “experiencing” continuous real time? We break these questions down into the following subquestions: • RQ1 (Performance): How successfully and efficiently do humans and models perform? • RQ2 (Strategy): What cognitive strategy best characterizes human and model behavior? • RQ3 (Adaptation): Do humans and models adapt their behaviors to increase success and efficiency with their partner across repeated interactions? • RQ4 (Behavior Consistency): Do models show consistent behavior when prompted to reason about time at different granularities? We implement five types of cognitive strategies as fixed partners, and evaluate how three types of players interact with them: (1) humans; (2) CUAs, which operate through a real-time graphical user interface; and (3) standard LLMs, which can only reason about time through prompts. We find that both humans’ and frontier LLMs’ behaviors are best characterized by a Bayesian strategy that reasons about time to infer the partner’s state ( Section 4.2 ). However, although frontier LLMs can match or even outperform humans in task success ( Section 4.1 ), humans show greater adaptation to their partners’ behavior over the course of the game ( Section 4.3 ).
