title: xDailyBench: Benchmarking LLMs on Professional Consultation for Real-Life Problems
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07784

§Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for everyday assistance, yet existing benchmarks only partially reflect the requests users naturally make in practice. Real-world requests are often open-ended, casually specified, and context-dependent, requiring models not only to follow explicit instructions but also to infer unstated needs from user background and situational context. We introduce xDailyBench, a benchmark of 248 carefully curated tasks spanning 51 scenarios across personal life, white-collar work, learning and research, and cross-domain activities. The tasks are grounded in requests that users have actually completed or genuinely intended to accomplish with AI, and are evaluated with fine-grained binary rubrics covering both explicit and implicit requirements. We evaluate 11 frontier models under standardized agentic settings. The best models achieve a task-level score of 75.6\%, while all models perform substantially worse on implicit than explicit requirements, with gaps no less than 9 percentage points. These results reveal implicit requirement inference as a persistent bottleneck for reliably satisfying real-world everyday user needs.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Agent capabilities have become the primary focus of frontier Large Language Models (LLMs) development, shifting the focus of progress from isolated capabilities toward reliable completion of complex real-world tasks. Consequently, recent benchmarks increasingly evaluate whether models can autonomously perform end-to-end work in authentic environments across diverse professional domains, such as software engineering, scientific research, web interaction, and computer use [ Jimenez et al.(2024)Jimenez, Yang, Wettig, Yao, Pei, Press, and Narasimhan , Boisvert et al.(2024)Boisvert, Thakkar, Gasse, Caccia, De Chezelles, Cappart, Chapados, Lacoste, and Drouin , Shi et al.(2026)Shi, Li, Ma, Tu, Wu, Xu, Yang, Wu, Xie, Wu, et al. , Wang et al.(2024)Wang, Cui, Zhong, Zhang, Yin, Lin, and Shang ] . Rather than measuring individual reasoning or tool-use skills in isolation, these evaluations emphasize a model’s ability to coordinate planning, information gathering, tool invocation, and decision making to accomplish practical objectives [ Patwardhan et al.(2026)Patwardhan, Dias, Proehl, Kim, Wang, Watkins, Fishman, Aljubeh, Thacker, Fauconnet, et al. , Yang et al.(2026)Yang, Liu, Li, Bai, Chen, Chen, Duan, Dong, Hu, Jia, et al. ] . As frontier models continue to improve, these benchmarks have rapidly evolved toward increasingly complex, long-horizon, and realistic tasks [ Shi et al.(2026)Shi, Li, Ma, Tu, Wu, Xu, Yang, Wu, Xie, Wu, et al. , Deng et al.(2025)Deng, Da, Pan, He, Ide, Garg, Lauffer, Park, Pasari, Rane, et al. , Ding et al.(2025)Ding, Long, Pu, Zhou, Gao, Gao, He, Hou, Hu, Li, et al. ] , continuously raising the bar for evaluating agent capabilities. However, autonomous execution of professional workflows represents only one practical role of AI agents. In real-world applications, frontier LLMs are increasingly expected to act as professional assistants helping users solve problems encountered in work, learning, and everyday life, particularly when users lack the expertise required to complete these tasks independently. Such scenarios typically require models to interpret underspecified requests, identify implicit and personalized constraints, integrate domain-specific knowledge with user context, and provide directly actionable recommendations rather than standardized answers. Consequently, evaluating these tasks demands a broader assessment of an agent’s ability to reason under ambiguity, personalize solutions, and deliver reliable professional assistance. Despite the growing prevalence of this usage pattern, existing benchmarks remain largely centered on autonomous completion of professional productivity tasks, leaving this increasingly important capability unexplored. To bridge this gap, we introduce xDailyBench, a benchmark evaluating LLMs on realistic, open-ended tasks derived from authentic everyday user scenarios. To model the breadth of real user needs rather than constructing tasks from fixed benchmark templates, we surveyed more than 1,000 participants and asked them to contribute tasks that they had actually completed or genuinely intended to accomplish with AI, together with their original instructions and, when relevant, contextual workspace files. After quality screening and cross review, the final benchmark contains 248 tasks organized into four top-level domains—personal life, white-collar work, learning and research, and other cross-domain needs—and 51 fine-grained scenarios. These scenarios range from legal consultation, shopping decisions, family and major-life planning to project management, data analysis, document creation, and academic writing. Instead of rewriting the submissions as fully specified benchmark problems, we preserve the characteristics of practical interactions, including underspecified objectives, implicit and personalized requirements, dispersed constraints, rich contextual information, and multiple acceptable solutions. To evaluate such tasks without relying on a unique reference answer, we adopt a rubric-based framework in which each task is assessed against a set of task-specific criteria. These criteria are further organized under an interpretable capability taxonomy, enabling xDailyBench to measure both end-to-end task success and the underlying capabilities responsible for model performance.
