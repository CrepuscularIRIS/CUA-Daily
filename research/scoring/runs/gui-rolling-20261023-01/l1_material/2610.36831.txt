arxiv: 2610.36831 (2610.36831v1)
title: Right Click, Wrong Box: Grading GUI Grounding by What a Click Does
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36831

§Abstract
Leading GUI grounders score 93 to 96% on ScreenSpot-v2, and reported gaps between new and prior models are about a point. At this level the useful question is what the remaining errors are. Benchmarks count a click as correct only inside one annotated box, but interfaces often expose one action through several elements: a duplicated link, the label of a checkbox, the title next to a logo. The box is not wrong. The rule is incomplete. We call a click correct when executing it produces the same outcome as clicking the annotated target. A probe that executes and fingerprints about a thousand clicks per page turns this definition into outcome maps and an execution judge for live web applications, with no new annotation. We execute the predictions of three strong 7--8B grounders on a code forge and an online shop. About one in five and one in three of the clicks that box scoring rejects reach the intended outcome, and the shares remain one in seven and one in three when the instructions are rewritten as natural tasks. A blind audit of 11 models on ScreenSpot-v2 (three 7--8B grounders and eight 3B variants) finds the same effect on web screenshots, with two independent reviewers on a 60-unit subset. The share is highest for the strongest grounders. Training seeds and test-set sampling add noise that more runs and paired intervals control. Box scoring instead under-counts correct clicks, which repetition does not remove, and the audit-based correction of 0.2 to 1.8 points on ScreenSpot-v2 is as large as the gaps the field reports. The correction is needed in evaluation. In our 3B GRPO study, crediting equivalent clicks in the reward gives no stable gain over box rewards across three seeds and four test sets. We release the probe, the outcome maps, the audit and every prediction.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
GUI agents act through clicks, so grounding an instruction to a screen location is the perception step every agent depends on. The strongest open grounders we evaluate score 93 to 96% on ScreenSpot-v2 [ 28 ] ( Sec. 4 ), and across 29 recent grounding papers the median absolute gap between a new model and the strongest prior model of its size is 1.15 points. At this level the useful question is no longer how many clicks miss the box. It is what the remaining misses are. When the remaining mistakes of top ImageNet models were reviewed one by one, nearly half turned out not to be mistakes [ 25 ] . Grounding benchmarks score a predicted point with one rule. It is correct when it falls inside the annotated box of the target element [ 7 , 28 , 17 , 30 , 9 ] , and reinforcement learning for grounding uses the same rule as its reward [ 33 , 24 ] . The rule assumes that each instruction has one correct region. Interfaces are built the other way. A commit list links every author name to the same profile, a form label focuses its control, and a shop shows a product’s name in the breadcrumb and in the title, both linking to the product ( Fig. 1 ). Public screenshots show the same pattern ( Fig. 3 ). None of this is an annotation mistake. The box is not wrong. The rule is incomplete, because a click outside the box can do exactly what the instruction asks. The converse also happens, and parts of an annotated box can be inert. We define correctness by what a click does. Given an interface state and an annotated target, a click is outcome-correct when executing it produces the same outcome as clicking the target. A probe executes a click in a browser, intercepts every request that would change server state, and records a fingerprint of the result. Running the probe densely over a page yields an outcome map , the set of pixels that achieve each element’s outcome. Running it on model predictions yields an execution judge . Neither needs annotation beyond the existing box. For public screenshot benchmarks, where nothing can be executed, we run a blind audit with the same definition. What the remaining errors are. On a self-hosted Gitea code forge and a Django Oscar shop, 17.7% and 31.2% of live targets have pixels outside the box that reach the same outcome or pixels inside it that do nothing. Executing the predictions of UI-Venus-1.5-8B, GTA1-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B shows that about one in five clicks rejected by box scoring is outcome-correct on Gitea, and about one in three on Oscar. Rewriting the instructions as natural tasks keeps these shares at 14.3% and 34.8%. On ScreenSpot-v2, a blind audit of 11 models, cross-checked by two independent reviewers, finds 6.4% of rejected clicks outcome-correct overall and 14.4% on web screenshots. Among the audited models the share is highest for the strongest grounders, so box scoring misreads more of the errors that still separate strong grounders than of the errors of weaker models. Bias, not noise. Another training seed moves ScreenSpot-v2 accuracy by up to 2.6 points, and test-set sampling limits paired comparisons of 7–8B models to about 1.4 points. Both are noise that more seeds and a paired interval control, yet none of the 29 papers reports run-to-run variation on ScreenSpot-v2. Box scoring adds a bias, a one-sided under-count of the correct clicks of every model. It is estimated at 0.2 to 1.8 points on ScreenSpot-v2, and no number of runs or test items removes it. That is the size of the gaps the field reports, and the part that differs between models changes the point-estimate order of models within a point of each other. Where the fix belongs. In our 3B study, a GRPO reward that credits the equivalent clicks found by the probe brings no stable gain over box rewards across three seeds and four test sets. Trained models already reach the target outcome on every held-out item whose target has an exact duplicate, so the correction is needed in evaluation, while its value as a training reward remains unshown at this scale. Our contributions are: • A definition of outcome-defined correctness for GUI grounding, and a probe that turns it into outcome maps and an execution judge for live web applications without new annotation. • An audit of what box scoring rejects on two live applications and two public benchmarks, with execution judging of strong grounders and a reviewed screenshot audit of 11 models. • An error budget for one-point comparisons on ScreenSpot-v2 that separates the scoring bias from seed and sampling noise, with reporting recommendations. • A controlled training study that places the fix in evaluation rather than in the reward. All maps, judgments, predictions and protocols are released.
