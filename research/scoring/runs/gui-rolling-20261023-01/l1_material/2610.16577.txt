title: CineRecon: Benchmarking Frontier Models for Agentic Cinematic Reconstruction
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16577

§Abstract
Reconstructing reference videos as editable dynamic 3D scenes is an important path toward controllable video generation for filmmaking: these scenes provide modifiable, renderable visual proxies that enable explicit control over scene layout, subject actions, and camera behavior. To evaluate agents' ability to reconstruct editable dynamic 3D scenes from dynamic reference videos, we introduce CineRecon, a benchmark comprising 50 reference videos and 360 annotated evaluation clips. The reference videos span film, real-world footage, and games. Given a complete video, an agent uses tools to construct, animate, render, and refine its reconstruction. We evaluate this task under three representation settings: Proxy, Simplified, and Detailed. These settings increase geometric and appearance requirements while retaining the same requirements for spatial relations, camera behavior, and subject dynamics. This design tests whether agents can reproduce the events in a video when detailed appearance is unnecessary, and how performance changes as representation requirements increase. For evaluation, we construct checklists that associate observable properties and events with specific entities and times. A vision-language judge compares timestamp-aligned reference and rendered frames across 11 categories, grouped into camera, static, and dynamic consistency dimensions. We evaluate 12 models using their corresponding agent harnesses and find failures across all three dimensions. Subject motions and interactions remain difficult to reproduce even under the Proxy setting.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Precisely expressing and refining creative intent is a central requirement for bringing video generation into filmmaking [ 39 , 17 ] . Reconstructing reference videos as editable dynamic 3D scenes provides an important path toward this goal. These scenes let creators explicitly adjust spatial relations, action timing, and camera trajectories by editing layout, subject actions, and camera behavior. Their rendered outputs serve as visual proxies to guide video generation [ 20 , 32 , 10 ] . Building such proxies requires faithfully reproducing the dynamic processes in the reference video. For example, a camera follows a person approaching another person, and the two then shake hands. The agent must coordinate the person’s movement, the handshake, and the camera motion so that the interaction occurs at the correct time and location. This raises a concrete question: can agents autonomously construct editable 3D scenes from complete videos to faithfully reenact their content and events? Recent work has expanded the evaluation of graphics agents from scene editing and static reconstruction [ 15 , 40 , 30 ] to video reconstruction. BVB studies real indoor videos [ 38 ] , while 4DCodeBench examines dynamic phenomena such as deformation, fluid flow, and fracture [ 36 ] . Cinematic reconstruction requires jointly reproducing scene content, subject actions and interactions, camera motion, and shot transitions. We evaluate this joint capability using complete reference videos, without supplied scene assets or camera parameters. We introduce CineRecon , a benchmark of 50 reference videos from film, real-world footage, and games. An agent receives the complete video and generic task instructions, then autonomously constructs an editable Blender scene and renders its animation. The agent can inspect rendered feedback and revise the scene. Success requires faithfully reproducing the reference video’s content and events at corresponding timestamps. We evaluate reconstruction along three dimensions. Camera Consistency covers camera behavior and shot transitions. Static Consistency covers object presence, attributes, and spatial relations. Dynamic Consistency covers subject motion and action events, including interactions, deformation, and state changes, with attention to their timing. For each reference video, we construct checklists that associate observable properties and events with specific entities and times. A vision-language judge compares timestamp-aligned reference and rendered frames to assess these requirements. To examine how geometric and appearance requirements affect reconstruction performance, we define three settings for the same reference videos: Proxy , Simplified , and Detailed . They require geometric stand-ins, recognizable object structures, and reference-specific visible details, respectively. Geometric and appearance-detail requirements within Static Consistency vary across settings, while Camera and Dynamic Consistency use the same check items and spatial-relation requirements remain unchanged. This design allows us to compare reconstruction performance across representation settings and examine whether errors in reproducing dynamic events persist when appearance requirements are simplified. We systematically evaluate existing multimodal coding models. Figures 2 and 3 summarize reconstruction performance and generation cost. The results identify spatial relations, specific action events, and camera changes as shared weaknesses across models. Cross-setting comparisons show that most models achieve higher camera and dynamic consistency scores when appearance requirements are simplified, but still struggle to reproduce action timing, contact relations, and coordinated motion between subjects accurately. In summary, our contributions are threefold: (1) We introduce CineRecon, a benchmark comprising 50 reference videos and 360 annotated evaluation clips for evaluating agents that reconstruct complete dynamic videos as editable Blender scenes. (2) We define three representation settings and evaluate camera, static, and dynamic consistency using evaluation clips and checklists that have undergone multiple rounds of human review and refinement. (3) We analyze how 12 multimodal coding models perform with their corresponding agent harnesses and identify shared deficits in spatial relations, specific action events, and camera changes. Cross-setting comparisons further show that simplifying appearance requirements improves performance for most models, while substantial errors in action timing, contact relations, and coordinated subject motion persist under Proxy.
