title: Back to Basics: A Skill-Centric Approach to Generalist Web Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35823

§Abstract
Browser agents do not just need more practice. They need to practice the right things. We introduce MiniWeb, a realistic miniature of the web comprising 65 interconnected websites across 15 domains, where agents navigate, search, and transact as people do. Its 369 human-annotated tasks cover everyday workflows and label 37 reusable interaction primitives grouped into 7 skill families, allowing us to diagnose which skills fail rather than only whether a task succeeds. Benchmarking ten open-weight agents, we find that drag & gesture is consistently the weakest family, with no agent solving more than 24% of its instances. To address this gap, we introduce MiniWebGen, a framework that autonomously generates verified, skill-centric training data from MiniWeb. Fine-tuning Qwen3.5-4B on just 3.1K verified trajectories improves single-primitive success by 24.9pp across 13 unseen websites, with gains across every skill family. However, these trajectories teach short-horizon interactions, which do not directly translate to full-task success. We build on this result with MiniWebAgent, which pairs the trained executor with a planner that decomposes full tasks into the skill-level steps it learned to perform. This approach improves task success by 19.4pp points on unseen MiniWeb sites and transfers to external benchmarks, with gains of 9.3pp on WebArena-Lite, 16.1pp on Online-Mind2Web, and 20.0pp on WebVoyager, without training on their trajectories. We provide a simple recipe for compact browser agents: practice reusable skills directly, then compose them to solve unfamiliar tasks.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
What makes humans generalists? We rarely learn a complex behavior from scratch. Before learning to ride a bike, we have already spent years learning to balance, coordinate our limbs, recover from mistakes, and navigate through space. New tasks become easier because they reuse skills we have practiced in many other settings. Generalization comes not only from seeing more tasks, but from building a repertoire of reusable skills and learning how to compose them. Web agents should be able to learn in the same way. Current approaches to training general web agents focus on collecting more task trajectories on diverse websites, difficulty, and solution strategies [ 4 , 5 , 31 , 12 ] . While large models can benefit from this scale, compact open-weight agents have limited capacity and training budgets, making the choice of what to practice especially important [ 3 ] . We therefore ask: should a small web agent spend its training budget on more complete tasks, or on the reusable interaction skills those tasks are built from? We study the latter, training on short-horizon, skill-centric trajectories and composing the learned skills at inference time. We introduce MiniWeb , a collection of 65 websites (Fig. 2 ) built around 37 reusable interaction primitives. Rather than starting from tasks and labeling their skills afterward, we build the environment from the primitives upward (Fig. 3 ) and compose them into 369 human-authored tasks with skill-level verification. This gives us a controlled place to both measure what agents know and practice what they do not. Across ten open-weight agents, we found that drag & gesture is the weakest family for every model (Fig. 1 ), with only 12.4% average success compared with 38.0% for the next-weakest family. Web- and computer-use-specialized agents perform even worse on dragging than general VLMs despite being stronger on common interactions such as typing. We believe that scaling end-to-end task trajectories can systematically favor frequently encountered interactions while leaving rarer but reusable skills underpracticed. To test whether skill-specific practice closes these gaps, we introduce MiniWebGen , a multi-agent pipeline that generates verified trajectories for skills in MiniWeb . Training Qwen3.5-4B on 3.1K generated trajectories improves success on held-out single-primitive tasks by 24.9 points, with gains across all skill families. These gains do not automatically transfer to full tasks, so we introduce MiniWebAgent , which pairs the trained executor with a planner that decomposes tasks into practiced interactions. This approach improves success on held-out MiniWeb sites by 19.4 points and transfers to three external benchmarks without target-benchmark training: +9.3 points on WebArena-Lite, +20.0 on WebVoyager, and +16.1 on Online-Mind2Web. Overall, we contribute the following: ❖ MiniWeb , a benchmark of 65 websites and 369 tasks built from 37 reusable interaction primitives with skill-level verification, revealing that drag & gesture is the weakest skill family for all ten open-weight agents we evaluate. ❖ MiniWebGen , a multi-agent pipeline for generating verified, skill-specific trajectories, which at matched training size outperforms task-centric trajectories from InSTA [ 27 ] on all 4 benchmarks. ❖ MiniWebAgent , a compact browser agent that composes practiced skills via a planner and transfers to WebArena-Lite, WebVoyager, and Online-Mind2Web without target-benchmark training.
