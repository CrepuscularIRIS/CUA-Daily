title: Agentic Robot Creation through Generation, Verification, and Revision
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17881

§Abstract
Can an AI agent turn high-level user intent into a functioning robot and teach it how to behave? We study this question from two complementary aspects: embodiment and behavior creation. For embodiment creation, we investigate how well current multimodal models capture mechanical requirements, and introduce computational metrics for evaluating generated designs before fabrication. For behavior creation, we present Roboforge, an agentic framework that turns task specifications into executable policies through motion generation, policy learning, and execution-grounded revision. Given a robot embodiment and task, the agent generates a reference motion, configures the learning objective, and trains a policy. Crucially, motion remains in the loop: rollout feedback can revise the reference motion, reward, or both. We evaluate a generated embodiment across multiple MLLMs and behavior creation across four diverse robots against reward-refinement and one-shot motion baselines. Our computational metrics expose recurring mechanical failures in generated designs, while behavior experiments show that execution-grounded refinement improves task-level behavior and execution quality. Finally, we fabricate an agent-generated Gorilla and directly deploy learned behaviors on the physical robot.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
“Design is not just what it looks like and feels like; design is how it works.” — Steve Jobs Small expressive robots such as Microduck and recent character robots ( Grandia et al., 2025 ; Müller et al., 2026 ) suggest a future in which people can personalize not only how a robot looks, but also how it moves and behaves. Yet creating such a robot today still demands substantial expertise. A high-level concept must be translated into a mechanical structure, suitable joints and actuators, a valid simulation model, desired motions, and ultimately a control policy capable of executing them. These stages typically require mechanical designers, animators, and control engineers to iterate together. With increasingly capable multimodal foundation models, we ask: can robot creation become an agentic process, where users describe what they want and agents carry out much of the engineering required to make it physically realizable? Recent work has begun to automate individual stages of this process. Foundation models have been used to generate robot morphologies and articulated designs ( Ringel et al., 2025 ; Sontakke et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . In parallel, LLMs can synthesize and iteratively refine rewards for reinforcement learning ( Ma et al., 2024b ; Lee et al., 2026 ) , while MimicAgent generates reference motions for downstream policy learning ( Nayak et al., 2026 ) . Together, these advances suggest that much of the robot-creation pipeline may become amenable to agentic automation. Connecting these pieces, however, reveals a fundamental gap: a robot that looks plausible is not necessarily buildable, and a motion that looks plausible is not necessarily executable . We study this gap from two aspects of robot creation. For embodiment creation , rather than introducing another morphology generator, we ask how well current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) understand the mechanical requirements that matter before fabrication. A visually convincing CAD model may still contain disconnected structures, insufficient actuator clearance, self-collisions, or unsuitable contact geometry. We therefore identify a set of mechanically relevant requirements and introduce multi-faceted computational metrics to quantify them before fabrication. Using these metrics, we systematically compare different MLLMs, characterize the mechanical knowledge they already possess, and expose recurring failure modes in their generated designs. Importantly, we have fabricated an agent-generated robot Gorilla and demonstrate that designs passing these checks can be assembled and operated as physical systems. For behavior creation , physical feasibility is harder to assess from generation alone. A motion may appear natural but remain incompatible with a particular embodiment because of its timing, pose, contact sequence or joint excursion. Such incompatibilities often become apparent only when a policy attempts to execute the motion. This observation motivates RoboForge , an agentic framework for turning desired behaviors into executable robot policies through generation, physical execution, and revision . Given a robot embodiment and a task description, the agent generates a reference motion and a learning objective, trains a policy, and inspects the resulting rollouts. Execution therefore serves as a physical verification signal rather than merely the endpoint of learning. When the policy fails, the agent reasons about whether the failure stems from what behavior is being requested —such as motion timing, target pose, contact sequence, or trajectory—or from how the behavior is being learned —such as reward terms and their relative weights. It then revises the corresponding component and retrains the policy. This closes a motion–policy feedback loop in which learning outcomes expose deficiencies in generated motions, while generated motions continue to provide structured guidance for learning. We evaluate behavior creation across four diverse embodiments, including existing platforms such as Unitree Go2 and Microduck as well as agent-generated robots, and compare RoboForge with representative reward-refinement and one-shot motion-generation approaches. RoboForge leads to policies that are better aligned with the intended task, as evidenced by both user preference studies and complementary quantitative metrics. Besides, we analyze the intermediate failures and revisions throughout the process to understand how execution feedback drives improvement. We further deploy the resulting policies on the physical robot we fabricate, demonstrating behaviors that transfer from high-level user intent to real-world execution.
