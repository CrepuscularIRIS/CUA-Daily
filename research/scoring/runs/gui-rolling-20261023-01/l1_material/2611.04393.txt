title: Voluntary Servitude: Weaponized Safety Warning Mechanisms for Controlling AI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04393

§Abstract
LLM agents increasingly act on information retrieved from public sources they treat as credible, which turns a manipulated recommendation into a transaction, a dependency, or a decision with physical consequences. The security literature models this threat as instruction injection, in which an adversary writes a command into retrieved content and deployed defenses learn to recognize command syntax. We show that an attacker can instead steer an agent through the agent's own harm-avoidance. A single fabricated hazard, written as an ordinary review, leads the agent to disqualify the legitimate options and select the attacker's target by its own reasoning, with no command for a detector to recognize. We call this voluntary servitude. These payloads steer agents about as often as instruction injection (60.4% vs. 66.0%), and deployed injection detectors score them as benign, so behind a filter they are 1.7-4.4x more effective. We measure this at the generation stage across six models and ten consumer-service domains, for three attack families, and show that it transfers to production browser agents, outperforming instruction injection, and to a retrieval pipeline (1.0-57.0% end-to-end success, depending on the retriever). We then show that a corroboration requirement added to the agent's instructions removes roughly 95% of the exposure against a non-adaptive attacker without retraining. With the requirement in place, the agent still reads the planted hazard and no longer acts on it.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Modern LLM agents act on a user’s behalf, browsing pages, calling tools and completing tasks ( Debenedetti et al., 2024 ) . To act, they read third-party content from the open web, and deep-research systems ground their output in the same user-generated pages people read ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) . A recommendation from such an agent often becomes an action, such as a booking, a dependency added to a build or a visit to a clinic ( Luo and Chen, 2026 ; Spracklen et al., 2025 ) . When the evidence behind that choice is manipulated, the user can suffer financial loss or physical harm ( Greshake et al., 2023 ) . Anyone can post on the pages agents read, and adversarial text on them already reaches agents at scale. A crawl of 1.2 billion URLs finds validated injections on 11.7 thousand pages, about 70% of them hidden in page markup that only a machine reads ( Khodayari et al., 2026 ) . Current defenses are instruction-centric. They model indirect prompt injection as a command written into retrieved content that the agent obeys as though it came from its principal ( Greshake et al., 2023 ; Zou et al., 2025 ) . Deployed detectors screen retrieved text for that command syntax ( ProtectAI.com, 2024 ; Lakera AI, 2024 ) , and injection benchmarks are assembled almost entirely from payloads that carry a command ( Yi et al., 2025 ; Debenedetti et al., 2024 ) . Online review platforms faced a similar problem before agents existed. Fake reviews are written to read like real ones, and classifying the text alone proved insufficient. Detection therefore moved to signals outside the text, such as coordination between accounts, the timing of reviews and the graph of who reviews what ( Cao et al., 2021 ) . Judging a claim by whether independent sources support it becomes more important as generated text grows harder to tell from human writing ( Gambetti and Han, 2023 ) . Agent defenses have not adopted such signals. A prompt-injection detector still judges each retrieved item on its own text, and detectors miss payloads outside the command patterns they were trained on with near-certainty ( Biswas, 2026 ) . In this work we show that an attacker can steer an agent through the agent’s own harm-avoidance. A single fabricated hazard posted as an ordinary review, such as a lapsed certification or an unpatched software vulnerability, leads the agent to disqualify the legitimate providers and select the attacker’s target by its own reasoning, although nothing in the review addresses the model. We call this voluntary servitude , since the agent serves the attacker by following its own safety judgment (Figure 1 ). We show that (1) these payloads steer six models across ten consumer-service domains, (2) the safety framing drives the agent’s choice, (3) the attack transfers to production browser agents and to a retrieval pipeline, at a rate that depends on the retriever, and (4) a corroboration requirement added to the agent’s instructions removes most of the exposure. With no filter in place, these payloads reach about the same attack success rate (ASR) as instruction injection, and behind two deployed detectors their ASR is 1.7 to 4.4 times that of instruction injection. In the following, we outline our main contributions. • We identify voluntary servitude, a lightweight attack in which a handful of ordinary reviews on a public page steer the agent’s selection, with no access to the model, its prompt or its retrieval pipeline. • We show that the effect comes from the safety framing itself. The hazard moves even the model most resistant to other persuasion, while an accusation of misconduct placed the same way does no better than a benign review. • We measure effective success, the attack’s success after a deployed injection detector, across three attack families, six models and ten domains, and show that the attack reaches four production browser agents on a live web page and holds up through a retrieval pipeline. • We propose a corroboration requirement for the agent’s instructions, which asks for independent support before the agent discards a provider on safety grounds. Against a non-adaptive attacker, it lowers attack success from 68.4% to 3.6%. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 states the threat model and how the attack is carried out. Section 4 gives the experimental setup and the metrics, and Section 5 answers our research questions. Section 6 presents the corroboration defense, and Section 7 summarizes the findings and discusses limitations and broader impact.
