arxiv: 2611.22722 (2611.22722v1)
title: Practice in Text, Act in Pixels: Correcting Where Perception Fails
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22722

§Abstract
GUI agents can learn cheaply from a text view of an interface and act on screenshots through a vision-language screen reader. Yet on a simulated mobile platform with a low-cost reader, policies that complete most tasks in text fail on many of the same tasks on screenshots. We ask where a small budget of real screenshots is best spent to close this gap. We find that the reader's misreads are systematic and recur on the same interface elements. Noise matched to the reader's error rates, a natural sim-to-real remedy, misses this structure, and in a pre-specified comparison it did not beat training the policy directly on screenshots. We therefore correct the misreads at their source with reader alignment, which fine-tunes the reader on screenshots paired with their text views. The reader learns to output each screen's text view, as in text practice, and the policy stays unchanged. Spent on the reader, the screenshot budget gives 80.9% full-task success on new task instances, against 55.6% when spent on training the policy. Given the same labeled screenshots, adapting the reader also beats adapting the policy, and a single aligned reader improves every text-trained policy we tried without retraining any of them.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Agents that operate graphical interfaces can learn from screenshots or from a text view of the interface, a list of its visible elements. Verifiable simulated GUI platforms that run many episodes in parallel [ 24 ] and LLM-based simulation of UI states [ 23 ] make large-scale practice cheap, and far cheaper in text than in pixels. On MobileGym, the simulated mobile platform we use, the text view is generated from each rendered screen, and an episode costs about 7.2 \times more GPU time on screenshots than in text (supplementary Sec. I ). At test time, however, an agent often has only the screen, because structured text such as HTML can be lengthy or unavailable [ 4 ] . A policy that learned in text then needs a vision-language screen reader that turns each screenshot into the same kind of element list. We call this recipe practice in text, act in pixels : the policy does all its learning in the text view, from imitating scripted demonstrations to retraining on its own successful episodes, and only the reader sees pixels ( Fig. 1 , left). Yet with a low-cost reader, Qwen3-VL-4B on half-size screenshots, a policy trained in text completes 87.5% of new instances of its practiced MobileGym tasks in text but only 43.0% on screenshots of the same instances. This text-to-pixels gap is at least 29.5 points for each of five training methods, including the three that already spend screenshots on practice or on the policy. The question is where a fixed budget of real screenshots is best spent to close this gap: on the practice environment, on the policy or on the reader (uses (a)–(c) in Fig. 1 ). The reader’s output differs from the text view in two ways ( Fig. 1 , middle): the reader misreads some of the content, leaving elements out or getting their text or state wrong, and it lists the elements in its own order instead of the top-to-bottom layout order of the text view. A controlled decomposition indicates that each difference lowers success and that together they cost less than the sum of their separate costs ( Sec. 4.2 ). The key observation is that the misreads are systematic ( Fig. 2 ). An element that the reader misread before is misread again in other episodes far more often than independent errors at the same rate would predict (73.2% vs. 30.9%; Sec. 4.3 ); the same holds with sampled decoding and for a larger reader of the same family. A natural way to spend the budget, in the spirit of domain randomization [ 22 ] , is to measure the reader’s errors and inject such errors into the text view, so that practice looks like the reader. Noise matched to the reader’s measured error rates ( rate-matched noise ) draws its errors independently in each episode, so the errors do not stay on the elements that the reader misreads ( Fig. 2 ). Practice environments built this way approximately match the reader’s overall effect on success, but they predict which tasks fail on screenshots less accurately than a second run on screenshots does (correlation across tasks of at most 0.70, against 0.90). In a pre-specified comparison with equal screenshot budgets, practice with rate-matched noise showed no measurable difference from training the policy directly on screenshots. Instead of making practice look like the reader, we make the reader look like practice. Reader alignment fine-tunes the screen reader on about 2,000 screenshots paired with their text views. The reader learns to output each screen’s text view, with the content and order the policy practiced on, and the policy stays unchanged. Each screenshot thus teaches the reader a full element list, whereas direct training on screenshots learns only from episodes that succeed. Because the misreads recur on largely the same elements, we expect a small budget of labeled screenshots to correct them at their source. Reader alignment is supervised fine-tuning, the GUI counterpart of mapping real inputs to a canonical simulated view in robotic grasping [ 9 ] ; we add evidence on where the screenshots should go. With the same budget of about 2,000 screenshots, aligning the reader gives 80.9% full-task success on new instances of the practiced tasks, against 55.6% for training the policy directly on screenshots ( Fig. 1 , right), and the comparison replicates on two further instance sets. Given the same labeled screenshots, adapting the reader also beats adapting the policy. The aligned 4B reader clearly exceeds an unaligned 8B reader, and it raises all three text-trained policies we tried by at least 24.0 points without retraining any of them. Because MobileGym renders repeated screens identically, a lookup baseline that returns the stored text view for exact repeats of alignment screenshots, and the sorted reader output otherwise, recovers 70% of alignment’s gain over the original reader.
