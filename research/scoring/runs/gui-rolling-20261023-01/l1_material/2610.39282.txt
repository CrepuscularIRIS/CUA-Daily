arxiv: 2610.39282 (2610.39282v1)
title: What Does "Now" Mean to Your Computer-Use Agent? Stale Screenshots, Page Loads, and Which Check to Run Before a Click
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39282

§Abstract
Computer-use agents plan on a screenshot for several seconds and then click at a position on the screen; if the page has changed in the meantime, the click lands on an unintended element. Prior work treats the planning interval as the source of this risk. Measuring planned clicks on 31 public web pages, we find that the risk lies in page loading: 0.4% of clicks planned on fully loaded pages became stale within 30 seconds, against 17% of clicks planned in the first second after a page opened. On these pages, waiting about one second for network activity to stop removes most of this risk, yet the wait rules of eight of ten widely used open-source agents leave more than 1% of clicks stale. Commercial pages keep loading for longer: in the HTTP Archive's public crawl, 15% of 65 desktop booking and buying home pages went 500 ms without network requests within 3 seconds of their HTML being parsed, against 63% of 3,028 .gov pages, and 52% shifted their layout within 6.5 seconds of a network-idle wait capped at 3 seconds, against 8% (61% against 26% among pages recorded in full). Where a check before the click is needed, as before irreversible actions and on pages that are still loading, a pixel comparison verifies the target's position as accurately as a frontier vision-language model at one eighth of the latency, and a 4B model matches the frontier model in verifying whether the previous action succeeded. We release a benchmark generator, a break-even formula for when a check pays, and three rules for agent developers: https://github.com/multimodal-ai-engineering/preflight-bench

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Consider an agent searching for a flight. It opens an airline’s home page, waits until network activity stops or three seconds pass, captures a screenshot, and spends 6.5 seconds deciding where to click. Figure 1 shows what can happen on a real airline home page. The screenshot shows a finished search form, and the agent picks the search button. Before the click is sent, the page inserts a credit-card offer above the form and pushes the form down 52 pixels, so the click lands on a checkbox that switches the search to loyalty points. The agent’s decision was correct for the screenshot it planned on; the page it acted on had changed. Most computer-use agents follow this pattern: capture a screenshot, plan with a large multimodal model, and click at screen coordinates [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Planning takes 1–13 s [ 1 , 2 ] . Browser-testing frameworks guard against this failure by verifying, immediately before each click, that the target element is present, stable and able to receive the click [ 4 ] . Agents that click at coordinates omit this verification. How often the omission causes errors on real pages, and which form of perception should perform the verification, had not been measured. The prevailing assumption and our research questions. Existing work treats the planning delay as the source of risk. PUSV measured a 6.5-second interval between screenshot and click and protects it with pixel re-verification [ 1 ] ; AOI and pre-compiled policy trees aim to shorten the interval [ 2 , 5 ] . Under this assumption, the natural remedy is a fast visual check immediately before each click, which we call a preflight . We tested the assumption and the remedy through three research questions. Throughout, a planned click is stale when, at the moment of the click, its target has moved, disappeared or been covered. RQ1. When does the screenshot become stale? During the planning interval, or at another point? RQ2. Which form of perception should verify the screen? Pixel comparison, a small vision–language model (VLM), or a frontier model? RQ3. When is a check worth its cost? Each check adds latency to every step and occasionally raises false alarms. Findings. RQ1: the prevailing assumption does not hold, and planning time has little effect. On fully loaded pages, 0.4% of planned clicks became stale within 30 s. The risk is concentrated in the first second after a page opens, when 17% of planned clicks become stale because the page is still loading. On these pages, waiting until network activity stops removes most of this risk, and most widely used agents do not wait long enough. Commercial pages keep loading for longer: in a public web crawl, 85% of desktop booking and buying home pages had not reached network idle within 3 s of DOM-ready, and half shifted their layout after the wait. RQ2: where a check remains necessary (pages that are still loading, live dashboards, maps, shared documents and irreversible actions), the form of perception should match the question. Whether the target is still in place is a question about a few hundred pixels at one location; direct pixel comparison answers it as accurately as the best model tested, with fewer false alarms and a fraction of the latency. Whether the previous action succeeded is a question about what the screen now reports, and a 4B model answers it as accurately as the frontier model. RQ3: a break-even formula based only on the planner’s latency and the check’s latency and accuracy predicted when a check pays, agreeing with held-out data on 95% of decisions. Implications. The findings yield three low-cost rules (Section 6 ): wait up to about 3 s for network activity to stop before capturing the screenshot; before an irreversible click, verify the target with a pixel check (0.3 s, no model), which matters more on commercial pages, since they are often still loading; and when the check fails, re-plan from a new screenshot instead of adjusting the original click. Figure 4 in Section 6 contrasts one agent step under current practice and under these rules. Contributions. (1) The first measurement of how often an agent’s planned click becomes stale on real web pages, locating the risk in page loading, together with an evaluation of the post-navigation wait rules of ten open-source agents and a crawl-based measurement of how long commercial pages keep loading. (2) A direct comparison of pixel methods, OCR, small VLMs and frontier models on the three questions a pre-click check must answer. (3) A break-even formula for when a check pays, validated on held-out data. (4) An open benchmark generator that renders moving web pages with exact timing and labels every example from the page’s own state. All experiments were preregistered, and we report every outcome, including refuted hypotheses.
