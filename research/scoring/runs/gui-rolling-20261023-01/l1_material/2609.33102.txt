title: ORBIT: A Framework for Multi-Agent Safety and Security Evaluations
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33102

§Abstract
Multi-agent LLM systems are increasingly deployed for complex, long-horizon tasks or emerge as a natural consequence of agents interacting in the wild. Yet they give rise to significant safety and security risks: the flexible protocols that enable task generalization also expose novel threats, from cascading prompt injection to inter-agent collusion. Progress in defending against these threats has been slowed by a lack of shared empirical infrastructure, which forces bespoke environment development for every new defense and makes standardized comparison impossible. Existing evaluations address isolated threat models or single-agent settings, but none jointly vary attack, defense, and architecture across realistic multi-agent environments. To address this gap, we introduce ORBIT, a configurable evaluation framework for empirical multi-agent safety and security research, built on UK AISI's Inspect. ORBIT lets researchers configure communication topologies, memory, scheduling, and agent roles. It supports four threat types and four defense strategies, as well as non-adversarial failures, with a benchmark suite spanning five scenario families covering browser use, computer use, agentic coding, customer service, and cooperative allocation. Our central finding is a gap in defense transferability across threats: per-action defenses that cut a compromised agent's attack success by 60 points on multi-issue coding give no measurable protection against colluding agents, and none of the defenses we tested generalized over all attacks tested. We further demonstrate security-performance tradeoffs and interactions between architecture and defense effectiveness. We make ORBIT available open-source at https://github.com/wlanderson0/orbit.

Frontier LLM agents have become increasingly capable, autonomous, and long-horizon ( Motwani et al., 2026 ) , now able to carry out hours-long software engineering tasks ( METR, 2026 ) , conduct significant research tasks ( Schmidgall et al., 2025 ; Wen et al., 2026 ; Epoch AI, 2026 ; Anthropic, 2026b ) , and complete complex computer-use work ( Anthropic, 2026a ; OpenClaw, 2026 ) . These systems are increasingly deployed as multi-agent systems (MAS), networks of interacting agents ( Li et al., 2024 ; Xiao et al., 2024 ; Gottweis et al., 2025 ) . This shift is driven both by intentional system design and by the emergent composition of independently deployed models into larger systems ( Anthropic, 2025 ; Lin, 2026 ) . As LLM agents proliferate throughout the economy, we will increasingly see decentralized multi-agent systems, with many agents interacting through flexible protocols across the internet ( Moltbook, 2026 ; Tomasev et al., 2025 ) . And yet, progress in securing these systems lags the pace of development. Several new threat models have emerged, including memory poisoning and tool-use failures, infection-style attacks, and viral misalignment through subliminal prompting ( Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026 ; Lee and Tiwari, 2024 ; Motwani et al., 2024 ; Weckbecker et al., 2026 ) . Defenses that are sufficient in single agent systems do not necessarily extend to multi-agent settings. Vulnerabilities in MAS are amplified through task decomposition, partial observability, responsibility distribution, while security-relevant properties may not be compositional ( Hagag et al., 2026 ; Agrawal et al., 2026 ) . New attack surfaces in multi-agent systems. Many threats are unique to or amplified by multi-agent settings ( Hammond et al., 2025 ) , opening the field of multi-agent security ( Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026 ) . Task decomposition transforms a harmful goal into benign-looking subtasks across agents, allowing a harmful goal to bypass safety filters ( Jones et al., 2024 ) . Insider compromise sees a malicious or compromised agent use its trusted position to exploit its peer agents and cause damage through communication channels ( Yu et al., 2026 ) . Further, collusion threats see agents covertly coordinate at the expense of system-level objectives ( Motwani et al., 2024 ) . The infrastructure gap. Despite increasing evidence of these vulnerabilities and early work to characterize them ( Krawiecka and de Witt, 2025 ) , defenses have lagged. Limited studies have explored defenses using memory vaccination ( Peigné et al., 2025 ) and carefully designed isolation ( Debenedetti et al., 2025 ) , but progress has been slowed by a lack of empirical evaluations for multi-agent security ( Sharma et al., 2025 ) . Most defense studies evaluate a single mechanism on a single attack type on a custom-built environment, making results difficult to compare. Existing multi-agent frameworks lack support for configurable topologies, threat-model parametrization, or integration with defenses, leaving developers without principled guidance on safeguarding their systems. This work directly addresses that gap by introducing Orbit , a configurable evaluation framework for empirical multi-agent safety and security research built on UK AISI’s Inspect . Orbit (Figure 1 ) provides configurable setups, flexible orchestration to run arbitrary MAS, new multi-agent benchmarks, and robust evaluation and logging infrastructure. Our contributions are: 1. Configurable MAS evaluation framework : We introduce the Orbit framework, which decomposes experiments into six configurable layers, setup, scenario, attack, defense, execution, and evaluation, enabling controlled ablation. New experiments can be configured with a YAML file, while adding new attacks, defenses, and scenarios only requires implementing a short protocol. Orbit provides scoring to calculate system-level and per-agent metrics, (see Section 3.4 ) and an LLM-judge implementation ( MASJudgeScorer ). 2. Multi-agent safety and security benchmark suite : We construct a benchmark suite spanning five scenario families (browser, coding, desktop, customer service, cooperative allocation) by adapting established single-agent benchmarks into multi-agent configurations. Adaptations include functional decomposition into specialist roles, combining samples to create multi-goal environments for many agents, and integration of natively multi-agent scenarios. We support four attack types, non-adversarial failure settings (agent misbehavior, coordination failure), and four defense mechanisms, providing a large configuration space for controlled experiments. Full construction details are in Section 4 and Appendix F . 3.
