title: Anchor: Preventing Artifact Drift for Reliable Agent Verification
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26321

§Abstract
Reliable evaluation of long-horizon agents requires verifiers that match what the task actually asks. Verifiable rewards have driven progress in domains like math and coding, yet building similarly reliable environments for open-ended enterprise workflows repeatedly struggles to balance realism, verifiability, and scale. This creates a validity problem we call artifact drift: when instructions, environments, oracles, and verifiers are created separately, they frequently fail to agree on what a task requires, resulting in environments that are unsolvable, reward-hackable, or inconsistent. We introduce Anchor, a benchmark-construction pipeline that formalizes domain expert specifications of business workflows into constrained optimization programs. From a single parametric specification, the pipeline jointly produces a natural-language instruction, environment configuration, solver-certified ground-truth solution, and state-based verifier. With this pipeline, altering parameters yields new tasks with controlled difficulty and known optimal solutions, producing harness-agnostic environments whose rewards depend solely on end-state business correctness. We apply Anchor to produce ERP-Bench: a benchmark of 300 long-horizon tasks spanning procurement and manufacturing workflows in a production-grade ERP system. Our pipeline organizes tasks into diverse families with calibrated difficulty profiles, and allows generated environments to evaluate coding, browser, and computer-use agents using a shared verifier. Across these harnesses, we find that frontier models follow all rules and instructions in 25.9% of trials and reach fully optimal decisions in only 17.4% of trials. Overall, Anchor and ERP-Bench offer a concrete recipe for building auditable evaluation environments whose verifiers remain aligned with the economically valuable tasks they are intended to score.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Reliable evaluation of long-horizon agents depends on verifiers that correctly capture what a task requires. This is especially difficult for economically valuable enterprise workflows, where agents must operate over persistent business state, follow procedural rules, and make quantitatively correct decisions [ 17 , 7 ] . Yet even as agents improve rapidly on "real-world" benchmarks, practical deployments continue to underperform benchmark expectations [ 16 , 10 , 27 ] . We argue that part of this gap comes from how agent benchmarks are constructed: the task presented to the agent and the verifier used to score it are often produced through separate processes. A benchmark task for interactive agents consists of four artifacts: a natural-language instruction describing the task, an environment configuration specifying the initial state of the software the agent operates in, an oracle solution representing a successful completion, and a verifier that inspects the post-execution state to determine success. These artifacts must agree on what constitutes successful completion. In practice, they often do not. Most benchmarks either simplify the underlying workflow or rely on post-hoc audits to catch inconsistencies between task artifacts [ 5 , 27 ] . The consequences can be severe: do-nothing agents pass 38% of \tau -bench airline tasks because the verifier accepts empty responses [ 31 ] . A benchmark can therefore appear realistic while still rewarding states that do not satisfy the task, rejecting valid solutions, or exposing opportunities for reward hacking. We call this failure mode artifact drift : the instruction, environment, oracle, and verifier describe subtly different versions of the same task. Drift is not just a verifier bug. It is a construction problem. When the four artifacts are authored independently, consistency is checked only after the fact, usually through audit and hand-written tests. These checks can catch individual mistakes, but they do not prevent systematic disagreement between what the task asks for and what the verifier rewards. At the same time, making verification easy can push benchmark designers toward overly constrained tasks with a single expected solution path [ 31 , 9 ] , trading realism for tractability. We address this with Anchor , a benchmark-construction pipeline that derives the task and its verifier from the same solved specification. A domain expert, working with an engineer, formalizes a business workflow as a parametric constraint program in OR-Tools CP-SAT [ 18 ] with decision variables, business-rule constraints, and an objective metric. For a proposed parameter setting, the solver either rejects the sample as infeasible or certifies an optimal solution. The instruction, environment configuration, oracle solution, and verifier are then generated deterministically from that solved specification. Because the verifier and the task are projections of the same source, Anchor mitigates task-verifier misalignment by construction rather than relying on post-hoc repair. The same parameters also control task difficulty, allowing many task instances to be generated while preserving a consistent definition of success. We instantiate Anchor as ERP-Bench : 300 tasks across procurement and manufacturing workflows in Odoo, a widely deployed open-source ERP system. Tasks span calibrated difficulty profiles and diverse workflow families within each domain. We evaluate frontier models on these tasks under three widely deployed agent paradigms: coding, browser, and computer-use. Our evaluations surface two empirical findings central to the contribution: frontier models consistently break explicit constraints rather than merely make worse tradeoffs at higher task difficulties; and UI-based agents are several times more expensive on the same task instances. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce Anchor , which generates the instruction, environment, oracle, and verifier from one constrained-optimization specification. • We show that single-source construction reduces cross-artifact inconsistency while preserving the need for independent specification validation. • We release ERP-Bench , 300 long-horizon Odoo tasks spanning 29 procurement and manufacturing workflow patterns. • We evaluate frontier models across coding, browser, and computer-use harnesses, finding 25.9% constraint satisfaction but only 17.4% fully optimal solutions.
