title: Sivia: Learning to Draw, Learning to Learn
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33386

§Abstract
A paper's overview figure is often the first thing readers see: it must explain the method correctly and look polished. We present Sivia, an overview-figure system that reads the paper, plans its relations, adapts visual organization from recent top-conference papers, and composes a design for image generation. Sivia improves with use through human-guided recursive self-improvement (RSI) at two levels: learning to draw revises its drawing skill from feedback on accuracy and presentation, and learning to learn revises the updater that proposes those revisions. A personal profile adapts presentation to its user's aesthetic preferences while the scientific content stays fixed. A diagnostic study of 1,964 overviews for 445 papers (17,973 relation judgments) shows that most errors are omissions and that source-derived relation plans raise required-relation accuracy (RRA) by up to 19.85 points. On 48 test papers, learning both levels reaches 93.54\% RRA, 15.31, 9.38, and 8.33 points above the initial skill, the Fixed-updater, and the GEPA baselines, while readability and visual quality rise from 3.95 and 4.00 to 4.70 and 4.78 on five-point scales. Learned presentation profiles raise liking from 4.02 to 4.73 and adoption from 34 to 45 of 48 tasks. Code and released artifacts are available at https://github.com/exsinger-hub/Sivia

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The overview figure is the visual core of a paper. It is often the first thing reviewers and readers examine, and it shapes how they understand the method: which component consumes which artifact, in which stage, and under which condition. A good overview must therefore be both accurate and beautiful, with the clarity and polish expected at top venues. Image generators now render attractive diagrams from text ( Zala et al., 2024 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ) , yet an attractive figure can still route a verifier to the wrong input or drop a perception step, and an accurate figure can still be too dense or plain to communicate. To locate such failures, we assess 1,964 first-draft overviews of 445 papers against 4,033 source-anchored relation requirements, totaling 17,973 human judgments by three raters (Section 3 ). Omitted relations account for 63% of non-correct judgments, and a relation plan extracted from the target paper raises required-relation accuracy (RRA) by up to 19.85 points, whereas a retrieved reference figure does not. Accuracy and appearance can also pull apart: in one cohort, plans make figures more complete but less readable. Improving such figures over time is not straightforward. A lesson from one paper, such as draw the observation that follows each state-changing action , must transfer as a question to ask rather than as content to copy, because similar-looking operations in another paper may consume different inputs. Aesthetic judgments are contextual and personal: what looks right depends on the figure’s purpose and on its author’s taste. The procedure that turns feedback into instruction edits is itself a design choice; prompt optimizers fix it ( Agrawal et al., 2026 ; Pryzant et al., 2023 ) , and Promptbreeder and the Darwin Gödel Machine evolve it ( Fernando et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) , but without a criterion of fidelity to each new source. We present Sivia, an overview-figure system that becomes more accurate and more attractive with use (Figure 1 ). It reads the paper, plans source-grounded relations, adapts the visual organization of overview figures from recent top-conference papers, and composes a detailed design for the image generator. Its state consists of a drawing skill K , an updater U , and a history H of edits and their outcomes. Learning to draw : the updater converts human findings on accuracy and presentation into bounded skill edits. Learning to learn : a meta-proposal revises the updater from the outcomes of earlier edits; the current and proposed updaters edit the same parent skill, and humans select and admit a skill together with its producing updater. A personal profile P_{u} further adapts presentation to its user’s aesthetic preferences while the scientific content stays fixed. On 48 test papers, learning both levels reaches 93.54% RRA, 15.31, 9.38, and 8.33 points above the initial skill, the Fixed-updater, and the GEPA baselines, and raises readability and visual quality from 3.95 and 4.00 to 4.70 and 4.78 on five-point scales. From a shared parent skill, the learned updater’s proposal exceeds that of the initial updater by 9.90 points, and learned presentation profiles raise liking from 4.02 to 4.73. Our contributions are: • Sivia, an overview-figure system that targets accuracy and aesthetics together, combining source-grounded relation planning with detailed conference-style visual design; • human-guided recursive self-improvement (RSI) at two levels, in which Sivia improves both its drawing skill and the procedure that improves it: skill edits phrased as transferable reading rules, an updater revised from the outcomes of earlier edits under same-parent comparison and two-stage human admission, and a personal profile that adapts presentation to its user; • a 445-paper diagnostic study and an evaluation that separately measures relation accuracy, error types, readability, visual quality, and preference.
