title: DynaMesh: Dynamic 3D Texture Generation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05529

§Abstract
We present DynaMesh, a dynamic texture generation method for 3D meshes. Given a textureless shape and a text prompt describing an effect, our method produces an appearance that evolves while the object's geometry remains unchanged. Previous works on dynamic 3D content generation have focused on motion, where an object's geometry and position change while keeping its appearance the same. Methods on texture generation sit on the other side of the problem, painting appearance onto a shape as a fixed surface property and not as an evolving process. Neither addresses a visual effect that propagates on a 3D object. A natural route consists of two generators: a video model that shows the effect from a single view, and an image-to-3D generator that lifts each frame to 3D. However, the latter has no notion of time, so running it per video frame produces a sequence that flickers, loses effect details, and yields a different mesh at every video frame. Our method addresses these failures by conditioning a video model on a render of the mesh and the prompt to obtain a reference video, then running a frozen image-to-3D generator on the video with two changes. The conditioning of each frame is blended over a temporal window, and low-rank adapters are fit per shape to restore the lost details. The mesh is encoded once for the whole sequence, so geometry is constant by construction, and the output is a single mesh with a texture per frame. Applied to various objects and effects, DynaMesh substantially improves over recent video-to-4D and texturing methods, and can generalize its temporal effect to different shapes never seen during training. Our project page is at https://threedle.github.io/dynamesh/.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Appearance change is an everyday phenomenon: ink spreading through paper, rust developing across a railing, frost blooming over a window, or paint peeling off a chair. Each of these examples is a process that propagates over the surface and changes its appearance over time. While the graphics literature has studied static object appearance [ 13 , 43 , 25 , 30 , 37 , 10 , 56 ] and object motion [ 42 , 46 , 41 , 44 , 34 , 32 , 29 ] at length, giving a 3D asset an evolving surface texture remains largely unexplored. Recent generative models each capture part of this problem. Video diffusion models [ 4 , 52 , 45 ] synthesize such phenomena convincingly, but their output is a flat sequence showing a single view , with no underlying mesh. A growing body of work in dynamic 3D generation lifts video priors into four dimensions, producing objects and scenes that change over time from text, an image, or a single video [ 40 , 2 , 57 , 21 , 35 , 36 , 53 , 1 ] . In all of them, however, the driving change is motion . The target object transforms while its appearance is settled at the outset and carried along by the moving geometry. In parallel, image-to-3D models [ 18 , 58 , 48 , 47 ] turn one picture into a detailed textured mesh, an asset that can be viewed and edited from any direction but has a static appearance . The same can be said for texture generation methods with a mesh as input, along with either text [ 7 , 6 , 49 ] , an image [ 55 , 56 ] , or several modalities [ 51 ] . To date, the generation of dynamic textures for 3D shapes has not been sufficiently explored, with existing efforts confined to repeating patterns on a mesh [ 31 ] or to texturing an object whose motion is given [ 3 ] . None of the model classes above offers the phenomena we look for, a surface seen from anywhere that changes over time. This raises the interesting question we pursue: can we generate a dynamic appearance for a 3D object? The difficulty is that this problem cannot be solved by simply pairing a video model with a 3D generator. A 3D generator has no temporal aspect as it simply maps one image to one object with no context of what came before. Running it frame by frame thus produces a sequence that flickers, since even near-identical frames will look different. Simply projecting the video onto the mesh also fails, as the video only shows one view of the object. Learning the task directly is equally out of reach without datasets of 3D objects with time-varying appearance. We thus introduce DynaMesh, a method for generating dynamic textures on 3D meshes. Given a mesh and a text prompt describing a temporal effect, we render a single view of the object and ask a frozen video model [ 24 ] for that effect unfolding from the given view. Because the camera and object are held still, the returned video focuses only on appearance change. Then, we take an image-to-3D generator built for static objects [ 47 ] and temporally blend its input frames to tackle flicker, allowing the effect seen from one view to be learned as appearance evolving over the entire surface. The result is a single mesh with one triangulation and a texture per frame, an asset that can be rendered from any viewpoint at any moment of the effect. DynaMesh contributes in two ways. First, the generator inputs are temporally blended. Rather than conditioning at each time step on its own video frame, we blend the conditioning over neighboring frames by augmenting the 3D generator with temporal attention. Second, we fit low-rank adapters per scene against the driving video, recovering the fine detail that a generator constrained toward 3D consistency would otherwise lose. The mesh is encoded as input for the generator and shared by every frame, so the object’s identity is preserved by construction and only the texture varies. We show that DynaMesh is highly flexible. Across a variety of meshes and prompts, it produces diverse temporal effects that are coherent from viewpoints unseen in the reference video (see Fig. 2 ). The adaptation also generalizes beyond the object it was trained on, as seen in Fig. 5 , where the texture transfers across unseen shapes without requiring retraining.
