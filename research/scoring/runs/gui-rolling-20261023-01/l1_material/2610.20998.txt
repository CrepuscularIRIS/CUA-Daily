title: MoCaP: Mobile Code-as-Policies for Zero-Shot Mobile Manipulation from Passive Human Videos
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20998

§Abstract
Passive human videos of everyday activity offer robots access to a massive amount of human experience without requiring a demonstration for each task. However, using this experience requires recovering knowledge from unstructured video recordings and adapting it to a different body (i.e., the robot). To overcome these challenges, we present mobile code-as-policy (MoCaP), an agentic framework that turns passive human video recordings into guidance for an end-to-end mobile manipulation policy. MoCaP extracts spatial memory and cues of how people prepare to interact with objects, which inform navigation and the search for a manipulation-ready configuration. Guided by passive human experience and current observations, MoCaP uses a closed-loop code-as-policy to coordinate planning and tool use primitives to solve motions under the robot's geometry and kinematic constraints. On a real mobile manipulator, MoCaP achieves 80% success on each of two mobile manipulation tasks in an indoor environment whereas existing approaches cannot succeed at the task. Video-derived spatial memory improves object-navigation success from 70% to 100%, and human approach prior raises the rate of finding a reachable base pose from 0% to 100% at an unfavorable start location. Videos and code are available on the project website: https://mocap-7.github.io/MoCAP-page/.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Household robots should be able to assist different users in a wide range of homes, responding to everyday requests without requiring users to specify every action. Open-vocabulary mobile manipulation moves toward this goal by enabling robots to act on natural-language instructions involving diverse objects and environments [ 1 ] . To fulfill a request such as “bring the cup back,” a robot must determine which objects and places are relevant, locate them, and decide how to act. Supporting this ability across homes and users motivates learning from experience beyond the robot’s own interactions. Passive recordings of human activity offer a potentially abundant source of such experience. Wearable cameras can capture how people interact with objects and their surroundings during everyday life, without requiring robot teleoperation or prescribed demonstrations [ 2 ] . We investigate how robots can draw on this accumulated human experience for open-vocabulary mobile manipulation. We consider the setting where a collection of human activity videos is passively recorded through people’s daily lives capturing diverse activities that may be requested of a robot. Rather than reproducing a particular demonstration, the robot must use relevant experience from the collection to guide its actions in its current environment. However, passive recordings of egocentric human data do not directly provide the information a robot needs to act. Specifically, using passive human experience for robots presents three challenges. First, the recorded experience lacks explicit spatial structure. A video supplies neither object coordinates nor labeled places and traversable connections. Relevant observations are distributed over time, with objects and regions reappearing from different viewpoints. Searching beyond the current view requires connecting objects to their spatial context. Second, human and robot sensing and morphology differ. Camera height and viewing angle change the visible scene, while different cameras introduce further differences in intrinsics, field of view, resolution, and color response. These visual discrepancies complicate matching recorded experience to the robot’s current observations. Body dimensions, joint structure, and available motions also determine which actions can be executed: a person can turn sideways through a narrow passage or manipulate an object with a dexterous hand, whereas a mobile manipulator has a different footprint and gripper. Transferring experience therefore requires interpreting observations across cameras and viewpoints and grounding actions in the robot’s own geometry and kinematics. Third, navigation and manipulation are not explicitly separated in human activity. People naturally adjust their approach and position as they get ready to interact, without marking when navigation ends and manipulation begins. For a robot, reaching the vicinity of an object does not ensure that it can manipulate it. The recording does not explicitly reveal which movements simply bring a person closer and which make the subsequent interaction possible. To overcome these challenges, we present MoCaP , which turns passive human video into guidance for mobile navigation and manipulation (Fig. 1 ). MoCaP extracts spatial memory about a place together with records of where people stood when they reached for objects. During task execution, the spatial memory helps locate the requested object, and the human approach prior guides the robot to approach the object in the right way. A code agent uses this guidance together with current observations to coordinate navigation and manipulation primitives. The primitives solve and check motions for the robot’s embodiment, returning execution feedback that lets the agent revise subsequent actions. Our contributions are: • A unified Code-as-Policy system for mobile manipulation. MoCaP flexibly coordinates navigation and manipulation through executable code, without a predefined division into navigation and manipulation stages. The agent selects and revises actions using prior human experience, live observations, and feedback from robot-grounded primitives. • Reusable spatial memory from passive human video. We develop a fine-grained spatial memory pipeline that draws on the abundance of everyday human activity recordings to extract object relations, landmarks, and place connectivity for subsequent robot search and navigation. This video is task-independent and can be reused to different tasks. • Human-guided base placement for manipulation. We infer the direction to approach object from how a person positioned themselves before manipulation in passive human videos, then search nearby base poses under the robot’s own reachability and collision constraints. A poor arrival side can leave no feasible pose within reach of local adjustment, and this guidance is designed to turn such arrivals into graspable configurations, using only video that accumulates from everyday activity [...]

## III Method
III-A Overview MoCaP takes a language instruction q , a prior human RGB recording V=\{I^{h}_{\tau}\} , and live robot observations o_{t}=(I_{t},D_{t},x_{t},z_{t}) containing RGB, depth, base pose, and joint state. Camera calibration, robot kinematics, and online local costmaps are available; the recording supplies no metric camera poses or target coordinates. All learned components remain frozen. Video processing produces reusable spatial memory and records of human interactions. At task startup, an advisor uses the memory, instruction, and initial robot view to provide guidance to a code agent. During execution, the code agent generates short Python programs that invoke robot-grounded primitives and revises subsequent programs using fresh observations and execution feedback. The human preparation prior guides docking, while the primitives resolve motions using live geometry and robot constraints (Fig. 2 ). III-B Offline Spatial and Interactive Memory Construction An offline builder organizes the recording into text m and timestamped reference frames E : M=(m,E)=\mathcal{B}(V),\qquad E=\{(\tau_{k},I^{h}_{\tau_{k}})\}_{k=1}^{K}. (1) The builder uses GPT-5.6 [ 26 ] to generate scene descriptions m that cite relevant video timestamps, then extracts the corresponding reference frames E . Together, the text and frames form the spatial memory M . The text retains places, connections, landmarks, and object descriptions with support surfaces and relative locations. The builder links small-object observations to their surrounding regions and records transition cues: where to turn, which landmark should become visible next, and how to recognize a revisited place. Ambiguous identities and inferred connections remain marked as uncertain. Up to 32 frames are extracted at timestamps cited by the memory, providing visual references for these descriptions. Given the instruction and initial robot image, a single startup advisor computes a_{0}=\mathcal{A}(M,q,I_{0}) . It receives the memory text and reference images with historical and live views explicitly distinguished. Its output describes the robot’s likely location, two to four navigation subgoals with progress cues, and the target’s appearance or support surface. This guidance is added to the code agent’s initial context and remains available through conversation history. The advisor is called once per task; the agent uses subsequent observations to revise how it follows the guidance. A separate event set \mathcal{H} records human interactions and the views preceding them. Mean grayscale frame differences identify low-motion windows, sampled at 2 Hz for VLM labeling. For each interaction, the model identifies the object, hand, last frame before reaching, and first contact. We save the ready frame, a view 2 s earlier, and the contact frame. The ready view captures the person’s position before arm motion changes the view or occludes the target; the preceding view provides additional scene context for geometric association. Both memory and event extraction precede downstream instructions. III-C Code-as-Policy and Robot-Grounded Primitives We implement closed-loop control through a Code-as-Policy module, in which a vision-language code agent generates Python programs that invoke robot-grounded primitives. We denote the agent’s code-generation policy by \pi . Given the instruction q , startup guidance a_{0} , current visual observation and state summaries \tilde{o}_{t} , and conversation history h_{t} , the agent generates a program c_{t} , which the runtime executes: c_{t}=\pi(q,a_{0},\tilde{o}_{t},h_{t}),\ (r_{t},o_{t+1})=\operatorname{ExecuteObserve}(c_{t}). (2) Execution feedback r_{t} and a fresh observation inform the next code-generation step. Failed action calls interrupt the remaining program, allowing the agent to revise its plan. History persists in the conversation. A typical manipulation sequence docks to a visible object, prepares the selected arm, samples a checked grasp, and requests its execution. If preparation fails, the subsequent grasp call is skipped, and the agent can change its approach using the returned diagnostics. SAM 3 [ 27 ] masks and live depth locate named targets. The docking primitive converts a visible target into a ground-plane standoff goal facing the object; Nav2 [ 20 ] handles navigation, and fresh perception verifies arrival. Bounded turning, forward/backward motion, and lateral translation support search and recovery when no docking target is available. These primitives ground video-derived guidance in the robot’s own viewpoint and motion capabilities. For manipulation, GraspGen-X [ 28 ] proposes grasps for the segmented target. Calibrated transforms convert model grasps to tool center point (TCP) goals, and open-gripper swept-volume checks reject approaches obstructed by observed non-target geometry. The following stages determine where the robot should stand and which grasps it can execute. III-D Approaching Object under Guidance from Human Prior At docking, we retrieve the first event in \mathcal{H} that shares a non-article word with the target description. Pretrained VGGT [ 29 ] takes the robot RGB image and the event’s preceding and ready views to estimate camera intrinsics and relative poses. We align the human camera with the robot camera using live depth, obtaining the human camera position C_{h} in the robot frame: \alpha=\operatorname{median}_{p\in\Omega}\frac{D_{t}(p)}{\widehat{D}_{t}(p)},\qquad C_{h}=-\alpha R_{h}^{\top}t_{h}, (3) where \Omega contains valid depth pixels and \alpha provides the metric scale. We project C_{h} and its optical axis onto the ground plane to obtain the human approach position p_{h} and heading \theta_{h} . We then approximate the human stance as p_{s}=p_{h}-\delta e(\theta_{h}),\qquad e(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\top}, (4) and derive the approach bearing toward the target position p_{o} as \beta_{h}=\begin{cases}\theta_{h}+\pi,&\|p_{h}-p_{o}\|\leq\epsilon,\\ \operatorname{atan2}((p_{s}-p_{o})_{y},(p_{s}-p_{o})_{x}),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} (5) When the human is already close to the object, the heading provides a more stable estimate than the relative position. The resulting bearing provides a human-derived approach prior rather than a fixed robot goal. We transfer \beta_{h} to the robot while choosing a robot-specific standoff distance \rho and checking reachability through local grasp and IK constraints. The desired camera position is p_{o}+\rho e(\beta_{h}) , from which the corresponding chassis goal is computed using the camera-to-base transform. If the resulting navigation goal is infeasible, we retry nearby approach directions before falling back to the original arrival direction. The grasping arm is selected according to the target’s side relative to the robot, with the human hand used to resolve ambiguous cases. Thus, human observations determine where and from which direction to approach , while robot-specific geometry determines the executable configuration. III-E Manipulation Preparation and Grasp Execution The manipulation preparation primitive searches nearby base configurations before moving. For each candidate base pose, we transform the retrieved grasp poses into the corresponding arm-base frame and use gripper collision and IK checks to efficiently filter infeasible configurations before invoking whole-arm trajectory planning. We require the target to be visible within 0.8 m and evaluate a local grid of planar translation and yaw adjustments around the current base pose. The search includes the zero adjustment, allowing an already suitable pose to proceed directly to grasping. Let T_{XY} denote the transformation from frame Y to frame X . Let C_{0} and C_{u} denote the current and candidate camera frames, and let H(u)=T_{C_{0}C_{u}} be the induced camera motion. For a grasp pose G_{j}=T_{C_{0}G_{j}} , the corresponding candidate TCP goal is T_{A,j}(u)=T_{AC}H(u)^{-1}G_{j}T_{GE}, (6) where T_{AC} is the fixed camera-to-arm-base calibration for the selected arm and T_{GE} expresses the TCP frame E in the grasp-model frame G . Thus, the grasp is first expressed in the candidate camera frame and then transformed into the arm-base frame. We shortlist candidate base poses using workspace constraints and a lightweight geometric score, then retain poses for which at least one grasp passes gripper collision and nominal IK checks. Among feasible candidates, we prefer small base adjustments while favoring configurations with multiple and robustly feasible grasps. The resulting grasp set and base configuration are stored together for subsequent execution. The selected adjustment is executed as an absolute planar base goal. After motion, grasp feasibility is re-evaluated using fresh observations because controller error can invalidate the virtual prediction. The retained alternatives are then passed to cuRobo [ 30 ] , which plans whole-arm motion through a pre-grasp pose and constrained approach using the measured robot state and fresh scene geometry. Planning or execution failure returns control to the agent. Spatial memory guides where to search, while the human approach prior guides the initial base placement. The code agent combines these priors with live observations and execution feedback, while robot-specific feasibility checks determine the motions that can be executed.
