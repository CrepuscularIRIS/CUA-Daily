title: GapLeak: When Privacy-Preserving LLM-based Web Agents Become a Privacy Risk
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22223

§Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM)-based web agents rely on data minimization and least privilege to protect user privacy. We show that these principles are necessary but insufficient: they constrain authorized sources, but not higher-sensitivity sources that remain reachable. A website can request a field unavailable from authorized sources, creating an information gap. To complete the task, a goal-driven agent may escalate to reachable but unauthorized sources and disclose accumulated sensitive information. We formalize this vulnerability as GapLeak, where an adversary exploits a legitimate web form without prompt injection or other malicious instructions. We evaluate GapLeak on three web-agent frameworks and six LLMs from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI across recruitment, e-commerce, travel, and healthcare. GapLeak induces an average unauthorized disclosure of approximately 37%. The information gap is the primary driver: eliminating it reduces disclosure by more than 50%. Existing instruction-level defenses based on least privilege partially mitigate the vulnerability but often reduce task success. We propose submission-time verification, which filters unauthorized content before form submission, eliminating over 96% of unauthorized disclosures while preserving task success.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language model (LLM)-based web agents can autonomously navigate websites, fill out forms, and submit data, transforming the browser from a user-operated interface into a delegated task-execution environment ( Ersoy et al., 2025 ; Ning et al., 2025 ; Zhou et al., 2023 ; Gartner, 2025 ) . Commercial agents, such as DoBrowser ( , 2025 ) and OpenAI’s Operator ( OpenAI, 2025 ) , have already demonstrated multi-step workflows, including job applications, e-commerce returns, travel bookings, and medical intake form completion. By mid-2025, 62% of organizations reported experimenting with LLM-based agents, while 23% had already begun deploying them at scale in at least one business function ( McKinsey & Company, 2025 ) . Agentic automation is transitioning from a proof-of-concept technology to a foundational component of everyday digital infrastructure. However, the successful execution of these delegated tasks inherently requires agents to access users’ personal information ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) . For example, booking a single trip may require access to a user’s travel itinerary, payment credentials, calendar availability, and even the contact information of accompanying travelers. Consequently, each delegated task grants autonomous agents broad visibility into sensitive personal data. Because these agents independently interact with websites and external services on behalf of users, private information may be disclosed, inferred, or propagated across multiple domains without the user’s awareness or explicit consent ( Zhang and Yang, 2025 ; Chiang et al., 2025 ; Ukani et al., 2025 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ) . These privacy risks have attracted growing attention from both the research community and standards organizations. In 2026, the U.S. NIST launched an AI Agent Standards initiative and solicited public input on emerging security and privacy threats associated with autonomous agents ( National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2026 ) . In parallel, researchers have advocated two fundamental privacy-preserving principles for agent design: data minimization , which restricts the information exposed to the agent, and least privilege , which limits data sources the agent is permitted to access. AgentDAM ( Zharmagambetov et al., 2026 ) introduced a benchmark for evaluating whether agents adhere to data minimization by accessing sensitive information only when necessary. AirGapAgent ( Bagdasarian et al., 2024 ) applies the least-privilege principle by filtering user data and restricting the agent’s access to only the information required for the current task. The recent studies ( Zharmagambetov et al., 2026 ; Ghalebikesabi et al., 2025 ; Mireshghallah et al., 2024 ; Bagdasarian et al., 2024 ) share a common assumption: if an agent is provided with only the minimum necessary information and granted only the minimum necessary access, unauthorized disclosure can be effectively prevented. However, we observe that these principles are necessary but not sufficient. Data minimization and least privilege constrain the agent’s authorized scope, meaning the information the user provides and the sources the task sanctions. However, the two principles do not constrain the agent’s reachable scope, which still includes higher-sensitivity sources present in the agent’s environment. As a result, a residual gap remains between what the agent is authorized to use and what it can actually reach. Consider a user who requests a product return and provides only the item name, purchase date, and reason for return. The user withholds the complete order history and payment details, yet both records remain reachable through the user’s logged-in account. When a website then requests a field that no authorized source can provide, an information gap arises. The agent must now choose between two options. It can leave the field blank and risk task failure, or it can escalate to a reachable but unauthorized source and risk the user’s privacy. Since current web agents are goal-driven by design, they tend to escalate rather than abandon the task ( Yao et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) . The information gap is the primary driver of unauthorized disclosure. When no authorized source can supply the requested field, the agent reaches into unauthorized sources to complete the task, and sensitive content the user never intended to share is exposed in the process. The exposed content is not limited to the user’s own data. It routinely includes third-party information such as reference contacts, accompanying travelers, and family members, whose exposure constitutes a privacy harm regardless of how the user chose to interact with the site. We formalize this vulnerability as GapLeak , which exploits the gap between the agent’s authorized and reachable scope to induce disclosure beyond the task’s authorized scope.
