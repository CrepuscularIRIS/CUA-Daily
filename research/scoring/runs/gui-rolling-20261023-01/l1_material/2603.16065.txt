title: Large Reward Models: Generalizable Online Robot Reward Generation with Vision-Language Models
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16065

§Abstract
Reinforcement Learning (RL) has shown strong potential for improving robotic manipulation policies, yet its practical use remains bottlenecked by the difficulty of specifying reward functions that are both semantically meaningful and reusable across tasks. In this paper, we propose Large Reward Models (LRMs), a framework that adapts foundation VLMs into frame-level reward generators for robot policy refinement. We specialize a state-of-the-art VLM on a multi-source dataset spanning real-world robot trajectories, human-object interactions, and simulated manipulation environments. Unlike prior approaches that mainly evaluate trajectories post-hoc, LRMs expose multiple reward interfaces from visual observations: progress estimation, task completion, and temporal contrastive comparison. Starting from an imitation-learned policy, we use these VLM-derived rewards to guide PPO refinement on held-out long-horizon manipulation tasks. Our experiments show that LRM progress rewards provide the strongest non-privileged online refinement signal, improving the IL baseline and narrowing the gap to privileged environment rewards. We further deploy progress rewards for progress-weighted behavioral cloning on four real-world manipulation tasks spanning two robot platforms, improving over SFT on all four tasks. These results suggest that modality-specific specialization of foundation VLMs can provide practical visual reward signals for both simulated policy refinement and physical robot self-improvement without hand-coded task rewards.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The development of generalist robot policies has been accelerated by large-scale visuomotor and vision-language-action pre-training across diverse robotic datasets [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . These policies provide strong imitation-learning (IL) backbones, but IL pre-training alone can still plateau on tasks requiring high precision, semantic judgment, or long-horizon coordination. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a principled mechanism for post-training policy refinement and has shown progress in both real-world manipulation and broader robot learning pipelines [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] . Its effectiveness, however, remains tightly coupled to the availability of accurate, dense, and task-aware reward functions. In real-world and complex simulated environments, obtaining such rewards is still a major bottleneck, as traditional approaches often rely on labor-intensive human feedback or brittle task-specific reward engineering. Learning visual rewards has therefore become a central direction for scalable robot policy improvement. Prior work has learned reward representations or temporal structure from demonstrations and human videos [ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] , used language or vision-language models to generate reward programs, preferences, progress estimates, or token-probability rewards [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] , and adapted process-level or critic-style supervision for robotic manipulation [ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] . These methods demonstrate that foundation models can provide semantically meaningful feedback, but online policy refinement imposes additional interface constraints: rewards must be state-dependent, temporally informative, easy to parse into scalar feedback, and computationally practical during closed-loop interaction. This motivates directly specializing foundation VLMs into compact, frame-level reward interfaces rather than relying on a single monolithic reward format. To bridge this gap, we introduce Large Reward Models (LRMs) , a framework for adapting foundation VLMs into frame-level reward generators. We develop a suite of LRMs based on the Qwen3-VL [ 25 ] architecture and train them to formulate state-dependent rewards directly from visual observations. To support evaluation beyond the training domains, our models are trained on a balanced collection sampled from 24 data sources. This multi-domain dataset encompasses real-robot corpora (Open X-Embodiment [ 3 ] ), human-object interaction data (HOI4D [ 26 ] , EgoDex [ 27 ] ), and simulated environments (LIBERO [ 28 ] , RoboCasa [ 29 ] ). LRMs provide Temporal Contrastive , Absolute Progress , and Task Completion rewards for relative temporal ordering, dense progress shaping, and semantic success verification, respectively. This modular formulation allows us to compare reward modalities without assuming that their outputs should be directly combined. We evaluate our LRM framework on held-out long-horizon manipulation benchmarks within ManiSkill3 [ 30 ] . This benchmark serves as a testbed for coupling policy learning with learned visual rewards, allowing us to measure whether VLM-derived feedback can improve policies without privileged simulator rewards. We further deploy the progress interface for real-world weighted behavioral cloning on four physical manipulation tasks spanning two robot platforms. In simulation, all three LRM reward interfaces achieve higher mean success rates than the IL baseline, with progress reward performing best; in the real world, LRM progress scoring improves over SFT on all four tasks. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce Large Reward Models (LRMs) , a framework that adapts foundation VLMs into frame-level visual reward generators for robot policy refinement. • The framework provides contrastive, progress, and completion interfaces that convert VLM outputs into parseable, state-dependent reward signals. • Experiments validate LRMs through online PPO refinement on held-out ManiSkill3 tasks and progress-weighted behavioral cloning across four real-world tasks on two robot platforms. • We analyze reward combination, query intervals, and inference latency for practical deployment.

## III METHODOLOGY
While Reinforcement Learning (RL) can surpass imitation learning plateaus, its effectiveness depends on reward functions that provide both temporal resolution and semantic task understanding. To reduce the need for task-specific reward engineering, we propose Large Reward Models (LRMs), a framework that adapts foundation VLMs into frame-level visual reward generators. Our framework utilizes Reinforcement Learning (RL) to refine a robotic policy \pi_{\phi} by maximizing the expected return under a selected reward interface m : J(\pi_{\phi};m)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\phi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{m}(\mathcal{I}_{t}^{m},d)\right] (1) where \mathcal{I}_{t}^{m} denotes the modality-specific visual input and d is the task description. To obtain reward mappings without manual reward engineering, we construct a structured dataset \mathcal{D}=\{(I_{t},d,p_{t})\} from unlabeled videos and specialize a foundation VLM into three reward interfaces: Temporal Contrastive ( r_{cont} ), Absolute Progress ( r_{prog} ), and Task Completion ( r_{comp} ). Each interface maps visual observations and task language into parseable reward feedback, enabling LRMs to serve as frame-level reward providers for policy refinement. III-A Extracting Reward Signals from Multi-domain Unlabeled Videos To scale reward generation without manual reward labels, we extract supervision directly from a balanced subset of unlabeled video trajectories V sampled from 24 sources. We use the temporal order of successful or demonstrative episodes as weak supervision: later frames are treated as higher-progress states than earlier frames. By mapping each sampled video timeline to a discrete progress scale, we transform raw frames into a structured dataset \mathcal{D}=\{(I_{t},d,p_{t})\} for training visual reward models. Multi-domain Dataset Composition : To improve transfer to held-out environments, we sample training episodes from 24 data sources covering diverse physical interactions, visual domains, and task semantics. We count sources at the dataset level rather than the collection-family level: each named Open X-Embodiment sub-dataset is counted independently because it corresponds to a distinct robot embodiment, workspace, collection protocol, or task distribution. The source pool contains 20 Open X-Embodiment real-robot sub-datasets [ 3 ] , 2 egocentric human-object interaction datasets (HOI4D [ 26 ] and EgoDex [ 27 ] ), and 2 simulated benchmarks (LIBERO [ 28 ] and RoboCasa [ 29 ] ). Temporal Progression Sampling : To construct supervision for the three reward interfaces, we sample each episode at normalized times p\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9,1.0\} , yielding 11 keyframes spanning the recorded execution. Each frame is paired with its temporal label and the episode’s original task description d . Normalizing the timeline places episodes of different durations on a common scale and provides supervision without manual frame-level reward annotation. III-B Tri-faceted Reward Formulation To bridge high-level semantic instructions and the feedback requirements of policy optimization, we formulate three reward interfaces from the temporal progress of multi-domain videos. The interfaces are complementary but not assumed to be naturally commensurate: each is intended to support a different use case in robot policy refinement. The Temporal Contrastive Reward is designed to provide a relative directional gradient by comparing the current state against a previous observation. Given the current frame I_{t} , a previous frame I_{t-\Delta t} , and the task description d , this format evaluates which state is semantically closer to the task goal. The qualitative progression is mapped to a discrete numerical reward r_{cont} : r_{cont}=\begin{cases}+1.0,&\text{if }I_{t}\text{ is closer to the goal than }I_{t-\Delta t}\\ -1.0,&\text{if }I_{t-\Delta t}\text{ is closer to the goal than }I_{t}\\ 0.0,&\text{if no valid selection is produced}\end{cases} (2) By gauging relative progress between frames, this formulation avoids the need for globally calibrated absolute scores and provides directional feedback about local task improvement. To provide spatial-temporal grounding, the Absolute Progress Reward is formulated as ordinal progress prediction over discretized normalized progress bins. Given the current observation I_{t} , optionally with the initial state I_{0} as a visual anchor, and the task description d , the reward signal r_{prog} represents a normalized completion score: r_{prog}\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9,1.0\} (3) The eleven bins represent normalized temporal progress under the training supervision, allowing the interface to assign graded scores to intermediate observations. The Task Completion Reward provides a definitive binary assessment of the current state I_{t} . Given the observation I_{t} , optionally with the initial state I_{0} as visual context, and task description d , the signal is defined as a binary classification to determine if the semantic requirements of the goal have been fully satisfied: r_{comp}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if semantic requirements are met}\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} (4) By outputting a binary success signal r_{comp} , this modality provides a semantic terminal judgment that is useful for success verification, sparse reward assignment, and offline trajectory filtering. III-C Training LRMs for Reward Generation We initialize each LRM from Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and train the three reward modalities separately on the structured dataset \mathcal{D} . Completion and contrastive update distinct Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters [ 40 ] while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen. Progress updates its LoRA adapter together with the vision encoder and multimodal merger, while the remaining backbone parameters stay frozen. We do not introduce new reward tokens or architectural reward heads. Each model generates text using the normal autoregressive vocabulary, and a deterministic parser converts the structured output into a scalar or binary reward. Prompting specifies the visual context, task instruction, and output schema rather than task-specific hand-coded reward rules. For all three modalities, training mixes anchored and unanchored visual inputs: unanchored inputs contain only the minimal observation set for that modality, while anchored inputs additionally include the episode’s initial frame I_{0} as visual context. Thus, progress and completion examples may use either I_{t} or (I_{0},I_{t}) , while contrastive examples may use either (I_{t-\Delta t},I_{t}) or (I_{0},I_{t-\Delta t},I_{t}) . Contrastive deployment additionally generates a short physical comparison before the final reward. The zero-shot Qwen3-VL baselines use the original pretrained weights with the same prompts and parsers. The contrastive discrimination model is trained as a preference judge to provide relative directional gradients by comparing the visual input \mathcal{I}^{cont} , which contains the two temporal frames I_{t-\Delta t} and I_{t} in randomized order and may additionally include the initial frame I_{0} . We utilize Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [ 41 ] to align the model’s preferences with the temporal progression recorded in \mathcal{D} using the following objective: \begin{split}L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(&\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid\mathcal{I}^{cont},d)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid\mathcal{I}^{cont},d)}\\ &-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid\mathcal{I}^{cont},d)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid\mathcal{I}^{cont},d)}\Big)\Big]\end{split} (5) where (y_{w},y_{l}) are the preferred and rejected text responses induced by temporal order. We ask the model to produce a short physical comparison before outputting r_{cont}\in\{+1,-1,0\} , so the reward is based on task-relevant object interactions rather than low-level visual changes. The progress estimation and completion judgment models are both trained via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to map their visual inputs to r_{prog}\in\{0.0,\dots,1.0\} and r_{comp}\in\{0,1\} , respectively. For the progress estimation model , we maximize the likelihood of a joint reasoning-and-label sequence: \mathcal{L}_{\text{prog}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log P(\mathrm{CoT},r_{\text{prog}}\mid\mathcal{I}^{prog},d)\right] (6) This reasoning-first supervision encourages the model to articulate physical cues before concluding r_{prog} . Although CoT is used for supervision, progress inference returns only the final structured score. In contrast, the completion judgment model is optimized to directly predict the binary success signal: \mathcal{L}_{\text{comp}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log P(r_{\text{comp}}\mid\mathcal{I}^{comp},d)\right] (7) This provides a terminal signal for goal verification and policy refinement. III-D Online Policy Refinement via LRM-Generated Rewards To move beyond imitation learning plateaus, we use the frozen LRMs as visual reward providers for refining a base policy \pi_{\phi} initialized from \pi_{SFT} . This stage uses closed-loop interaction and PPO updates, while keeping the reward model fixed. Online Interaction and Reward Integration : During each episode, the policy interacts with the environment by sampling actions a_{t}\sim\pi_{\phi}(a_{t}\mid I_{t},d) based on the current visual observation I_{t} and task description d . To bridge the computational gap between VLM inference and control, we use an Interval-Hold strategy. The selected LRM is queried every K environment steps to compute r_{m}=\text{LRM}_{m}(\mathcal{I}_{t}^{m},d) for one reward modality m\in\{cont,prog,comp\} . r_{t}=w_{m}r_{m}, (8) where w_{m} is the scaling factor for the selected modality. The reward r_{t} is cached and held for K steps. Each single-interface policy refinement run uses one selected reward model and its modality-specific parameters. Policy Optimization and Refinement : We utilize Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to update the policy parameters \phi . The policy is optimized with the clipped surrogate objective: \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\min\!\left(\rho_{t}\hat{A}_{t},\bar{\rho}_{t}\hat{A}_{t}\right)\right], (9) where \rho_{t}=\pi_{\phi}(a_{t}\mid I_{t},d)/\pi_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{t}\mid I_{t},d) is the probability ratio between the updated and behavior policies, and \bar{\rho}_{t}=\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon) is its clipped value. To ensure stable convergence and handle the dense nature of LRM feedback, we employ Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to compute the advantage \hat{A}_{t} : \hat{A}_{t}=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\delta_{t+l},\quad\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V_{\psi}(I_{t+1})-V_{\psi}(I_{t}), (10) where \delta_{t} is the TD error computed from the LRM reward r_{t} , enabling optimization from visual feedback rather than privileged simulator state or hand-coded rewards.
