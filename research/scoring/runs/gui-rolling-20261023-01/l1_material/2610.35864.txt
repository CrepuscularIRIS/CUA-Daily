title: VibeVideoBench: Evaluating Multimodal Agents on Code-Driven Video Generation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35864

§Abstract
Diffusion video models produce striking clips, but they cannot reliably render an exact formula, a correct chart, a rule-consistent game or narration synchronized to on-screen events. A different route is emerging: multimodal agents that write code to render every frame and synthesize every sound. We introduce VibeVideoBench, a benchmark for evaluating agents on code-driven video generation. Its 239 self-contained tasks in English and Chinese span 12 categories, from data stories and music-driven pieces to classroom lessons and paper explainers; each asks an agent, running in its native harness inside a sandbox with no image or video generation model, to deliver a finished MP4 with sound within one hour. Evaluation is hierarchical and evidence-based: program checks of the delivery spec, about 13k task-specific rubric checks traced to the requirements they enforce, and five quality dimensions anchored per task, scored by three VLM judge panels. Across nine frontier agent systems and 2,151 scored rollouts, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 rank first and second with both the judges and human annotators, and the strongest system satisfies every core requirement on 76.3% of tasks. Delivery is a second axis: counting missing videos as zero changes the ranking, and three quarters of the lost videos are final renders that started too late, ran too slowly or never started, a failure of how agents plan their hour. Agents build their own rendering pipelines and inspect their own frames, yet how much they look does not predict quality. The judges' ranking of the systems closely matches blind human preferences.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Video generation has advanced rapidly through diffusion transformers [ 7 , 21 , 32 , 51 , 56 , 45 ] , and benchmarks such as VBench [ 27 , 59 ] and EvalCrafter [ 36 ] measure how plausible and prompt-aligned the generated clips look. Yet many of the videos people actually make—classroom lessons, paper explainers, data stories, product walkthroughs, assembly sequences, rhythm games—demand properties that pixel generators do not guarantee: exact text and numbers, rule-consistent state changes, multi-minute structure, sound aligned to events , and an artifact that can be revised. These are natural properties of programs . Humans already make such videos with Manim or Remotion, and coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex [ 1 , 41 ] can now work autonomously for hours, driving a full toolchain—a renderer, a headless browser, a TTS engine, ffmpeg—on their own. Users have begun to ask agents for finished videos this way, which requires a multimodal agent that reads input assets, produces picture and sound through code, and inspects its own renders to correct them. How good are agents at making videos? Existing evaluations do not answer this. Video-generation benchmarks score short, silent clips from generative models [ 27 , 36 , 24 ] . Code-to-visual benchmarks score single programs or static artifacts such as charts, web pages and SVGs [ 48 , 49 , 58 , 11 ] . Code-based lesson and paper-to-video systems [ 33 , 12 , 63 ] are language-model pipelines: a workflow designed by hand for one domain and one renderer, in which a model is called at preset steps; they do not test general agents. Agentic benchmarks [ 30 , 38 , 54 ] verify textual or functional outcomes with unit tests, whereas a video is a perceptual, temporal and audio-visual outcome for which no unit test exists. Video therefore tests what text-only agentic benchmarks cannot: whether an agent can check its own output across picture, sound and time. We present VibeVideoBench (Figs. 1 and 2 ), a benchmark that evaluates multimodal agents end to end on code-driven video generation : given a specification and optional input assets, an agent must deliver a finished MP4, built entirely with code, inside a sandbox within one hour, with narration, music and sound effects as each task’s audio policy specifies. Every frame is rendered by code: no image or video generation model is available, and the only generative tool is TTS. Its 239 tasks span 12 categories, from data stories and music-driven pieces to classroom lessons and paper explainers, in English and Chinese. Three design choices shape it. Tasks must be realistic yet checkable. Real requests are open-ended, vague and often missing the content they refer to, so they cannot be checked as written. We build tasks from four sources—real user requests to code-video tools (rewritten), real videos (seeded from their dense captions), real asset packs such as 3D scans and game levels, and lecture notes and research papers—and rewrite each into a self-contained specification that fixes facts, identities, stages, timing and the audio policy while preserving creative freedom. Evaluation must rest on evidence in the video. A video has no unit test, a holistic score cannot be traced to evidence, a judge may credit a result the video merely claims, and many VLMs cannot hear. Evaluation therefore has three layers with different executors. Program checks (L1) automatically verify the delivery spec—resolution, frame rate, duration, audible sound, source project. Rubric checks (L2; about 13k in total) are each traced to the requirement they enforce, separate process shown from result claimed , and are tagged by modality. Quality scores (L3) rate five dimensions—visual quality, aesthetics, content logic, timing and audio—each anchored at 1, 3 and 5 by descriptions written for the task. L2 and L3 are judged by VLM judges in three independent panels: two watch the full video with sound, and the third routes visual checks to a vision model and audio checks to an audio-capable model, so audio checks never reach a judge that cannot hear. Agents are evaluated as they are deployed. Users adopt a model together with its harness, yet a fair comparison needs identical conditions. Each model therefore runs fully autonomously in its vendor’s native harness at the highest effort its provider accepts, with no follow-up turns and all tool permissions pre-approved, in identical containers with the same one-hour budget; we evaluate complete agent systems rather than separating model from harness.
