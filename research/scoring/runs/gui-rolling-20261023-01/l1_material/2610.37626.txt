title: Know, then Draw: A New Learning Paradigm for Visual Generation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37626

§Abstract
Visual generation requires both high-level semantic knowledge and fine-grained pixel details. We propose a learning paradigm that assigns these roles to autoregressive pretraining for knowledge acquisition and diffusion post-training for high-fidelity rendering. Within this framework, we study the objective, representation, and scaling of knowledge acquisition and rendering. Controlled comparisons identify autoregression as the stronger pretraining objective for downstream rendering, improving both learning efficiency and final generation quality. We further find that AR-only generation quality does not necessarily correlate with final performance; semantic quality matters more. This motivates a purely semantic tokenizer trained with language supervision alone, without an image decoder or reconstruction loss. We establish compute-optimal scaling laws for knowledge acquisition and rendering, and find that autoregressive pretraining lowers the fitted asymptotic rendering loss compared with pixel diffusion models trained from scratch. Guided by the laws, we train a 3B-parameter text-to-image model with only 6,600 H100 GPU-hours, achieving 86 on GenEval and 87 on DPG-Bench and validating the proposed paradigm at scale.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Visual generation has advanced primarily through two modeling paradigms: autoregressive models predict discrete visual tokens ( Sun et al., 2024 ; Tian et al., 2024 ; Pang et al., 2024 ) , while latent diffusion models (LDMs) and pixel diffusion models (PDMs) denoise continuous signals ( Ho et al., 2020 ; Rombach et al., 2022 ; Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) . They are typically compared as competing paradigms, asking which produces better images. Yet this comparison asks each paradigm to solve two distinct learning problems: acquiring high-level visual knowledge and rendering it into detailed images . We therefore argue that these problems need not favor the same objective, and look beyond their competition to uncover the synergy between autoregression and diffusion. We find that the two problems indeed favor different objectives: autoregression for visual knowledge acquisition and diffusion for rendering . This specialization gives rise to a natural pretraining–post-training paradigm , where autoregressive pretraining provides the foundation on which diffusion is post-trained for rendering. This mirrors modern language models, where broad knowledge is first acquired and subsequently adapted toward desired behaviors. At inference, the autoregressive rollout provides a structured visual representation that conditions diffusion, connecting the two learning processes. Across the evaluated protocols and compute budgets, this specialization consistently yields stronger generators, revealing the synergy between the two objectives. Specializing autoregression for knowledge acquisition changes what it should learn. In visual autoregressive models, the tokenizer defines the prediction target and therefore shapes the information the model is incentivized to capture. Conventional tokenizers preserve low-level appearance for image reconstruction. Once rendering is delegated to diffusion, however, the autoregressive representation no longer needs to support reconstruction or standalone image generation. Across different tokenizers, we find a striking dissociation: neither reconstruction quality nor AR-only generation quality determines post-training generation performance; semantic quality does. This motivates Semantic-LM , a semantic tokenizer trained through language supervision alone, without an image decoder or reconstruction loss. Visual tokenization thus shifts from preserving pixels for reconstruction to providing semantic learning targets for autoregressive knowledge acquisition. Separating knowledge acquisition from rendering introduces a new question for scaling visual generation: how should compute be allocated between the two stages? We establish scaling laws for autoregressive pretraining and diffusion post-training that characterize the effects of model size, data, and compute and guide compute-optimal allocation. These laws also reveal a broader benefit of autoregressive pretraining. At matched model and data scales, diffusion post-training achieves both lower loss and a lower fitted asymptotic rendering loss than PDM trained from scratch. This finding suggests that autoregressive pretraining improves the attainable performance of diffusion rendering beyond accelerating convergence speed. Given an available training budget of approximately 6,600 H100 GPU-hours, we use our scaling laws to select a roughly 3B-parameter model and allocate compute between pretraining and post-training. The resulting model achieves strong text-to-image generation (Fig. 1 ), reaching 0.86 on GenEval ( Ghosh et al., 2023 ) and 87.78 on DPG-Bench ( Hu et al., 2024 ) . This performance is achieved with approximately 1/48 of the estimated training compute of Z-Image-Base ( Z-Image Team et al., 2025 ) , while attaining comparable DPG-Bench performance.
