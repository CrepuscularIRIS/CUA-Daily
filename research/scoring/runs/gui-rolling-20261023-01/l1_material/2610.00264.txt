title: A Mobile Agent-Based Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Framework for Energy-Balanced Data Collection and Wireless Charging in WSN
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00264

§Abstract
Energy imbalance remains a key challenge in Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), as nodes near the base station deplete their energy faster due to heavy forwarding loads. While mobile agents (MAs) have been employed for either data collection or sensor charging, existing approaches lack adaptability and fail to integrate both functions under realistic hardware constraints. This paper introduces a unified mobile agent framework that performs both data collection and wireless charging sequentially under single-antenna limitations. The agent's decision-making is formulated as a two-layer Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) problem, where the upper layer optimizes movement planning and the lower layer determines the appropriate service based on real-time network states. This hierarchical structure enables the agent to learn adaptive task scheduling policies without predefined rules. Extensive simulations demonstrate that the proposed method achieves up to 15% longer network lifetime and more balanced energy distribution compared with state-of-the-art mobile agent and deep RL approaches.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), composed of small battery-powered nodes that collect and transmit environmental data [ 1 ] , are widely applied in diverse domains such as industry, environment, and healthcare [ 2 ] . They have become fundamental to modern Internet of Things (IoT) applications [ 3 ] . However, as WSNs expand, new challenges emerge in data transmission, energy efficiency, and long-term network performance [ 4 ] . Due to their limited communication range, sensor nodes typically rely on multi-hop transmission to forward data to the base station [ 5 , 6 ] . Consequently, nodes located closer to the base station experience a heavier communication load, as they must not only send their own data but also relay data from other nodes [ 7 ] . This uneven energy consumption leads to bottlenecks, causing these nodes to deplete their energy rapidly, which in turn shortens the overall network lifetime and may eventually disrupt multi-hop communication links [ 8 ] . As a result, data transmission is interrupted, and the base station loses visibility into the network’s status. To balance energy consumption and extend the lifetime of WSNs, researchers have explored various strategies [ 4 , 9 ] . Common approaches include energy harvesting from the environment [ 10 ] , improving clustering algorithms [ 11 ] , data aggregation [ 12 ] , fixed charging stations [ 13 ] , and the use of mobile sinks [ 14 ] . More recently, mobile agents (MAs)—autonomous entities capable of moving within the network—have gained increasing attention due to their flexibility and multifunctional capabilities [ 15 , 16 ] . By traversing the network, mobile agents can collect data directly from sensor nodes and deliver them to the base station, thereby reducing multi-hop communication and alleviating network congestion [ 17 ] . Furthermore, they can enhance network longevity by performing on-demand tasks or recharging sensor nodes through radio-frequency (RF)-based wireless energy transfer [ 18 ] . In previous research on mobile agents in WSNs, many studies have focused solely on either data collection [ 19 ] or sensor charging [ 20 ] , and only a few have attempted to integrate both tasks into a single mobile agent [ 21 ] . Even in those few cases, the agent usually follows fixed rules—for example, predefined paths and actions [ 22 ] . However, fixed rules make it difficult for mobile agents to adapt their actions—such as choosing when and where to charge or collect data—especially in networks where conditions frequently change. This challenge is further complicated by technical limitations that prevent a mobile agent from performing both data collection and sensor charging simultaneously in practical single-antenna systems [ 23 , 24 ] . Attempting simultaneous operations leads to electromagnetic interference and signal separation difficulties [ 25 ] . Therefore, practical implementations require the mobile agent to alternate between these tasks sequentially. These limitations highlight the need for an adaptive decision-making framework that intelligently schedules when to perform each task based on network conditions. To address the limitations of previous rule-based mobile agent strategies, we propose a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning-based framework, called DCR-HRL (Data Collection, Charging, and Routing with Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning), in which a single mobile agent adaptively alternates between data collection and sensor charging tasks under realistic single-antenna constraints. The proposed model explicitly decomposes the agent’s decision-making into two coordinated layers: (i) a service-selection layer that determines whether to collect data or recharge nodes based on local energy and buffer states, and (ii) a movement-planning layer that selects the next destination considering aggregate energy and buffer conditions of the adjacent subdomains. This hierarchical separation enables the agent to learn optimal sequential policies through experience, without relying on predefined rules or simultaneous transmission assumptions. Unlike existing single-layer or heuristic approaches, DCR-HRL unifies data collection, energy replenishment, and mobility optimization within a single adaptive learning framework. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that integrates both data collection and wireless charging in a single mobile agent through hierarchical decision learning, achieving significant improvements in energy balance and network lifetime over existing methods. The behavior of the mobile agent is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) [ 26 ] divided into two sub-problems and solved using a Hierarchical Q-learning algorithm [ 27 ] . At the higher layer, the agent learns to choose the optimal path through the network based on the buffer occupancy and node energy levels of the candidate destination subdomains. ## IV System Model and Assumptions
This section presents the system model along with the key assumptions underlying the proposed approach. IV-A Network Model In this study, the network is divided into several square-shaped subdomains, where each subdomain represents a possible stationing location for the mobile agent. Each subdomain contains N nodes using uniform distribution to make sure each grid has almost the same number of sensors. The RF capability of the mobile agent operates within a well-defined service radius of R_{\text{service}}=10 meters, allowing it to serve up to M_{\text{max}}=5 nodes simultaneously within each subdomain through broadcast-based energy transfer and data collection protocols. By using a uniform distribution [ 49 ] , we ensure that each subdomain contains approximately the same number of sensors. The placement for each sensor is determined in Equation ( 1 ). y\sim\text{Uniform}(y_{\text{min}},\ y_{\text{max}}),\quad x\sim\text{Uniform}(x_{\text{min}},\ x_{\text{max}}) (1) Each sensor discovers its neighbors using a communication range of R_{\text{comm}} . At the discovery stage, multi-hop communication is established with the base station so that each sensor can find its neighbors and determine the corresponding distance to the next node. In order to minimize energy consumption, sensors send their data packets to the neighbor within their RF communication range that is closest to the base station, following a greedy approach. Once deployed in the network, the sensors remain static. Each sensor saves the list of neighbors in its memory in a sorted manner so that if the next hop dies and the connection with the closest node is terminated, a new connection will be established with the second closest node to the base station within the neighbors. In every timestep, each sensor generates between 1 and 3 data packets, depending on the available buffer space. In this case, energy depletion increases as packet generation grows. Each sensor has the capacity to store up to 100 data packets in its buffer. IV-B Communication Model In this research, the success rate of data transmission depends on several factors. A node can only transmit data if the receiving node (next hop) has enough energy to accept it; otherwise, the packet is dropped. A sender continues to transmit data packets until its buffer becomes empty. If it lacks sufficient energy, the sensor sends packets one by one until it runs out of energy, aiming to make the most of what remains. Only sensors within the sender’s communication range can receive the packets. The transmission process also stops if the receiving node does not have enough buffer space. Since multi-hop communication consumes a significant amount of energy, direct transmission to the MA—when it is within range—is usually much more efficient. IV-C Energy Model Each sensor is initialized with an energy E_{\text{init}} . The energy consumed for transmitting a packet, as given in [ 50 ] , is calculated in Equation ( 2 ). E_{\text{tx}}(k,d)=E_{\text{elec}}\times k+E_{\text{amp}}\times k\times d^{n} (2) where E_{\text{tx}}(k,d) is the energy to transmit a k -bit packet over a distance d . E_{\text{elec}} nJ/bit is the energy used in the transmitter and receiver circuitry, and E_{\text{amp}} \text{pJ/bit/m}^{2} is the energy needed for signal amplification. The parameter n is the path loss exponent, which depends on the propagation environment. In free-space conditions, with no obstructions, the energy loss is a quadratic function ( n=2 ). In obstructed environments, like urban or indoor environments, the signal decays more quickly, and the path loss exponent n>2 . Similarly, the energy consumed for receiving a packet, as given in [ 50 ] , is calculated in Equation ( 3 ). E_{\text{rx}}(k)=E_{\text{elec}}\times k (3) The energy required to charge a sensor at a distance d by the mobile agent, as given in [ 51 ] , is calculated in Equation ( 4 ). E_{\text{charge}}=\eta\times P_{\text{MA}}\times\left(\frac{d_{0}}{d}\right)^{n}\times\Delta t_{\text{charge}} (4) where \eta represents the efficiency factor of energy transfer, indicating that half of the energy effectively reaches the sensors. The term P_{\text{MA}} denotes the rate of energy consumption by the mobile agent during the charging process. The parameter d_{0} is a reference distance, typically assumed to be 1 meter, used to model the path loss in wireless sensor networks. \Delta t_{\text{charge}}=8 seconds is the charging duration. Finally, n represents the path loss exponent, which quantifies how the signal weakens as the distance increases [ 52 ] . IV-E Mobile Agent Model In this study, it is assumed that the mobile agent and all sensor nodes use a single-antenna architecture [ 53 ] . Due to fundamental limitations of single-antenna systems, the mobile agent performs data collection and sensor charging tasks sequentially. When using a single antenna, attempting SWIPT causes severe electromagnetic interference between power and data signals that cannot be effectively separated [ 25 ] . The signal separation required for simultaneous operation would require complex hardware that significantly increases cost and power consumption [ 23 ] . Additionally, attempting to transmit energy and data simultaneously leads to signal collisions and degrades the reliability of both operations, especially in networks with many nodes. To avoid these problems, our approach requires the mobile agent to alternate between data collection and sensor charging tasks. Upon arriving at a grid location, the agent first assesses local conditions and then performs either charging or data collection—never both—before moving to the next location. This sequential operation ensures signal integrity, keeps the system simpler, and improves both energy efficiency and overall reliability. IV-E 1 Service Capacity and Coverage Specifications To address the quantitative requirements for mobile agent service capability, we provide explicit specifications for concurrent coverage and operational parameters. The mobile agent can serve a maximum of M_{\text{max}}=5 sensor nodes simultaneously within its service radius of R_{\text{service}}=10 meters. This limitation ensures manageable power distribution and prevents system overload during energy transfer operations. Each subdomain spans L_{\text{grid}}\times L_{\text{grid}}=20\times 20 m², containing an average of N_{\text{avg}}=4 sensor nodes uniformly distributed. The maximum number of nodes that can be present in a single subdomain is N_{\text{max}}=7 nodes, ensuring the mobile agent can always service all nodes within a subdomain during a single visit. The mobile agent operates under strict timing constraints with charging operations requiring a fixed duration of \Delta t_{\text{charge}}=8 seconds per subdomain, data collection operations lasting \Delta t_{\text{collect}}=6 seconds per subdomain, and movement between adjacent subdomains taking t_{\text{move}}=5 seconds. The charging rate varies with distance and number of active nodes according to: P_{\text{effective}}(d_{i},N_{\text{active}})=\frac{P_{\text{charge}}\times\eta\times(d_{0}/d_{i})^{n}}{N_{\text{active}}} (8) where d_{i} is the distance to node i , P_{\text{charge}}=1.5~\mathrm{J/s} is the charging power at the reference distance d_{0}=1~\mathrm{m} , \eta is the energy transfer efficiency, and the total power is shared among N_{\text{active}} nodes being charged simultaneously. It can charge multiple sensors simultaneously within this radius for a duration of 8 seconds per subdomain, serving up to 5 nodes in a single charging operation.
