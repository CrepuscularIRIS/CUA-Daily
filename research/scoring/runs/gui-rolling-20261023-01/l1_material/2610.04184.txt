title: EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Build Their Own Feedback for Recursive Self-Improvement?
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04184

§Abstract
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) relies on evaluation feedback to assess progress and guide further research, yet repeatedly running complex benchmarks is costly and slows iteration. Human experts reduce this cost by selecting benchmark subsets or designing compact suites. We ask whether AI agents can automate this design process and introduce EvalResearchBench (ERB), a benchmark for autonomous evaluation research. Given target materials, development references, candidate APIs, and fixed time and API budgets, an agent called the researcher selects or synthesizes tasks, implements graders, and revises them in pilot tests before freezing an executable evaluator for coding, co-work, and reasoning. We study 9 researchers and 13 candidate models and compare each frozen evaluator with 14 target benchmarks on score concordance and pairwise agreement. The best evaluators order about 75\% of candidate pairs as the targets do, below the 91\% ceiling set by disagreements among the targets. No researcher leads on every metric, and the best evaluator on development targets is not the best on sealed targets hidden from the researcher. A human-designed sample of public tasks remains a strong baseline, and the evaluator with the lowest recorded execution cost attains the highest pairwise agreement. Agents repair tasks and graders through pilot feedback, yet their evaluators can still truncate answers, exhaust the evaluation budget, or let a few questions dominate a domain score.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) relies on evaluation feedback to determine whether a change improves a system and which direction to explore next. Each new training recipe, dataset, or program creates another version to assess. Repeatedly running complex benchmarks incurs API and compute costs and delays the next experiment, particularly when evaluation requires long tool-using trajectories or specialized environments. Human experts reduce this burden by selecting representative subsets or designing cheaper proxies, as in tinyBenchmarks ( Maia Polo et al., 2024 ) . An autonomous improvement loop would need to perform this design step itself and obtain feedback that is informative enough to guide decisions and cheap enough to repeat. Recent work automates improvement while relying on supplied evaluation protocols. In PostTrainBench, agents choose data and post-training algorithms, but the target benchmark and evaluation harness are fixed ( Rank et al., 2026 ) . RSIBench-Data lets agents revise training-data strategies using checkpoint scores and trajectories from a fixed evaluation service ( Meng et al., 2026 ) . AlphaEvolve evolves programs using a user-provided evaluation function, with optional user-specified test cascades to screen proposals efficiently ( Novikov et al., 2025 ) . In each case, humans decide what to test, how to score it, and how to keep repeated measurement affordable, so extending autonomy to a new domain still requires a human-designed evaluation. Other work automates parts of evaluation design. PACE selects inexpensive source questions and calibrates them against expensive agentic targets ( Song et al., 2026 ) . AutoBencher and BenchAgents automate benchmark construction and refinement ( Li et al., 2025 ; Butt et al., 2025 ) , and BenchBench compares the quality of model-designed suites ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) . None of these settings poses the full research problem, in which an agent chooses a measurement strategy, spends a limited experimental budget, and revises an executable evaluator until it tracks complex target benchmarks. Solving it requires inspecting target evidence, choosing or creating tasks, verifying graders, and deciding whether a pilot failure calls for harder questions, different coverage, a repaired rubric, or more execution budget. We therefore ask whether an agent with fixed time and API budgets can build an evaluator that reproduces how complex target benchmarks score and rank models. We call this capability autonomous evaluation research. To address these gaps, we introduce EvalResearchBench (ERB) , in which the researcher designs the measurement itself, pays for every pilot from a fixed budget, and submits an executable evaluator that is scored against complex target benchmarks. A researcher is a model in an agent harness that designs the evaluator, a candidate is a model being tested, and a target benchmark is an established evaluation whose candidate scores the evaluator should reproduce. The researcher receives target descriptions, permitted tasks and grading resources, execution traces, development reference scores, and APIs for development candidates (Figure 2 ). In pilot tests, it observes candidate responses, costs, and agreement with the visible references, then revises its tasks, graders, weights, and answer allowances. It then freezes an evaluator that returns one score per domain. Sealed candidates and targets stay hidden during research, so the researcher can neither call those candidates nor see those reference scores. Independent assessment runs the frozen evaluator on all candidates under a separate budget and compares its scores with both development and sealed targets. We evaluate 9 researcher configurations, each a model in the Codex or Claude Code harness run for one episode, on 13 open-weight and proprietary candidates and 14 target benchmarks in coding, co-work, and reasoning (Table 1 ). These domains cover the implementation, professional work, and analysis that autonomous research relies on. As a baseline, Random120 samples public tasks under fixed source quotas designed by humans. We measure agreement in two ways. Score is 100 times Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient between measured and reference scores ( Lin, 1989 ) , so it rewards matching score level and spread as well as order. Pairwise agreement (PA) is the fraction of candidate pairs that the evaluator orders as the target does. We also separate the one-time cost of building an evaluator from the cost of running it, which recurs for every new candidate. No evaluator leads in every domain (Figure 1 ). Grok 4.7 leads coding, Astra leads co-work, and Opus 5 leads reasoning, while coding is the weakest domain for most evaluators. Astra attains the highest overall Score and Grok 4.7 the highest PA.
