title: TuringSpace: Grounding Spatial Intelligence in Action with Interactive CAPTCHAs
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25387

§Abstract
Humans act in the visual world by inferring the 3D layout behind a 2D view, predicting how an action changes it, and correcting themselves after seeing the result. Can multimodal large language models (MLLMs) do the same? CAPTCHAs offer a natural test, since humans solve them through this very loop, clicking and dragging on the screen and adjusting after each result. Yet existing CAPTCHA benchmarks are largely solved and rarely spatial, while spatial benchmarks rarely require action. We therefore present TuringSpace, a synthetic web environment of 125 interactive CAPTCHAs that span five spatial abilities and must be solved through clicks and drags on the screen. Every task is a program that generates new instances with a checker and an oracle, so the tasks also form a scalable training gym. Humans solve 94.1% of the tasks, whereas the best model, GPT-6 Astra, still fails 25.9% of them, and the best open-weight model, Kimi K3, fails 68.0%. Our further analysis shows that most failures come from misreading the screenshot, and that model reasoning rarely corrects a wrong belief but hallucinates evidence to defend it. To test whether such spatial abilities can be learned, we fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B on this gym. Its success rate rises from 1.6% to 33.1%, above Kimi K3, and it improves on all seven out-of-distribution benchmarks, including a 9.9-point gain on the real-world indoor videos of VSI-Bench. These results show that grounding spatial intelligence in screen actions exposes what MLLMs lack and offers a scalable way to teach it, with gains that transfer to real-world scenes. We will release TuringSpace to support further study.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Spatial understanding underlies how humans act in the visual world. People can easily recover 3D structure from a flat view, mentally simulate the effect of an intervention, and refine each movement through the visual feedback it produces ( Li et al., 2026b ; Li et al., 2026a ) . Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) ( Anthropic, 2026c ; OpenAI, 2026 ; Google DeepMind, 2026b ; Qwen Team, 2026b ) now perform well on 2D perception but remain far behind humans in 3D spatial understanding, such as imagining a scene from another viewpoint or picturing an object after it rotates ( Cai et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2026 ; Ramakrishnan et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026c ) . CAPTCHAs (Completely Automated Public Turing tests to tell Computers and Humans Apart) need exactly such gaps between humans and machines ( von Ahn et al., 2003 ; von Ahn et al., 2008 ) , so they must move to new abilities once automated agents master the old ones. However, computer-use agents (CUAs) built on MLLM backbones ( Xie et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Anthropic, 2026c ; OpenAI, 2026 ; Qin et al., 2025 ) already solve conventional CAPTCHAs with high success rates ( Kharlamova et al., 2026 ; Teoh et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) ( Fig. 1 a). These puzzles require only simple GUI actions on a single screen, such as typing distorted text, clicking matching image tiles, or dragging a slider. A more promising and challenging direction shifts to building CAPTCHAs on spatial understanding, and recent work has already turned to it. For example, Spatial CAPTCHA ( Kharlamova et al., 2026 ) asks which cube a flat net folds into or what a person in the scene can see, and shows a wide gap between humans and MLLMs. Spatial understanding thus gives CAPTCHAs the gap between humans and machines that conventional puzzles have lost. Despite this progress, current spatial CAPTCHAs ( Kharlamova et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) , like most spatial benchmarks ( Wang et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026c ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Jia et al., 2026 ) , are largely evaluated with multiple-choice questions. Models could guess or rule out options in such a format, so a correct answer may not show that they understand the scene. Where actions are allowed, they become symbolic choices: spatial environments accept only text commands such as “turn left” ( Mayer et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026d ; Gao et al., 2026 ) , and multiple CAPTCHA benchmarks expose the page’s document object model (DOM), so the agent merely picks a labeled element ( Luo et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) . Some CAPTCHA benchmarks do support real GUI interaction ( Chen et al., 2026 ) , but their puzzles have largely been solved by CUAs ( Team et al., 2026b ) . As a result, existing CAPTCHA benchmarks cannot serve as a good testbed for spatial reasoning through a sequence of actions. This limitation matters because spatial reasoning in human cognition is not a one-shot judgment but a loop. People read the 3D scene, predict the outcome of a move, act, and adjust after seeing the result. Actions express every step of this loop far more richly than options ( Hong et al., 2026 ) : a click specifies a location, a drag specifies a direction and a distance, and a sequence of actions specifies how the scene should change. Consider an interactive spatial CAPTCHA that asks the player to move a sofa around a corridor corner. When the next screenshot shows the sofa blocked, the player moves it back and rotates it again. Solving it thus takes several rounds of observing and adjusting. This loop thus grounds spatial understanding in action: each step reflects the agent’s current belief about the scene, and each new observation can correct that belief. To test this loop, we introduce TuringSpace , a synthetic web environment of interactive spatial puzzles. We evaluate models on TuringSpace-Bench , which contains 125 interactive CAPTCHAs that cover five spatial abilities ( Fig. 2 , top). Each task is solved through computer use, where an agent reads screenshots of a real browser and acts with the mouse and keyboard. To guarantee their quality, all tasks are validated by human players. Since a deployed CAPTCHA must synthesize a new puzzle for every visitor so that its answers cannot be memorized, each of our tasks is a program rather than a fixed puzzle. The program can synthesize unlimited fresh instances from random seeds and verify the final state of each. Replaying the solution that each task program computes yields a trajectory that pairs every action with its visual outcome, so the same tasks also form a training environment, TuringSpace-Gym . To test whether such trajectories can teach models the spatial understanding they lack, we fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) on them.
