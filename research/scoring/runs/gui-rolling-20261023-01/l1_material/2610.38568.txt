title: A-RSI: Adaptive Task Refresh for Zero-Data Multimodal Self-Improvement
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38568

§Abstract
Recursive self-improvement requires models not only to acquire useful training experience, but also to decide when their current experience has ceased to be useful. Existing methods often focus on what experience to generate next, while paying less attention to when fresh experience should be introduced. Many also construct training targets through self-consistency or majority voting, making supervision depend on the model's own uncertain predictions. We address these two questions separately: training dynamics determine when to acquire new experience, while the answers to new tasks remain independently checkable. A-RSI monitors reward variation and accuracy in training logs to refresh a task pool when its learning signal declines, without consulting validation or test scores. A static library of model-authored programs produces multimodal questions and derives their reference answers; we specify the task-generation contract, automatically verify chosen properties of the reward and refresh rules, and separately audit the generated answers. The training loop uses no external training examples or human-written answer labels. On the primary eleven-benchmark suite, A-RSI raises the performance average from 57.77 to 61.05 with 800 fresh tasks.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recursive self-improvement depends on both acquiring useful experience and recognizing when the current experience is no longer useful. Recent methods have emphasized what to generate next, such as harder problems or tasks near the model’s estimated competence. Yet a system can continue spending updates on a pool whose learning signal has declined, while refreshing too often consumes new tasks that may not be needed. This makes the timing of replacement a distinct decision from the choice of replacement tasks. The answers attached to self-generated tasks raise a separate reliability question. When training targets come from self-consistency or majority voting, errors in the model’s predictions can become labels for its next update. We therefore separate the source of a refresh decision from the source of answer correctness: training dynamics can indicate when to draw new experience, while the new tasks should have independently checkable answers. We implement this separation in \bm{\mathcal{A}} -RSI (Adaptive Recursive Self-Improvement). In group-based reinforcement learning, prompts whose sampled responses receive different rewards provide nonzero relative-advantage signal. \mathcal{A} -RSI monitors how many distinct prompts retain this signal, together with logged answer accuracy, and refreshes the active pool when the specified training-log condition fires. This is an operational indicator of declining learning signal, not a proof that the pool has been mastered. The controller does not consult validation or test scores. A fixed library of model-written programs supplies the new multimodal tasks. Each program constructs an image and question from sampled parameters and computes a reference answer from the same underlying configuration. We specify this shared-configuration contract, machine-check selected reward and refresh properties, and independently audit generated answers. These checks support the use of program-computed supervision without asserting that every rendered question has been formally proved correct. The loop trains the Solver on the current pool, reads its training logs, and either retains the pool or activates a prepared, unused replacement. A refresh keeps the learned model weights; the task templates and sampling quotas remain fixed. The training stage uses no external training examples, human-written answer labels, or evaluation scores. In the tested Qwen comparisons, difficulty-aware and competence-aware replacement rules do not outperform random sampling under the fixed task-family shares. On the primary eleven-benchmark suite, Qwen improves from 57.77 at the post-initialization checkpoint S_{t} to 61.05 after eight continuation windows and 800 fresh tasks. MiMo improves from 59.08 at its public base model S_{0} to 60.71. On the same eleven-benchmark protocol, \mathcal{A} -RSI scores 61.05 with 800 fresh tasks, while every-window refresh scores 60.64 with 3,200. At the matched sixth continuation window, \mathcal{A} -RSI scores 60.40 against 58.35 for continued training on the old pool. Figure 1 also motivates our response-level audit: a score can rise because more answers finish within the output budget or because already completed answers are corrected. We report these outcomes separately. Our contributions are threefold: • A training-log refresh rule. We formulate a pool-replacement rule using the decline in prompts with within-group reward variation, without consulting evaluation scores. • Programmatically verified experience. Model-written programs instantiate multimodal tasks and compute their reference answers, allowing the training loop to operate without external training examples or human-written answer labels. • Matched comparisons and output-level analysis. We compare refresh policies at matched training progress across full benchmarks and distinguish gains linked to response completion from corrections among completed answers. The Qwen results identify the benefit of the implemented replacement policy and the limits of the timing claim.
