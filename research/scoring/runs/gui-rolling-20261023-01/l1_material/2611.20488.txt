title: EVIDENT: Evaluating Evidence Grounding in GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20488

§Abstract
GUI agents are increasingly evaluated by whether they complete web tasks, but surface success can hide a critical failure: the final answer may not be supported by evidence observed during navigation. We introduce EVIDENT, an evidence-driven benchmark for evaluating whether GUI agents ground their decisions in observable web evidence. EVIDENT contains 220 real-world English and Korean tasks spanning semantic grounding, contextual inference, and visual grounding, each paired with human-curated evidence metadata specifying what must be verified. We further separate surface task completion from evidence-grounded success using three metrics: TSR, ESR, and GSR, which respectively measure outcome success, required evidence observation, and claim-level evidence support. Experiments on commercial and open-source GUI agents reveal substantial gaps between surface success and evidence-grounded success, especially in contextual and visual tasks. These results show that current evaluations can overestimate agent reliability and highlight the need to assess not only whether agents answer, but whether they verify.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in large language models have accelerated the development of GUI agents that can control web browsers and interact with real-world interfaces ( Deng et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2024 ; ByteDance Seed, 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) . In parallel, a growing body of benchmarks has evaluated whether such agents can complete user tasks ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) , understand GUI screens ( Li et al., 2025 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) , or generate reasonable action trajectories ( He et al., 2024 ; Lù et al., 2025 ; Gou et al., 2025 ) . Yet these evaluations leave an important blind spot. They primarily ask whether an agent reaches a plausible outcome, but not whether that outcome is supported by evidence actually observed during web navigation. This distinction is central to evaluating the reliability of GUI agents. Real-world user requests are often underspecified ( Sun et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ) : a user may ask for a child-friendly library space, a compact furniture option for occasional guests, or a travel choice suitable for elderly parents. In such cases, an agent must interpret the user’s intent, form a plausible hypothesis, and verify that hypothesis against the current state of a live website. Parametric knowledge can help generate candidate answers, but relying on it without verification risks hallucinated content ( Huang et al., 2025 ) ; it cannot replace checking observable evidence such as schedules, prices, reviews, product specifications, badges, images, or layout cues. In this sense, web-agent success is not merely a matter of producing the right-looking answer; it is a matter of using the web as an external source of evidence to validate that answer. This raises the central question of this work: when a GUI agent appears to complete a web task, can we verify that its final answer is supported by evidence actually observed during navigation? Figure 1 illustrates this gap. Asked what WebMD says about burnout, an agent produced a confident, authoritative answer: that WebMD recognizes burnout as a real medical condition tied to stress and emotional exhaustion, and offers resources for managing it. Its trajectory, however, shows that the page it actually reached was a generic keyword search returning only tangential hits (e.g., “Diabetes Burnout” and “Parental Burnout”); the asserted statement never appears on screen. The answer is plausible, yet it is reconstructed from the model’s parametric knowledge rather than from evidence observed on the website. Under conventional outcome-based evaluation such an answer may be counted as successful; in a live web environment, where content changes and the specific on-screen evidence matters, it is better understood as a hidden failure ( Arman et al., 2026 ) . To study this problem, we introduce evident , an evidence-driven benchmark for GUI-agent evaluation. evident contains 220 real-world user scenarios in English and Korean. Each task is paired with human-curated evidence metadata that specifies what information must be checked to justify a reliable answer. Rather than evaluating only whether an agent reaches a final answer, evident asks whether the answer can be traced back to evidence observed along the trajectory. The core idea of evident is to separate surface success from evidence-grounded success. We distinguish whether the final answer appears to complete the task, whether the agent observed the necessary evidence during navigation, and whether the final answer’s core claims are supported by that evidence. This decomposition allows us to identify cases where an agent produces a successful-looking answer while skipping required evidence, or where it observes relevant evidence but fails to ground its final claims in it. Our experiments show that current GUI agents exhibit a substantial gap between surface success and evidence-grounded success. Even strong commercial agents often achieve high outcome-level success, but their performance drops sharply when we require evidence observation and claim-level grounding. The gap is especially pronounced when agents must reason over implicit user constraints or non-textual visual cues. These findings suggest that existing success metrics can substantially overestimate the reliability of GUI agents, and that future evaluation should measure not only whether agents navigate to plausible answers, but whether those answers are grounded in observable web evidence.
