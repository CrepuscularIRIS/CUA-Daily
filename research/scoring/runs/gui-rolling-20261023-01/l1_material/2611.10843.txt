title: Agents of Chaos: A Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) on Malicious Attacks on Agentic AI Systems
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10843

§Abstract
Agentic AI systems extend large language models with tools, memory, retrieval, browser or computer-use interfaces, code execution, external services, and multi-agent coordination. Although these capabilities enable agents to act beyond text generation and perform tasks, they also create attack paths through which adversaries can exploit untrusted content, deceptive metadata, poisoned state, compromised skills, mutable dependencies, or malicious code. Such attacks can manipulate planning and tool use, corrupt memory, abuse code-execution environments, and trigger harmful downstream effects. In this paper, we present a systematic review of malicious attacks targeting agentic AI systems. We screened 186 candidate sources and retained 98. Following independent coding and resolver adjudication, we identified 284 atomic attacks, 452 atomic safeguards, and 660 explicit attack--safeguard relationships. We organize attacks into eight causal-mechanism classes while retaining insertion points, activation conditions, abused capabilities, objectives, impacts, persistence, and evidence as orthogonal dimensions. Our analysis shows that tool, MCP, and action-layer attacks account for the largest share of atomic attacks, while the prompt-injection and action-hijacking class has the broadest paper coverage. We identify distinct class-specific activation patterns and separation between insertion and later activation. Safeguard coverage remains uneven: only 278 of 660 relationships are directly assessed, and strong coverage is uncommon. These findings support lifecycle-aware threat modeling, agent evaluation, tool governance, and defense-in-depth security for agentic AI systems.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Modern agentic AI systems are no longer passive text generators. Unlike standalone language models, which primarily receive an input and produce an output, agentic AI systems can observe external content, reason over intermediate state, choose actions, and cause side effects outside the model [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . To do so, agentic AI systems extend large language models with tools, memory, retrieval, browser and computer-use interfaces, code execution, external services, and multi-agent coordination [ 3 , 4 , 1 ] . These capabilities expand the security boundary beyond the model to workflows connecting instructions, data, tools, memory, artifacts, permissions, and external services [ 2 , 4 , 3 ] . This expanded perimeter introduces attack surfaces that differ substantially from those of standalone models, partly because input channels can serve as injection vectors [ 5 , 2 , 3 ] . For example, malicious instructions embedded in webpages, emails, documents, tool responses, retrieved passages, memory entries, or skill files can influence how an agent plans and acts [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . Deceptive tool descriptions or malicious Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers can bias tool selection and parameter generation [ 9 , 3 , 4 ] . Poisoned memory entries or retrieval content can persist across sessions and influence future decisions [ 10 , 11 ] . Malicious skills, dependencies, or installers can cause agents to access secrets, execute commands, modify files, or communicate with external endpoints [ 8 , 7 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] . In multi-agent settings, compromised instructions or outputs can propagate through communication channels and collaborative workflows [ 15 , 16 ] . As in software supply chains, reusable artifacts such as agent skills, plugins, MCP servers, prompts, workflows, datasets, model components, configuration files, and dependencies can deliver malicious content across trust boundaries before integration into agentic systems [ 13 , 14 , 8 , 7 ] . However, the central security challenge extends beyond artifact provenance to how untrusted content and compromised components influence agent reasoning, memory, tool use, permissions, and downstream effects. Research on these attacks remains fragmented. Recent surveys and SoKs cover agent design, attack surfaces, threat models, defenses, evaluation, benchmarks, governance, and deployment [ 2 , 3 , 4 ] . However, they do not provide a focused, cross-mechanism systematization of malicious attack vectors and corresponding defenses. This gap motivates the following three research questions in this paper: • RQ1: What is a comprehensive list of malicious attack vectors targeting agentic AI? • RQ2: What patterns emerge across documented malicious attack vectors? • RQ3: Which safeguards address each attack class, and where do significant coverage gaps remain? To answer these questions, we conducted a systematic review of peer-reviewed publications, preprints, platform advisories, repository disclosures, incident reports, and other technically attributable grey literature. Our final analytical corpus comprises 98 sources. Following independent coding and resolver adjudication, we identified 284 atomic attacks, 452 atomic safeguards, and 660 explicit source-supported attack–safeguard relationships. Our primary analytical unit is the atomic attack, not a source quotation or broad thematic vector. We distinguish attacks when their causal mechanism or attack surface differs materially, or when differences in access, delivery, insertion, activation, or an indispensable target define distinct attack processes. Each attack receives one primary causal class, while target, delivery, insertion, activation, capability, objective, impact, persistence, and evidence remain orthogonal dimensions. For RQ2, we analyze relationships among insertion point, activation condition, abused capability, objective, impact, and persistence. For RQ3, we treat safeguards as atomic controls and separately record their control type, evaluation status, origin, and enforcement target. We retain only explicit source-supported attack–safeguard relationships and distinguish whether each relationship was directly assessed and, when evaluated, whether its coverage was strong, partial, or limited. The resulting taxonomy and safeguard mapping offer a mechanism-based view of agentic-AI attacks and reveal gaps in defensive evidence.

## III Methodology
Our methodology comprises eight sequential stages, as illustrated in Figure 1 : source discovery, eligibility screening, data extraction, evidence grading, normalization and structural adjudication, taxonomy construction, safeguard coding, and consistency review. We considered peer-reviewed literature and technically attributable grey literature to capture both archival studies and early disclosures of agentic-AI attacks. A source was eligible if it (1) described an agentic AI system, component, workflow, artifact, or related security mechanism; (2) reported an attack that affected, or was designed to affect, agentic behavior or downstream actions; and (3) provided sufficient technical detail to identify a concrete attack path, security-relevant effect, or safeguard. We included attacks that targeted an agentic workflow and produced, or were designed to produce, a security-relevant effect beyond an undesirable text response. We excluded ordinary model errors, hallucinations, bias, general performance degradation, direct adversarial examples without downstream agentic action, and speculative discussions that did not specify a concrete attack path or security impact. The literature collection was bounded by a July 2026 cutoff. Sources published or disclosed after this cutoff were not included in the analytical corpus. Google Scholar and arXiv served as the primary discovery interfaces. We supplemented these searches with backward and forward snowballing, targeted venue searches, and technically attributable grey literature, including platform disclosures, repository reports, security advisories, vulnerability records, and incident reports. Because terminology varies across prompt injection, tool and MCP attacks, memory and RAG poisoning, multi-agent security, backdoors, malicious skills, and AI supply-chain security, we used multiple query families rather than a single search string. III-A Normalization, Atomicity, and Deduplication Different sources often use different terms for similar mechanisms, while the same study may describe multiple payloads, experimental settings, objectives, or downstream effects. We therefore distinguish source evidence from analytical records. Quotations, highlighted passages, benchmark results, and source descriptions provide evidence for coding but are not themselves counting units. Our primary attack unit is an atomic attack record . We split two observations into distinct attacks only when their causal mechanism or attack surface materially differs, or when differences in attacker access, delivery, insertion point, activation condition, or an indispensable target define a different attack process. Differences only in terminology, prompt wording, victim model, experimental configuration, objective, or downstream impact do not by themselves justify separate attack records. For example, malicious instructions delivered through webpages, emails, documents, or tool outputs may represent the same prompt-injection mechanism when they exploit the same instruction–data boundary. Conversely, two attacks delivered through the same artifact are treated as distinct when one depends on semantic instruction following and the other depends on executable code, package installation, or malicious runtime behavior. We apply the same atomicity principle to safeguards. A safeguard is counted separately when the source presents it as a distinct control mechanism or independently evaluates it as a defense baseline. Internal implementation stages are not automatically counted as separate safeguards unless they enforce causally distinct security boundaries. After structural adjudication, the final analytical dataset contains 284 atomic attacks and 452 atomic safeguards. III-B Taxonomy Construction We constructed the taxonomy iteratively through bottom-up mechanism coding and top-down consistency review. During bottom-up analysis, we extracted the causal process through which each attack influences an agentic system. During top-down analysis, we grouped related mechanisms into eight primary attack classes. Each atomic attack receives exactly one primary class based on its decisive causal mechanism, while carriers, target components, abused capabilities, objectives, and impacts remain orthogonal dimensions. Primary assignments are mutually exclusive, although mechanisms may overlap within compound incidents. The final taxonomy contains eight classes: (1) prompt injection and action hijacking; (2) tool, MCP, and action-layer attacks; (3) memory and RAG poisoning; (4) multi-agent attacks; (5) backdoors and trigger-conditioned compromise; (6) computer-use and coding-agent attacks; (7) agent skills and supply-chain attacks; and (8) goal, task, and planning manipulation. To distinguish overlapping mechanisms, C1 captures instruction–data boundary violations, whereas C2 applies when manipulation of tool or MCP interfaces is the decisive mechanism. C3 is assigned when poisoning persistent memory or retrieval state is indispensable to the attack, rather than merely serving as a delivery channel for instructions. C8 captures manipulation of goal selection, task state, or planning logic, rather than ordinary instruction-following hijacking under C1. Similarly, C4 requires agent-to-agent interaction or propagation to be central to the compromise, rather than merely occurring in a multi-agent setting. C5 applies when an implanted backdoor is activated by a trigger, even if the resulting behavior involves tool use; C2 applies when the tool or MCP interface itself is manipulated. C6 captures attacks whose decisive mechanism exploits computer-use or coding-agent execution, whereas C7 applies when compromise through a reusable skill, package, dependency, or other supply-chain artifact is essential to the attack. For compound incidents, structural adjudication first determines whether the evidence supports one causal attack process or multiple distinct atomic attacks under Section 3.1. Crossing multiple lifecycle stages or producing several impacts does not alone justify splitting. Each resulting atomic record receives one primary causal class. For the cross-cutting analysis in Section 5, we additionally code target component, attacker access, delivery channel, insertion point, activation condition, abused capability, attacker objective, downstream impact, persistence or propagation, evidence context, evidence level, and evidence type. The complete set of attack, evidence, safeguard, and relationship-level coding dimensions is summarized in the final codebook in Section III-D . III-C Safeguard Coding and Coverage Assessment We extracted safeguards from attack studies, empirical evaluations, system designs, benchmark papers, governance proposals, and dedicated defense papers, and classified them into five control types. First, preventive controls seek to block attack insertion or unsafe selection through provenance checks, tool allowlists, input validation, artifact signing, and permission design. Second, detective controls identify suspicious content or behavior through prompt-injection detectors, scanner-based review, anomaly detection, runtime tracing, and tool-call inspection. Third, containment controls limit the effects of successful attacks through sandboxing, least privilege, network restrictions, scoped credentials, and human approval for sensitive actions. Fourth, corrective controls remove or repair compromised state through memory cleanup, dependency rollback, poisoned-corpus removal, and agent replacement. Finally, recovery controls support post-incident response through logging, revocation, downstream notification, audit trails, and incident response procedures. For RQ3, safeguards are analyzed as atomic control records. In addition to control type, we code each safeguard’s status, origin, and direct enforcement target. We evaluate coverage at the level of explicit attack–safeguard relationships rather than assuming that a safeguard applies uniformly to every attack in the same class. A relationship is retained only when the source explicitly links the safeguard to the attack mechanism or when a direct defense experiment establishes that relationship. For each explicit relationship, we record whether the safeguard was directly assessed against the corresponding attack.
