arxiv: 2610.20951 (2610.20951v1)
title: LEV: Thinking Fast and Deep with Looped Decision Models
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20951

§Abstract
Decision models answer typed questions about an input with one probability per option, computed in a single forward pass without generating text. Like the fast System 1 of dual-process theory, they answer at once, but their depth is fixed at training time, so they spend the same computation on easy and hard questions. Models that reason in generated text can think longer, but they pay for every extra step in decoded tokens. We present LEV, a decision model that thinks deeper by rerunning its layers in a loop instead of generating tokens. LEV builds on a language model pretrained as a loop, reads the options after every loop step and trains all steps jointly, so one checkpoint can answer at any depth. On three looped backbones, accuracy rises with depth on the same weights, mostly on hard questions, and a 2.6B LEV goes from 63.2% at depth one to 76.2% at depth four on JevBench. On-demand depth, set per question by a small stopper with a threshold fitted on unlabelled requests, saves 15% to 24% of the computation at a cost of under one accuracy point, level with or slightly above a fixed mix of depths. On a locked test set, the 1.4B and 3B models are 5.4 to 8.8 points more accurate than dense (non-looped) models and cascades of the same latency below 50 ms.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Generative language models that reason with chain-of-thought act as a System 2 ( Kahneman, 2011 ) . They write out their reasoning before choosing an option, so every decision is slow, even an easy one ( Villalón, 2026 ; razorback16, 2026 ) , and turning the reasoning off lowers their accuracy markedly on JevBench ( Standhartinger, 2026 ) . Decision models such as Jev ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) and Kev ( Palmer, 2026 ) are a System 1 for questions with a fixed set of options. They return a probability for each option from one forward pass ( Devlin et al., 2019 ; Robinson et al., 2023 ) in tens of milliseconds, but they spend the same computation on every question and cannot think longer on a hard one. Not every question needs more computation. On the public JevBench items, Kev-4B answers 28 more items correctly than Kev-0.8B, and Fig. 2 a shows that all of them come from the standard and hard tiers, because both models already answer every easy item. A decision model that could add computation only where it pays off would keep the speed of the small model on easy questions and approach the accuracy of the large one on hard questions. Existing ways to spend more computation without generating text fall short for decision models ( Section 3 ). A cascade passes uncertain questions to a larger model ( Varshney & Baral, 2022 ; Chen et al., 2024 ) , but reads them again and needs a second model. Early exit stops at a confident intermediate layer ( Xin et al., 2020 ; Schuster et al., 2022 ; Elhoushi et al., 2024 ) , but these layers are not trained to be read and are often confidently wrong on new data ( Meronen et al., 2024 ) . A loop reruns the same layers without adding parameters ( Dehghani et al., 2019 ; Geiping et al., 2025 ) , but added to a pretrained model it barely helps, as Jev-LCT ( Cao, 2026 ) reports, unless it gets far more continued pretraining than a decision model is trained on ( McLeish et al., 2025 ; Koishekenov et al., 2025 ) . We propose LEV, a System 1.5 decision model ( Wang et al., 2025 ) that thinks in loops rather than tokens. LEV builds on natively looped language models ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Nanbeige Lab, 2026 ) , whose loop is trained in pretraining rather than added afterwards. Decision training therefore does not have to teach the model to use its loop, whereas a loop added to a pretrained decision model stays unused ( Section 3 ). A shared readout scores the options after each step and all steps are trained jointly, so one set of weights answers at any depth, and a further step resumes from the previous hidden states without reading the input again. A lightweight stopper sets the depth of each question on demand by predicting whether its answer already agrees with the deepest one, with a threshold set on unlabelled requests. We use this target because the usual early-exit rule, a confidence threshold set on labelled data, does not beat a fixed mix of depths here, as shallow readouts are overconfident on new data. Since the target is the model’s own deepest answer, a deployment can fit the stopper on its own traffic without labels. We build LEV on three looped backbones and evaluate it on JevBench and two held-out benchmarks. Accuracy rises with depth on the same weights, mostly on hard questions ( Fig. 1 c), and the stopper spends more steps on harder questions without seeing their difficulty ( Fig. 1 a). On-demand depth saves 15% to 24% of the computation at a cost of under one accuracy point, level with or slightly above a fixed mix of depths. A locked test, read once after the recipe and the stopper were fixed, confirms that on-demand depth stays within one point of the deepest readout. Below 50 ms, the 1.4B and 3B models are 5.4 to 8.8 points more accurate than dense (non-looped) models and cascades of the same latency ( Fig. 1 b). Our contributions are as follows. • We propose LEV, a System 1.5 decision model that thinks deeper on hard questions through loop steps rather than generated text. It reads the options after every step of a natively looped backbone, so one checkpoint serves every depth. • We show that a stopper whose threshold is set on unlabelled requests gives on-demand depth without labels. It predicts agreement with the deepest answer rather than correctness, and it is level with or above a fixed mix of depths where confidence thresholds set on labelled data fall below it. • On three looped backbones, LEV gains most from depth on hard questions, and below 50 ms it is more accurate than dense models and cascades of the same latency. We also build LevBench, a benchmark frozen before any model read it, whose tiers move step by step away from the training data.
