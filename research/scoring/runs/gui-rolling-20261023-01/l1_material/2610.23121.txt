title: Scene-Designer: Learning Design Intelligence for Agentic 3D Scene Generation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23121

§Abstract
Generating complete, coherent, and usable 3D indoor scenes from natural language requires strong \textbf{design intelligence}: the ability to design high-quality scenes and critique their quality. However, existing approaches either rely on general-purpose models lacking domain-specific design experience or learn from static scenes. In this work, we introduce \textbf{Scene-Designer}, an agentic framework that learns design intelligence through complementary design and critique experiences. We construct two datasets: \textbf{Scene-OTA-35K}, which provides Observation--Thought--Action supervision for learning scene design and efficient error correction, and \textbf{Scene-OTC-15K}, which provides Observation--Thought--Critic supervision for learning scene quality critique across physical validity, functional correctness, and layout coherence. Based on these experiences, we propose \textbf{Scene-GRPO}, a Critic-guided GRPO framework that improves design decisions and reduces unnecessary trial-and-error corrections. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Scene-Designer achieves superior performance in physical validity, functional correctness, and layout coherence, while the learned Scene-Critic significantly outperforms general-purpose models in scene critique. Moreover, test-time Critic guidance further improves scene generation, showing that the Critic can provide useful guidance beyond training. Additional analysis suggests a trend of mutual enhancement between scene design and critique abilities.

“The clearer one’s understanding becomes, the more steadfastly one acts; the more steadfastly one acts, the clearer one’s understanding becomes.” — Zhu Xi, Zhuzi yulei, vol. 14. Automatically generating complete, coherent, and usable 3D scenes from natural-language instructions is becoming increasingly important with the rapid progress of embodied AI, automated spatial design, virtual content creation, and related applications. At its core, high-quality scene generation is not merely a problem of placing objects, but a problem of design. This calls for a model to reason both about how a scene should take shape and about the quality of the resulting scene. We refer to this capability as Design Intelligence . We define design intelligence as the ability to both design a scene and critique its quality. These two capabilities correspond to two essential stages of the design process: making design decisions and evaluating their quality. Scene design concerns constructing physically valid, functionally correct, and layout-coherent scenes, while efficiently correcting errors without unnecessary trial and error when problems arise. Scene critique focuses on assessing scene quality along the same dimensions and distinguishing better designs from poorer ones. These two capabilities are closely connected, since effective design requires reliable critique to guide construction, while meaningful critique requires an understanding of what constitutes good scene design. However, even the strongest general-purpose models still exhibit clear limitations in both capabilities, as revealed by the following diagnostic studies. Challenge 1: Inefficient correction caused by unnecessary trial and error. Error correction is an essential part of scene design, but effective correction should avoid unnecessary trial and error. When the solution is straightforward, a capable model should correct the error directly. To examine this behavior, we analyze the generation trajectories of GPT-5.2 with a strong scene-construction scaffold. We categorize the observed construction errors into two major groups: physical errors and semantic and layout errors , each containing several fine-grained error types, as shown in Fig. 2 . Our analysis of 100 generated scenes shows that GPT-5.2 can identify many design errors, yet often performs a sequence of unnecessary corrective attempts before reaching an effective solution. This behavior is particularly evident for errors that could be resolved with only one or two simple operations. For example, a window accessibility blockage can usually be corrected by directly moving the obstructing object, yet approximately 18% of such ases still require more than five corrective moves. Similar patterns appear across multiple error categories. These results indicate that the main limitation is not merely error recognition, but the lack of design experience needed to avoid unnecessary trial and error and perform efficient correction. Challenge 2: Insufficient scene critique ability. Scene critique is another essential component of design intelligence. Reliable critique requires more than recognizing obvious violations of physical validity ; it must also capture whether a scene is functionally correct and whether its overall layout is coherent. To examine this capability, we compare GPT-5.2 with our domain-trained model on the Scene-OTC test sets across these three dimensions. As shown in Fig. 3 , GPT-5.2 achieves around 72% accuracy in pairwise scene comparison, but its performance drops to around 50% in the more challenging four-scene preference ranking. In contrast, our model consistently achieves higher accuracy across all three dimensions. Fig. 3 further illustrates a representative failure case. Although Scene A forms a more coherent dining area by grouping functionally related furniture, GPT-5.2 prefers Scene B because of its more open central space, overlooking the stronger functional organization and layout coherence of Scene A. Together, these results suggest that general-purpose models lack the scene-specific critique experience needed to reliably distinguish scene quality across physical validity, functional correctness, and layout coherence. These challenges cannot be directly addressed by existing scene data alone. Existing scene datasets, such as 3D-FRONT Fu et al. (2021) and 3D-SynthPlace Yang et al. (2026b) , mainly provide static scene states with object poses, but do not capture experience from the scene design process and critique. For scene design, construction trajectories provide richer supervision, but raw trajectories often contain substantial unnecessary trial and error, reflecting the exploration process rather than effective design strategies. Extracting useful construction and correction decisions from such trajectories is therefore essential for obtaining high-quality design experience. For scene critique, the challenge is different.
