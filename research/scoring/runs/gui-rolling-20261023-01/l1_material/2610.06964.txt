title: Principles that Guide, Actions that Inform: Agent Evolution via Knowledge Abstraction
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06964

§Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents have demonstrated strong capabilities in interactive environments, yet their ability to continually evolve from experience remains limited. Although fine-tuning enables adaptation, its dependence on parameter access and high computational costs restrict its flexibility, especially for large-scale and closed-source LLMs. External memory offers an alternative by allowing agents to accumulate experience without modifying model parameters. However, existing methods mainly focus on experience representation and organization, while the acquired knowledge remains tightly coupled with specific tasks and contexts, limiting generalization. A key challenge is how to transform concrete interactions into abstract and reusable knowledge that guides future decisions beyond individual experiences. To address this challenge, we propose SAGA (\underline{\textbf{S}}elf-evolving \underline{\textbf{A}}gents through Experience-\underline{\textbf{G}}rounded \underline{\textbf{A}}bstraction), a framework for experience-grounded knowledge abstraction and utilization in LLM agents. SAGA progressively transforms interaction trajectories into episodic descriptions, reusable procedures, and principles with explicit applicability conditions, while maintaining links to execution evidence. Retrieved principles are instantiated into task-specific guidance and used to refine candidate actions through corrective feedback and resampling. This creates an execution--abstraction feedback loop, where accumulated knowledge guides future interactions and new experiences continuously update hierarchical memory. Experiments on ScienceWorld and ALFWorld demonstrate improved task performance, with ablation studies highlighting the importance of contextual instantiation and action regulation for leveraging principle-level knowledge.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has enabled a new paradigm of autonomous agents, where pretrained models are augmented with external tools, memory modules, and interaction interfaces for complex reasoning and decision-making tasks. Recent LLM-based agents have demonstrated promising capabilities in diverse interactive environments, including embodied systems ( Brohan et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) , text-based environments such as ALFWorld and ScienceWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2022 ) , and emerging computer-use scenarios. However, existing agents largely rely on the capabilities acquired during pretraining and lack mechanisms for continual adaptation after deployment. A fundamental challenge toward more capable agents is therefore enabling self-evolution, where agents can learn from interaction experiences, refine their behaviors, and gradually acquire task-specific or user-specific knowledge beyond the original foundation model. Recent studies have explored several directions toward self-evolving agents. One direction adapts the model itself through supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, enabling direct modification of agent behaviors but requiring substantial computational resources and access to model parameters ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Schulman et al., 2017 ) . Another direction performs adaptation at inference time through reasoning, reflection, and self-improvement mechanisms without updating model parameters ( Yao et al., 2022 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ) . Recent works further investigate experience-driven adaptation by extracting reusable skills or strategies from previous interactions ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ; Xue et al., 2026 ) . Among these approaches, external memory provides an effective mechanism for continual adaptation of LLM agents. Existing memory systems study persistent memory management ( Packer et al., 2023 ; Zhong et al., 2024 ) , structured memory representations ( Gutiérrez et al., 2024 ; Rasmussen et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2026 ) , and adaptive memory evolution ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Fan et al., 2026 ; Cao et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) . These methods demonstrate the importance of memory as an interface between foundation models and evolving environments. Despite recent progress in agent memory systems, existing approaches primarily focus on storing and retrieving information from past interactions. Memory architectures such as MemGPT and MemoryBank maintain persistent interaction histories for long-term context reuse ( Packer et al., 2023 ; Zhong et al., 2024 ) , while retrieval-based methods further improve access to stored experiences through structured memory mechanisms ( Gutiérrez et al., 2024 ) . However, these memories are typically represented as episodic records, retrieved contexts, or task-specific procedures, providing limited support for extracting transferable knowledge across tasks and environments. Recent experience-driven approaches, including Reflexion, ExpeL, and Voyager, begin to explore how accumulated experiences can improve future agent behavior through reflection, skill acquisition, and experience reuse ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) . Nevertheless, how to systematically transform interaction experiences into higher-level, experience-grounded knowledge that captures reusable principles and applicability conditions remains a fundamental challenge for lifelong adaptation of LLM-based agents ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026c ) . To address this challenge, we propose SAGA ( S elf-evolving A gents through Experience- G rounded A bstraction), a framework for experience-driven agent adaptation through evidence-linked knowledge abstraction and utilization. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , SAGA establishes an execution–abstraction feedback loop: accumulated knowledge guides subsequent interactions, while newly collected trajectories provide evidence for updating the knowledge representation. The framework organizes experiences into four abstraction levels, from raw trajectories and episodic descriptions to reusable procedures and principles with explicit applicability conditions. Links between adjacent levels preserve the connection between abstract knowledge and its supporting execution evidence. For knowledge utilization, SAGA instantiates retrieved principles into operational guidance conditioned on the current task and environmental context. Principle-derived checks further regulate candidate actions through corrective feedback and bounded resampling. These mechanisms connect knowledge abstraction with both task-level guidance and execution-time action refinement. We evaluate SAGA on ScienceWorld ( Wang et al., 2022 ) and ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2020 ) under memory-based and parameter-based adaptation settings.
