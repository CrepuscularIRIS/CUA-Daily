title: RoboCasa-Shift: Stage-Aware Robustness Evaluation of Vision-Language-Action Policies in Long-Horizon Household Manipulation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27861

§Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) robustness is commonly summarized by aggregate task success, which obscures how individual executions change under distribution shifts and where failures emerge in long-horizon tasks. We introduce \textbf{RoboCasa-Shift}, a diagnostic robustness benchmark built on RoboCasa365 that combines controlled single-factor shifts, matched clean-perturbed rollouts in which each pair shares the same underlying settings and differs only in the target shift, and simulator-grounded stage-aware diagnostics. We evaluate GR00T N1.5 on four long-horizon tasks that pass a predefined clean-performance gate under four perturbation factors and three calibrated severity levels, including conditions that drive the evaluated policy to complete task failure, totaling 1,040 formal rollouts. Robustness responses vary substantially across factors and tasks, and increasing severity does not always produce monotonic degradation. For example, one camera condition yields $Δ\mathrm{SR}=0$ despite 8 of 80 matched episodes changing outcome. Stage-aware analysis further shows that the same end-to-end degradation can correspond to different execution-depth profiles. Camera $L_2$ and Gaussian blur $L_1$ both yield $Δ\mathrm{SR}=0.188$, yet their first-stage failure rates are 19.0\% and 36.4\%, respectively. Our findings suggest that VLA robustness should be viewed as a multi-dimensional property of execution rather than a single end-to-end performance measure, motivating evaluation protocols that jointly capture outcome degradation, paired outcome sensitivity, and failure progression. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/HaochengWang23/RoboCasa-Shift.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have become an increasingly important paradigm for robotic manipulation by integrating visual observations, language instructions, and robot actions within unified policy frameworks [ 1 , 2 ] . Despite their growing capabilities, maintaining reliable behavior under distribution shifts remains a key challenge for deployment, as robots may encounter variations in observations, environments, and physical states that differ from those seen during training. Systematically evaluating VLA robustness under such distribution shifts is important for understanding the reliability of these models in robotic applications. Recent robustness benchmarks have expanded the evaluation of VLA policies beyond nominal task settings through diverse controlled distribution shifts and increasingly systematic evaluation protocols [ 3 , 4 , 5 ] . Despite this broader coverage, robustness is still commonly summarized at the task or evaluation-condition level through aggregate metrics such as success rate. Such summaries do not reveal how individual executions change under a distribution shift or where failures emerge within long-horizon task execution, leaving a need for more diagnostic evaluation at the execution level. Such diagnosis requires controlling episode-level variation for meaningful comparison, since clean and shifted evaluations with different task instances or initial conditions may confound episode difficulty with the target distribution shift. We therefore compare each perturbed execution with a corresponding clean execution under the same underlying task conditions, changing only the target perturbation. This counterfactual pairing provides a controlled basis for identifying episode-level outcome changes associated with the evaluated shift. Even under such controlled comparisons, a binary task outcome provides only a coarse description of failure in long-horizon manipulation. These tasks unfold through a sequence of semantically meaningful subgoals [ 6 , 7 ] , so two failed executions may terminate after very different amounts of task progress. Stage-aware evaluation can therefore complement final success or failure by tracking semantic progress and identifying the first incomplete subgoal, providing a clearer view of where robustness failures emerge during execution. We introduce RoboCasa-Shift, a diagnostic robustness benchmark built on RoboCasa365 [ 3 ] for evaluating VLA policies under controlled distribution shifts in long-horizon household manipulation, as illustrated in Figure 1 . The benchmark defines controlled single-factor shifts at multiple frozen severity levels and a matched clean-perturbed protocol for episode-level counterfactual comparison. It further incorporates simulator-grounded stage-aware diagnostics to track semantic execution progress and localize where failures emerge during long-horizon task execution. RoboCasa-Shift characterizes robustness from complementary perspectives, including end-to-end task outcomes, episode-level outcome changes, and stage-aware execution behavior. Our evaluation reveals that robustness responses vary across perturbation factors and tasks, and that increasing calibrated severity does not always lead to monotonic degradation in end-to-end performance. Aggregate success rates can also mask considerable episode-level outcome changes, while similar end-to-end degradation can correspond to failures emerging at different semantic depths of task execution. Stage-aware analysis further shows that perturbation severity can alter the semantic depth reached by failed executions, with stronger shifts in some conditions associated with earlier incomplete milestones. The main contributions of this work are as follows: • We develop RoboCasa-Shift , a diagnostic robustness benchmark built on RoboCasa365 for long-horizon household manipulation. The benchmark defines controlled single-factor distribution shifts at multiple frozen severity levels under an episode-level clean-perturbed counterfactual protocol, enabling robustness changes to be examined under matched task conditions. • We introduce a simulator-grounded stage-aware robustness diagnostic that complements final task success with semantic execution information. Long-horizon tasks are represented through ordered semantic subgoals evaluated by simulator-state predicates, allowing execution progress and the first incomplete subgoal to be tracked for failure localization. • We conduct a controlled robustness study on four clean-qualified long-horizon RoboCasa tasks across four perturbation factors and three severity levels, jointly analyzing end-to-end task outcomes, counterfactual episode-level changes, and stage-aware execution depth. The analysis reveals robustness behaviors not captured by aggregate success alone, including heterogeneous severity responses and differences in the semantic depth reached by failed executions.

III-B Controlled Distribution Shifts RoboCasa-Shift considers four representative single-factor shifts that preserve the task objective while perturbing distinct sources of variation in household manipulation. Following robustness dimensions explored in recent VLA manipulation benchmarks [ 3 , 4 , 17 ] , we evaluate illumination, third-person camera orientation, robot base initialization, and Gaussian blur. For L_{1} – L_{3} , illumination scales simulator light intensity by \alpha=\{0.8,0.6,0.4\} ; camera orientation samples third-person yaw/pitch offsets within \theta_{\max}=\{2^{\circ},6^{\circ},10^{\circ}\} while preserving the wrist view; base initialization uses \sigma_{xy}=\{0.02,0.05,0.10\} m and \sigma_{\psi}=\{2^{\circ},5^{\circ},10^{\circ}\} for planar position and yaw; and Gaussian blur applies \sigma=\{1,2,4\} to the RGB observations used by the policy. Implementation details and severity calibration procedures are provided in Appendix VI-B . Severity levels are calibrated before formal evaluation through model-free rendering and reset-state validity checks and are frozen independently of policy success. For camera and base shifts, the same factor-specific random draw is reused across L_{1} – L_{3} for each base episode, so severity changes only the perturbation magnitude; illumination and blur are deterministic. The levels are ordinal within each factor, and equal level indices across different factors do not imply physically equivalent perturbation magnitudes. III-C Counterfactual Paired Evaluation Protocol To isolate changes associated with a distribution shift from the inherent variability of RoboCasa episodes, we adopt a paired counterfactual evaluation protocol. For each base episode e_{i} , we construct a clean rollout E_{i}^{\mathrm{clean}} and its perturbed counterpart E_{i}^{p,s} , where p denotes the perturbation factor and s its severity. The paired rollouts share the same task and instruction, scene and object configuration, initial simulator state, episode horizon, and policy. For stochastic policies, the same policy-side random seed is initialized for both rollouts in a pair and is not reset at every action step. The target perturbation is therefore the only controlled difference between E_{i}^{\mathrm{clean}} and E_{i}^{p,s} , enabling direct comparison under otherwise matched conditions. We separately validate environment-state identity and policy-side repeatability, with the results summarized in Table II . Implementation details and pairing integrity audits are provided in Appendix VI-C . III-D Subgoal-Aware Failure Tracking Episode-level success indicates whether a task is ultimately completed, but does not reveal how far the policy progresses before failure. We therefore represent each long-horizon task as an ordered sequence of semantic subgoals, \mathcal{G}=(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{K}) , derived from the task logic and instruction. Each subgoal is associated with a simulator-state predicate that determines whether the corresponding task condition has been satisfied, such as an object reaching a target receptacle or a fixture entering a required state. This provides a simulator-grounded diagnostic of execution progress without requiring external stage annotations or model-predicted labels. Once satisfied, the subgoal is marked as completed, its completion step is recorded, and the tracker advances to the next one. The tracker also records the first incomplete subgoal. Completed subgoals are latched rather than re-evaluated later, so the resulting trajectory represents the deepest semantic milestone reached during execution rather than the set of conditions satisfied at the terminal state. StageTracker therefore complements, rather than replaces, RoboCasa’s official task-success criterion by distinguishing failed executions according to their semantic progress. III-E Stage-Aware Robustness Measures RoboCasa’s official task-success criterion remains the primary end-to-end outcome, while StageTracker provides complementary measures of execution depth. For a task with K ordered subgoals, let m_{i} denote how many are sequentially completed in episode i . We define the normalized subgoal progress as \mathrm{Progress}_{i}=\frac{m_{i}}{K}, (1) which measures the deepest normalized semantic milestone reached during execution. At the task level, for N evaluated episodes, the reach rate of g_{k} is R_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}[m_{i}\geq k], (2) representing the fraction of rollouts that reach at least the k -th semantic milestone. To localize where execution terminates, we further define the first incomplete subgoal for episodes with m_{i}<K as f_{i}=g_{m_{i}+1}. (3) The distribution of f_{i} provides an operational stage-wise localization of failed executions according to the first unreached semantic milestone. It does not identify the causal instant at which failure is initiated. Episodes with m_{i}=K are not assigned f_{i} , regardless of their final official task-success label. These measures separate final task completion from the depth and location of execution failure.
