title: Safactory: A Scalable Agentic Infrastructure for Training Trustworthy Autonomous Intelligence

§Abstract
As large models evolve from conversational assistants into autonomous agents, challenges increasingly arise from long-horizon decision making, tool use, and real environment interaction. Existing agenticinfrastructure remain fragmented across evaluation, data management, and agent evolution, making it difficult to discover risks systematically and improve models in a continuous closed loop. In this report, we present \textbf{Safactory}, a scalable agent factory for trustworthy autonomous intelligence. Safactory integrates three tightly coupled platforms: a \textbf{Parallel Simulation Platform} for trajectory generation, a \textbf{Trustworthy Data Platform} for trajectory storage and experience extraction, and an \textbf{Autonomous Evolution Platform} for asynchronous reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation. As far as we know, Safactory is the first framework to propose a unified evolutionary pipeline for next-generation trustworthy autonomous intelligence.

1.1 A Panoramic View of AI Agent Safety Risks With the rapid development of applications such as companion AI, DeepResearch, intelligent terminals, and embodied AI, large language models are moving from ”conversational assistants” to ”autonomous agents.” These systems are not only responsible for answering questions but also continuously perceive their environment, plan tasks, invoke tools, and directly influence external systems. This brings stronger application capabilities, but also imposes higher safety requirements. Unlike traditional single-turn models, the risks of agents are not limited to harmful outputs; they accumulate progressively throughout the process of ”perception → decision-making → execution → feedback,” ultimately manifesting as real-world environmental consequences. As shown in Figure 2 , violations, jailbreak inductions, and explicit attacks are merely the risks above the surface. Beneath the waterline lie more insidious issues such as tool misuse, state drift, process loss of control, data contamination, cross-turn inducement, and cascading errors. These risks are often harder to detect and more difficult to cover with static testing. Such risks typically exhibit three characteristics. First, they often emerge in long-horizon, multi-step interactions rather than single-turn input-output pairs. Second, they are closely tied to environmental states, tool interfaces, and execution contexts, making them difficult to characterize using static benchmarks alone. Third, when they do occur, the consequences often go beyond degraded response quality — they may further lead to erroneous operations, privacy breaches, system damage, or even real-world losses. Therefore, trustworthy AI for high-value scenarios requires not just ”safer responses,” but more importantly, the ability to remain controllable, auditable, and recoverable while taking continuous action. 1.2 Limitations of Existing Systems To address the above challenges, existing AI agent safety systems still exhibit significant gaps across three foundational dimensions: evaluation, data, and evolution. These issues are not isolated from one another. Collectively, they result in safety efforts often remaining in a passive ”find a problem, fix a problem” mode, making it difficult to form a continuous closed loop oriented toward real-world deployment scenarios. Limitations of Evaluation Infrastructure Existing security evaluations are still dominated by static question-answering, single-round adversarial examples, or fixed task sets. Such approaches are suitable for answering whether a model ”will say something wrong,” but struggle to answer whether an agent ”will do something wrong during continuous execution.” When risks depend on tool invocation chains, changes in environmental states, and the accumulation of long-horizon decisions, single-turn interactive evaluations can only capture the surface of the iceberg, making it difficult to systematically expose deep failure modes. Even in executable environments, many existing frameworks remain centered around fixed tasks and offline logs, lacking capabilities such as high-concurrency retesting, dynamic perturbation injection, and joint analysis of process–consequence coupling. For the relevant technical landscape, see Section 2.1 . Limitations of Data Infrastructure Currently, many evaluation and training frameworks treat data merely as a byproduct of the runtime process, with common practices relying on in-memory caches and temporary local files for storage. This approach struggles to stably support large-scale heterogeneous data management, nor does it facilitate cross-task, cross-version, or cross-environment retrieval, reuse, and auditing. More importantly, agents continuously generate a large volume of trajectories, reward signals, and failure contexts during evaluation, execution, and training. These are essentially high-value data assets, yet existing systems generally lack a unified mechanism for their structured accumulation, automated analysis, and subsequent feedback into model optimization. For further discussion, see Section 2.2 . Limitations of Evolution infrastructure At the level of model evolution, training, evaluation, and deployment remain largely disconnected from one another. Issues identified during evaluation are difficult to promptly convert into training samples, and real-world feedback generated during execution is also hard to smoothly reintegrate into subsequent optimization workflows. Meanwhile, reinforcement learning and online distillation for agent scenarios are transitioning from standalone algorithm implementations to system-level orchestration problems.
