title: ScamWorld: An Interactive Environment for Evaluating Web Agents Against Online Fraud
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04879

§Abstract
Web agents increasingly browse, shop, and manage accounts on behalf of their users. Prior work has shown that they can be misled by adversarial content, including prompt injections and deceptive interfaces. Beyond such attacks, agents operating on the open web also encounter online fraud designed to deceive humans, including fraudulent online stores, fake investment platforms, and advance-fee schemes. How web agents respond to such fraud during task execution remains underexplored, in part because scam websites are short-lived, making systematic evaluation difficult. To address this gap, we introduce ScamWorld, an interactive environment that reconstructs reported scam websites across diverse scenarios, fraud mechanisms, and languages. Each reconstructed website reproduces documented fraud mechanisms, making relevant signs of fraud observable before the agent's final action and enabling controlled, repeatable evaluation. Through an extensive study of web agents powered by six frontier LLMs, we find that baseline deception rates range from 11% to 35% across models. Agents proceed more readily with payments than with requests for credentials or wallet information. A closer analysis of agent trajectories shows that some agents acknowledge suspicious conditions yet proceed, at times treating the user's instructions as authorisation to accept additional terms imposed by the website. ScamWorld provides a reusable testbed for systematically evaluating and stress-testing web agents against real-world online fraud.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Web agents now search the web, shop in online stores, book travel, and manage accounts on behalf of their users ( Deng et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2022 ; He et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2026b ) . In carrying out these tasks, they also encounter untrustworthy content written for humans, from misinformation ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2026 ) to online fraud ( Kotzias et al., 2025 ; Bitaab et al., 2025b ) . Such fraud cost victims an estimated $442 billion in one year, with shopping scams the most common type ( Global Anti-Scam Alliance and Feedzai, 2025 ) . Browser protections offer incomplete coverage against fraudulent websites. Google Safe Browsing, for example, flags fewer than one in five phishing websites, with an average detection delay of 4.5 days ( Lee et al., 2025 ) . These gaps leave agents exposed to fraudulent websites during ordinary browsing and search ( Kotzias et al., 2025 ; Paudel and Stringhini, 2026 ) . For instance, Figure 1 documents a case from 2026 in which a website impersonating a major grocery chain appeared in Google Search results. This is a real-world problem. When web agents encounter such scam websites while carrying out a user’s request, do they recognise the fraud, or do they complete the task anyway? Existing studies on web-agent safety and online fraud fall into two lines of work. i) Work on web-agent safety primarily demonstrates the feasibility of attacks, injecting malicious instructions ( Debenedetti et al., 2024 ; Evtimov et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2026b ) or adding deceptive interface elements such as dark patterns ( Ersoy et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026a ; Shi et al., 2026 ; Cuvin et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) that steer the agent toward an attacker-chosen outcome. ii) Work on online fraud studies how automated methods, increasingly based on LLMs, can help humans detect malicious URLs or web pages from their address or content, mainly treating the task as a classification problem ( Bitaab et al., 2023 ; Nakano et al., 2025 ; Bitaab et al., 2025a ; Zhou et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, we focus on how increasingly capable web agents behave when they encounter online fraud during task execution. Studying this behaviour calls for an interactive testbed grounded in real-world scam websites, where agents’ decisions and actions can be systematically examined across scam scenarios, interaction modes, and languages. As summarised in Table 1 , existing benchmarks rarely evaluate web agents on real scam websites. To this end, we introduce ScamWorld , an interactive testbed for studying how web agents behave on reconstructed scam websites, as illustrated in Figure 2 . Since scam websites are short-lived ( Oest et al., 2020 ; Lee et al., 2025 ) , and an agent acting on them could hand real payments or personal data to fraudsters, we reconstruct them as persistent, locally hosted websites that support safe and repeatable evaluation. To build these websites, we first collect scam URLs reported by victims, watchdogs, and authorities ( Li et al., 2023 ) and retrieve their pages from the live web or, for offline websites, from archives such as the Wayback Machine . Because these copies are often incomplete ( e.g. , missing images or scripts), we manually rebuild 363 scam websites spanning 16 scenarios, 38 fraud mechanisms, and 26 languages, each preserving the original fraud mechanisms before the final action, such as a payment. Recording every interaction lets us trace whether agents identify red flags, warn the user, stop, or proceed. We also include 53 legitimate websites built the same way, so that scam avoidance can be distinguished from over-refusal. Extensive experiments with web agents using six frontier LLMs as backbones reveal that all evaluated agents are susceptible to online fraud, with baseline deception rates ranging from 11% to 35%. A closer analysis of their trajectories shows that agents may identify website-specific risks yet proceed, often treating the user’s request as sufficient authorisation. Under explicit assessment, the same agents also recognise many of the websites that deceive them during task execution. In deciding whether to proceed, they are more cautious about disclosing credentials or wallet details than about making payments. With a one-sentence warning, they are less often deceived but also less likely to complete tasks on legitimate websites. To support further research on these behaviours, we release ScamWorld as a reusable testbed for evaluating and stress-testing web agents across diverse scam scenarios, fraud mechanisms, languages, and browser interaction modes, alongside legitimate controls.
