title: WaitLens: Temporal VLM-Based Adaptive Waiting for Industrial GUI Testing Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32482

§Abstract
Improving the efficiency of GUI testing agents is important for industrial deployment. Beyond LLM inference, fixed waiting between consecutive interactions introduces substantial overhead. Our industrial study reveals that fixed waiting accounts for 42.3% of the time within the sampled interaction intervals, comparable to the 54.8% spent on model calls. Moreover, 62.9% of the waiting time is spent after the GUI has already finished loading. Although prior studies have proposed adaptive waiting strategies, their reliance on app-specific data or platform-specific information limits their applicability across diverse industrial environments. To this end, we propose WaitLens, which fine-tunes a lightweight VLM on GUI loading trajectories annotated with waiting end times. We introduce temporal resampling to construct diverse training examples, enabling the model to learn varying GUI loading behaviors from temporal observations. Experiments show that WaitLens achieves 86.08% accuracy in deciding whether to keep waiting, compared with 54.13% for the backbone VLM. Our evaluation using recordings from Meituan's industrial testing pipeline shows that WaitLens reduces mean extra waiting after the interface is ready by 94.98% compared with a 20-second fixed wait.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recently, large language model (LLM) based GUI agents have emerged as a new testing method, which reduces reliance on manually maintained test scripts [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . Given natural language testing tasks, these agents repeatedly observe GUI screenshots, reason about subsequent actions, and interact with apps to complete testing tasks. As illustrated in Figure 1 , each action is followed by a waiting period before the agent captures the next screenshot, allowing the interface to respond. Determining an appropriate waiting time, however, is challenging. If the wait is too short, agents may mistake an intermediate loading state for the result of the preceding action, leading to incorrect decisions [ 9 , 10 ] . To accommodate slow responses, existing GUI agents often adopt fixed waiting times after each action [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] . However, a long fixed wait also keeps agents idle when the interface becomes ready earlier. Our motivation study in Section II-B shows that 62.9% of the waiting time in our industrial GUI testing agent is spent idling after the GUI has already finished loading. This substantial overhead motivates adaptive waiting policies in GUI testing agents. Although prior studies have explored adaptive waiting for GUI testing [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] , these methods face limitations when applied to GUI agents across diverse industrial environments. First, learning-based methods rely on app-specific training data and may struggle with unseen GUIs. For example, AdaT [ 14 ] uses a convolutional neural network (CNN) to classify GUI rendering states. To adapt AdaT to WeChat, WeReplay [ 15 ] collects new training data from the app and fine-tunes the model. Second, analysis-based methods rely on internal runtime or platform-specific information that screenshot-based agents cannot directly access. For example, Zhang et al. [ 16 ] monitor DOM changes, while TurboDroid [ 17 ] uses method dependency analysis to determine waiting conditions. Therefore, GUI agents need an adaptive waiting approach that relies only on screenshots and can generalize across different GUIs. Vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in understanding diverse GUIs [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] , making them a promising alternative. However, directly applying existing VLMs to adaptive waiting is impractical for two reasons. First, unlike static GUI tasks, judging whether an interface has finished loading requires observing how the screen changes over time [ 22 , 23 ] . Simply feeding all historical screenshots into a VLM increases input size and inference latency as loading continues. This may offset the time saved by adaptive waiting. Second, general-purpose VLMs are not specifically trained to distinguish loading states from completed interfaces. As a result, they may struggle to accurately predict whether the GUI is ready. To address these challenges, we propose WaitLens , an VLM-based approach for adaptive waiting in GUI testing agents. Instead of waiting for a fixed duration, WaitLens periodically checks the screen after each action to decide when testing should proceed. To capture interface changes within a bounded input size, WaitLens uses a sliding window of screenshots as input to model. During VLM fine-tuning, we use temporal resampling to augment the training data and simulate different GUI loading speeds. Together, these designs enable WaitLens to predict GUI readiness across diverse loading conditions with low inference overhead. We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of WaitLens . Our in-house experiments show that WaitLens achieves 86.08% accuracy in GUI readiness prediction, compared with 54.13% for the backbone VLM Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [ 24 ] . Moreover, we evaluate WaitLens using industrial GUI testing recordings from Meituan, a major Chinese local-services platform with over 800 million transacting users [ 25 ] . In our industrial replay evaluation, WaitLens reduces mean post-Ready waiting by 94.98% compared with the existing fixed waiting strategy. We further analyze representative failure cases to understand the remaining challenges and identify directions for future research. We summarize the contributions of this paper as follows. • We share industrial observations that identify fixed post-action waiting as a primary source of overhead in GUI testing agents. Our motivation study reveals that over 60% of the fixed waiting time is wasted after the interface is already ready. • We introduce WaitLens , an VLM-based adaptive waiting approach that learns when to end waiting from temporal GUI observations. We make this approach practical by fine-tuning a lightweight open-source VLM on annotated GUI loading sequences. • Comprehensive experiments show that WaitLens outperforms existing baselines in detecting GUI loading completion with low runtime overhead. ## III Method
III-A Overview WaitLens is a VLM-based adaptive waiting plugin for GUI testing agents. It replaces the fixed waiting time after each interaction by determining when the interface is ready for the next observation. As shown in Figure 4 (right), WaitLens is invoked immediately after the agent completes an action. During online waiting, WaitLens periodically captures the latest screenshot and combines it with the pre-interaction screenshot and a limited number of recent historical screenshots. The trained VLM uses this temporal visual input to predict whether the GUI has reached the Ready state. Waiting ends early once the model produces the required number of consecutive ready predictions. WaitLens then returns the latest checked screenshot to the agent for its next observation. Otherwise, it continues checking subsequent screenshots. If the original waiting budget expires before this condition is met, WaitLens returns the latest available screenshot. To train the VLM, we construct labeled visual sequences from annotated interaction recordings and augment them through temporal resampling. We then fine-tune a lightweight pretrained VLM (Qwen3-VL-2B) using these samples, as illustrated in Figure 4 (left). III-B Bounded Temporal Visual Representation A single screenshot may not reveal whether the GUI has reached the Ready state, as the GUI evolves over time. For example, Figure 5 shows that the playlist header appears at 1.25 s, while the song list does not appear until 1.50 s. Without observing this change, the earlier screenshot may be mistakenly considered Ready. Therefore, we organize the VLM input using three complementary types of screenshots to capture GUI changes over time. The pre-interaction screenshot provides a reference for identifying changes triggered by the action. Historical screenshots capture how the interface evolves, while the current screenshot is used to determine whether waiting should end. Historical screenshots help the VLM identify recent GUI changes, but retaining every screenshot would increase the input size as waiting continues. To expose recent interface changes while keeping the input size bounded, we select historical screenshots using a sliding window. Specifically, let t_{0} denote action completion, \Delta the screenshot sampling interval, and K the maximum history length. Screenshots are sampled at regular intervals t_{k}=t_{0}+k\Delta ( k\geq 1 ). For the current observation at t_{k} , the history retains at most the K most recent preceding screenshots: H_{k}=[I(t_{k-N}),\ldots,I(t_{k-1})],\qquad N=\min(K,k-1), (1) where I(t) denotes the screenshot at time t . Thus, the history grows as early screenshots become available ( k\leq K ) and slides forward to drop older frames once it reaches the limit of K images. In the example of Figure 5 , the sampling interval is \Delta=0.25 s and the history limit is K=4 . After selecting the history, we concatenate its images from oldest to newest into a horizontal strip. The pre-interaction and current screenshots remain separate, preserving the distinction between contextual evidence and the image being judged. To preserve interface details while limiting input size, we retain the pre-interaction and current screenshots at higher resolutions and downsample the historical screenshots. When no history exists ( N=0 ), the history strip is omitted. Finally, we provide these images to the VLM with a prompt specifying their roles and temporal order. The VLM predicts whether the current interface is ready or not_ready . III-C VLM Fine-Tuning with Temporal Resampling To train the VLM to predict when waiting should end, we construct labeled examples from annotated action recordings S . For each recording, let t_{0} denote action completion, t_{R} the annotated earliest Ready time, and t_{E} the start of the next action or the end of the recording. Observations before t_{R} are labeled not_ready , while those from t_{R} to t_{E} are labeled ready . Following the online procedure described in Section III-B , we sample screenshots at regular intervals of \Delta and pair each current screenshot with the pre-interaction reference and up to K preceding screenshots. To avoid over-representing slow-loading actions, we retain at most one regularly sampled example per label for each action, forming the baseline set D_{G} . However, in D_{G} , fixed-interval sampling may introduce a spurious correlation between history length and training labels. Since history length increases with elapsed time until reaching K , early observations tend to have shorter histories than later ones. As GUIs typically become ready later in the loading process, the VLM may associate short histories with not_ready rather than learning from actual GUI changes . This correlation may mislead the model when GUI loading speeds vary across interactions and devices. To break this coupling, we introduce temporal resampling , which varies observation times and historical frame spacing while selecting history length independently of observation time . Specifically, we draw on the variability of GUI response speeds across devices and network conditions. A similar visual progression may unfold quickly on one device and slowly on another, suggesting that temporal compression or stretching can approximate variations in response speed. For an observer sampling at a fixed interval, these variations change how much visual progress is visible between successive screenshots. We can construct such temporal variations from a recorded interaction by selecting screenshots at different temporal spacings. For each action, target label, and history length N\in\{0,\ldots,K\} , we generate candidate examples in two steps and collect them in a set C . First, to cover the early, middle, and late stages of each phase, we divide the corresponding label interval into three equal-duration segments. We cycle through these segments, drawing a current observation time t uniformly within each selected segment, as shown in Figure 6 (a). Second, when N>0 , we partition the preceding interval (t_{0},t) into N equal-duration strata and draw one historical frame uniformly from each stratum, as illustrated in Figure 6 (b). When N=0 , the history is omitted. By specifying N independently of t , this process synthesizes short or empty histories for ready observations and full histories for not_ready observations while preserving chronological order. Finally, we select a subset C^{\star}\subseteq C to supplement D_{G} toward balanced coverage across the four groups formed by pairing short ( N<K ) or full ( N=K ) histories with ready or not_ready . Within the short-history groups, selection also targets balanced coverage of individual history lengths for each label. Algorithm 1 summarizes this procedure. We then fine-tune the pretrained VLM on the combined training set D=D_{G}\cup C^{\star} by minimizing the cross-entropy loss over the target label y(t) . Specifically, WaitLens integrates into screenshot-based GUI agents by replacing the fixed waiting period between action execution and the next observation, as shown in the upper of Figure 4 . Upon action completion at t_{0} , the agent provides WaitLens with the pre-interaction screenshot, screenshot access, and its original waiting budget W . The plugin determines when to return the next screenshot without modifying the agent’s reasoning or action-execution logic. Moreover, it operates entirely on screenshots, requiring no application instrumentation, source-code modification, or view-hierarchy access. During online waiting, WaitLens samples screenshots at interval \Delta and maintains a history of up to K screenshots. The waiting budget W sets a deadline at t_{0}+W . At each check, the fine-tuned VLM predicts whether the current interface is ready using the temporal visual input described in Section III-B . To reduce prediction fluctuations, WaitLens supports a configurable threshold n that requires n consecutive ready predictions before returning. Once this condition is met, WaitLens ends waiting and returns the latest checked screenshot to the agent. Otherwise, it continues checking until the original waiting budget W expires. If no ready prediction is obtained by then, WaitLens returns the latest available screenshot. This allows the agent to resume earlier when the GUI is ready while retaining the original waiting budget as a fallback.
