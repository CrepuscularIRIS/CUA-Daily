title: Beyond Plausible Prediction: Safety Sufficiency in Web World Models
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40798

§Abstract
Web world models increasingly predict the consequences of candidate actions before agents act, yet a plausible predicted future does not necessarily support the correct safety decision. In a matched human evaluation of 3,000 predictions from ten frozen open models, 86.1% are judged semantically plausible. However, several models exhibit highly asymmetric authorisation behaviour, combining low false-safe execution with poor recall of clarifiable ASK cases and a strong preference for BLOCK. This motivates safety sufficiency: whether a predictive interface preserves and exposes the policy-decisive information required to distinguish actions that may be EXECUTED, require clarification through ASK, or should be BLOCKED. We introduce SafeWebWorld, a benchmark for evaluating safety sufficiency across five web-safety domains through action-conditioned futures, policy-decisive evidence, and policy-conditioned decisions. Under a controlled Security-Aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning protocol, evidence preservation can improve substantially while authorisation-sensitive decisions deteriorate. To determine whether this deterioration reflects loss of authorisation-sensitive score structure or a failure of the decision readout, we introduce Latent Authorisation Recovery (LAR). Frozen-score recalibration reveals substantial but backbone-dependent recovery, while paired recovery provides only a modest additional gain. Yet within-family and matched-authorisation evaluations remain poor, showing that aggregate recoverability does not imply robust fine-grained authorisation behaviour. Overall, plausible prediction, evidence preservation, and aggregate recovery are each insufficient on their own to establish robust authorisation.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Web agents increasingly use world models to predict the consequences of candidate actions before interacting with live websites ( Xiao et al., 2026 ; Chae et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026 ) . In this setting, a world model predicts an action-conditioned future from the current context and a candidate action. Such predictions support planning, trajectory generation, agent training, and inference-time decision making ( Fang et al., 2025 ; Ding et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026b ; Kazantzidis et al., 2026 ) . Yet a plausible future does not necessarily contain the information needed for the correct safety decision. Consider an agent installing a software update from an unverified third-party source. A world model may plausibly predict that “the update will be installed successfully”, while omitting that the source is unverified. The prediction may therefore plausibly describe what will happen , yet be insufficient for deciding whether it should happen . This failure can persist even when the predicted future is semantically plausible: in a matched human evaluation of 3,000 predictions from ten frozen open models, 86.1% are judged semantically plausible. At the same time, low unsafe-execution rates can coexist with highly asymmetric authorisation behaviour. For example, WebSynthesis-Qwen3-8B achieves a 0.08% false-safe execution rate, but 0.00% Ask recall and 99.90% Block recall (Table 1 ). Thus, avoiding unsafe execution alone does not establish whether a model correctly distinguishes permitted, clarifiable, and prohibited actions. We call this requirement safety sufficiency : whether a predictive interface preserves and exposes the policy-decisive information required for the downstream authorisation decision. A false-safe future is one failure mode in which a plausible prediction omits such information and supports Execute when the applicable policy requires Ask or Block (Figure 1 ). Existing agent-safety benchmarks and predictive guardrails evaluate unsafe behaviour, risk, or intervention ( Levy et al., 2026 ; Tur et al., 2025 ; Evtimov et al., 2025 ; Debenedetti et al., 2024 ; Zheng et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Shen et al., 2026 ) , but do not directly evaluate whether a world-model predictive interface supports this authorisation boundary. To address this gap, we introduce SafeWebWorld , a benchmark that jointly evaluates action-conditioned futures, policy-decisive evidence, and policy-conditioned Execute / Ask / Block decisions across five web-safety domains. The three-way decision distinguishes actions that may proceed, those requiring clarification of authority, intent, or scope, and those prohibited regardless of further confirmation. SafeWebWorld also enables us to ask whether poor authorisation behaviour can be addressed simply by improving security-evidence preservation. Under controlled Security-Aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning (SA-SFT), evidence preservation can improve substantially while authorisation-sensitive decisions deteriorate. We call this dissociation Authorisation-Policy Collapse . This raises a further question: does authorisation-sensitive score structure disappear, or does it survive but fail to reach the emitted decision? We introduce Latent Authorisation Recovery (LAR) to distinguish these possibilities. LAR examines whether authorisation-sensitive score structure survives collapse, whether decision quality remains recoverable from frozen scores, and whether apparent recovery persists under finer-grained authorisation checks. We find that aggregate decision quality is substantially recoverable from frozen scores, with the extent of single-backbone recovery varying substantially by backbone; paired recovery provides only a modest gain beyond the strongest calibrated single-backbone readout. Yet this does not translate reliably to fine-grained authorisation behaviour. Our main contributions are: Safety sufficiency and SafeWebWorld. We formulate safety sufficiency around the information required to distinguish Execute , Ask , and Block , and introduce SafeWebWorld to evaluate it across five web-safety domains. Evidence preservation is not sufficient for authorisation. Low false-safe execution can coexist with asymmetric authorisation behaviour, while controlled SA-SFT can improve evidence preservation without reliably improving authorisation decisions. Recoverability is not sufficient for robust authorisation. LAR shows that aggregate decision quality remains recoverable from frozen scores, with the extent of single-backbone recovery varying substantially by backbone and only modest additional gain from paired recovery, while fine-grained authorisation failures persist.
