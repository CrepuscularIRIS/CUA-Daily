title: A Cross-Scenario Survey and Analysis of Self-Evolving Multimodal Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14256

§Abstract
Recent work shows that multimodal agents increasingly self-improve without recurring human annotation. However, each self-improvement mechanism is designed and evaluated within a single scenario, leaving its preconditions unverified under cross-scenario transfer. This survey investigates the structural source of asymmetric mechanism transfer across four scenarios: multimodal reasoning, multimodal generation, GUI and Web navigation, and embodied manipulation. We first formalize the self-evolving multimodal agent by four criteria and a differentia of self-reference, and adopt the three-stage self-evolving loop of experience generation, feedback acquisition, and update consolidation as our analytical framework to position the scenarios along ceilings on rollout count and feedback fidelity. Comparing self-evolving with fixed loops within each scenario separates the limitations that the scenario imposes from those inherent in the self-evolving design. We then recast each mechanism as a trade-off that raises a scarce factor by presupposing a task-structural resource, such as output equivalence or checkable outcomes, and judge it portable, conditional, blocked, or redundant across scenarios. We attribute the asymmetry to divergent combinations of scarce factors and resources across opposite transfer directions, and identify the matched-budget measurements that could overturn these still untested verdicts.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in multimodal foundation models have fueled an explosive proliferation of multimodal agents [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] , spawning a flourishing spectrum of self-evolution techniques across multimodal reasoning [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] , multimodal generation [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] , GUI and Web navigation [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] , and embodied manipulation [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] . Despite strong scenario-specific gains, existing self-evolving methods are designed and evaluated within single scenarios, and their mechanisms do not transfer equally well in both directions of a scenario pair. For example, majority voting derives label-free rewards for reasoning tasks from consistency across sampled answers [ 22 , 5 , 23 ] , yet finds no counterpart in embodied manipulation, where action trajectories admit no equivalence check and physical rollouts incur steep execution and reset costs. Conversely, world models circumvent expensive environmental interactions via imagined rollouts [ 24 , 25 ] , but raise a factor that is not scarce in reasoning, where sampling directly from the policy costs little. Beneath this directional breakdown in mechanism transferability, which suggests a cross-scenario asymmetry , also lies a shared self-evolving loop across diverse tasks. Specifically, across all four scenarios, self-evolving agents share the same cycle, i.e. , generating exploratory experience, acquiring evaluative feedback, and consolidating persistent updates. This survey investigates the structural source of cross-scenario asymmetry by characterizing this shared loop and explaining why the two directions of a scenario pair meet different obstacles to transfer. ## III The Three-Stage Self-Evolving Loop
We adopt the three-stage self-evolving loop as our analytical framework, decomposing each cycle of an autonomous learning loop into experience generation, feedback acquisition, and update consolidation. Fixed and self-evolving loops across all four scenarios sharing this structure, we differentiate them by where the bottleneck binds instead of by topology, treating rollout cost and feedback fidelity as bounds on viable mechanisms. We first formalize the three stages and their governing factors, then position the four scenarios within a positioning matrix spanned by these bounds, and trace how the stages couple across cycles. III-A Loop Decomposition As shown in Figure 2 , we factor each cycle into three scenario-invariant stages, each organized around one meta-operator ( \sigma_{\omega} , V_{\nu} , f_{\zeta} ). This decomposition applies across the entire genus of autonomous learning loops and treats auxiliary operations, such as credit assignment or data filtering, as refinements within a stage, not as additional stages. Stage 1 Experience Generation (EG): the goal sampler \sigma_{\omega} draws the goal batch \boldsymbol{g}_{k} , and the current agent \mathcal{A}_{k} rolls out in \mathcal{P}_{g} for each goal g\in\boldsymbol{g}_{k} , producing the trajectory batch \mathcal{D}_{k} . Through the quantity and informativeness of \mathcal{D}_{k} , the only trajectories FA and UC act on, EG bounds how fast and how broadly the loop can evolve ( speed and breadth ). Stage 2 Feedback Acquisition (FA): the feedback function V_{\nu} maps \mathcal{D}_{k} , together with the agent history \mathcal{A}_{0:k} when V_{\nu} reads the agent, to scores, preferences, or critiques sourced from the environment, the bootstrap, or the agent itself. FA is the loop’s only source of information about the idealized task reward R ( the R -aligned direction ). Stage 3 Update Consolidation (UC): the evolution operator f_{\zeta} computes an update from (\mathcal{A}_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{D}_{k},V_{\nu}(\mathcal{D}_{k})) and writes it into at least one persistent component of \mathcal{A}_{k} , producing the next-cycle agent \mathcal{A}_{k+1} . UC determines whether an update persists across later cycles and generalizes beyond the goals that produced it ( retention and generalization ). The six factors originate at the two scales of Figure 1 . The per-episode cost c(\tau) governing \bar{K} and action localization L_{\nu} arise within the micro-scale interaction loop, whereas information gain, evaluator fidelity F_{\nu} , and consolidation factors ( S_{\zeta} , G_{\zeta} ) emerge from the macro-scale learning loop, the positioning matrix thereby pairing a micro-scale axis with a macro-scale one. For each goal g in the batch \boldsymbol{g}_{k} drawn from \sigma_{\omega}(k,\mathcal{H}_{k}) , \mathcal{A}_{k} draws K_{k} rollouts from p^{\pi_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}}_{g} and pools them into \mathcal{D}_{k} . EG must supply experience of sufficient scale and informativeness under finite compute, wall-clock, and safety budgets, a constraint on quantity and one on quality. We summarize this bottleneck through an effective-experience decomposition, \mathcal{Q}_{k}^{\mathrm{EG}}\;:=\;K_{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\sigma_{\omega}(k,\mathcal{H}_{k})}\!\left[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}(g)\right], (9) where K_{k} is the number of per-goal rollouts in cycle k , and I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}(g)\!:=\!\mathrm{Var}_{\tau\sim p^{\pi}_{g}}[\mathrm{ret}_{g}(\tau)] is the outcome variance of g . Here, \mathrm{ret}_{g}(\tau)\!=\!\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},u_{t},g) denotes the return of \tau , and p^{\pi}_{g} abbreviates p^{\pi_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}}_{g} . This variance, a goal-level proxy for the learnable information gain [ 131 ] , vanishes on goals that \mathcal{A}_{k} has mastered or never solves, where GRPO-style group-relative updates receive zero advantage under an R -aligned evaluator. Under a binary terminal reward with \gamma\!=\!1 , it reduces to p_{g}(1-p_{g}) , which difficulty-targeting rewards and filters track via an estimated success probability p_{g} . Defined on the idealized reward R of Eq. 4 , the proxy renders \mathcal{Q}_{k}^{\mathrm{EG}} diagnostic, and it exposes two failure modes. As K_{k} shrinks, too few per-goal rollouts remain to contrast success with failure. As \mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}] approaches zero, the sampled goals are mastered or unreachable. Concretely, K_{k} is bounded by per-rollout interaction cost and action recoverability , while \mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}] is governed by the diversity and information gain targeted by \sigma_{\omega} and by the interaction mode realizing p^{\pi_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}}_{g} ( Table III ). Quantity Constraint. Interaction cost and action recoverability govern K_{k} via a single per-rollout cost c(\tau) , eliminating the need for a separate term for actions that resist reverting, resetting, or safe repetition. Specifically, c(\tau) absorbs the time, compute, synchronization, and reset cost of one rollout, with the reset term penalizing both environment restoration and expected damage from failed trajectories. Under a fixed per-goal budget B , the rollout count satisfies the affordability ceiling K_{k}\!\leq\!\bar{K}\!:=\!B/\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[c(\tau)] , where the expectation runs over episodes of the target task family at the horizon cap T_{\max} , not over the rollouts of a particular policy, which lets \bar{K} reflect task structure and not policy choice. When c(\tau) is small, group-sampling operators such as self-consistency [ 80 , 132 ] or GRPO [ 65 , 133 , 134 ] sample a group of rollouts per goal at negligible marginal cost. As c(\tau) grows, the bottleneck shifts from theoretical execution feasibility to the budget of exploratory failures the system can absorb. Quality Constraint. Even with a large K_{k} , a trajectory set is unproductive if its goals are already mastered or still unreachable for \mathcal{A}_{k} , leaving EG viable only while \mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}]\!>\!0 . For instance, self-play [ 131 ] stops improving once its synthetic pipeline no longer supplies learnable information gain. Diversity and information gain determines whether \sigma_{\omega} draws goals with nonzero I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}} . Sampling schemes escalate in intervention from random sampling [ 65 ] through curriculum mining over past failures [ 135 , 17 ] and active task selection [ 136 , 137 ] to environment synthesis [ 63 , 138 , 139 , 140 , 141 ] . Random sampling keeps \sigma_{\omega} stationary, whereas curriculum mining and active selection make it adaptive. Environment synthesis acts through the task argument g of Sec. II-A and is adaptive only when the generator reads the agent’s record. Only an adaptive sampler conditions the next goal draw on the agent’s record and places an a entry in the goal-sampler row. Interaction mode fixes what evidence each trajectory can expose. Single-pass generation and tool-augmented reasoning yield single-action episodes, whereas digital and embodied environments expose multi-step state transitions whose informativeness depends on platform dynamics, reset conditions, and observability. Asymmetric Substitutability. The product in Eq. 9 enables mutual compensation between its two factors, locating the cross-scenario asymmetry in the cost of raising each factor. Raising K_{k} offsets low information gain only when rollouts are cheap, parallelizable, and recoverable and feedback retains contrast as group size grows. Consensus rewards losing contrast beyond small groups [ 22 ] , the loop elsewhere must instead raise \mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}] via curriculum mining [ 115 , 17 ] , active exploration [ 5 , 18 ] , demonstrations at initialization [ 19 ] , or synthetic environments [ 6 , 63 , 138 ] . Equal values of K_{k} therefore do not imply identical bottlenecks. Multimodal generation and GUI/Web navigation report overlapping group sizes ( Table III ), yet generation samples cheap, reversible passes below \bar{K} and is bounded by \mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}] over a fixed prompt pool, whereas navigation incurs latency-bound episodes and is bounded by \bar{K} itself. Cheap but uninformative rollouts saturate the loop, whereas informative but costly rollouts throttle its scaling.
