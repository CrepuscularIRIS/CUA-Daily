title: WorldSketch: Sketching World Models for Zero-Shot Mobile Manipulation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00513

§Abstract
Most agentic robotic systems use vision-language models (VLMs) either to orchestrate predefined skills or to directly generate actions, constraints, and control programs. The former is limited by the available skill library, while the latter requires VLMs to reason about precise geometry and contact. In this paper, we propose WorldSketch, a new paradigm for agentic robotics in which a VLM constructs a world model rather than generating robot actions. The VLM acts as an agent that actively queries perception tools to build and visually validate a minimal kinematic world model, capturing only task-relevant objects, their interaction geometry, and possible motions. A geometric planner then jointly optimizes contact and whole-body motion on the resulting model. Moreover, the world model is self-improving: execution feedback verifies outcomes, updates uncertain hypotheses, and enables the agent to revise the model when its predictions are contradicted. We demonstrate the generality of this paradigm across RoboCasa, LIBERO-PRO, and R2R-CE navigation, achieving zero-shot success rates of 91%, 97%, and 76%, respectively. On RoboCasa, WorldSketch outperforms the next-best zero-shot agent baseline by 58 percentage points while using 10 times fewer VLM input tokens; on LIBERO-PRO, it exceeds CaP-X by 82 percentage points. In navigation, it matches the success rate of step-by-step VLM agents while using 6 times fewer input tokens.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) map images and language to robot actions after training on large robot datasets ( Zitkovich et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025b ) . However, they generalize poorly beyond the layouts and tasks they were trained on. Policies that solve 82–99% of the standard LIBERO suites, for example, succeed in 0–21% of episodes on average once the initial object positions change, and in at most 1% once the task changes ( Zhou et al., 2025c ) . Vision-language models (VLMs), trained on web-scale data, generalize much better across tasks, and they have become the decision maker of agentic robot systems that plan, call tools and improve themselves from feedback ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . In these agentic systems Wang et al. (2026) , the VLM does one of two jobs ( Figure 1 a,b). It either orchestrates the task, choosing the next call from a library of skills ( Ichter et al., 2022 ; Huang et al., 2022 ) , or it writes the motion , as value maps, keypoint constraints and waypoints ( Huang et al., 2023b ; Huang et al., 2024b ; Fang et al., 2024 ) or as control code that runs as the policy ( Liang et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2026 ) . Each job has a drawback. An orchestrator is only as general as its skill library, while a VLM that writes motion or code either stays inside the slow control loop or must reason directly about metric geometry and contact. In this paper, we give the VLM a third job, sketching the world ( Figure 1 c). Instead of asking the VLM what the robot should do, we ask it what the robot needs to know about the world, and we leave the doing to a planner. We ask the VLM to draw a world sketch , a minimal world model of the task. Like a sketch, it holds only what matters. Only the task-relevant entities carry semantic labels. To open a cabinet, for example, the sketch holds the door panel, its handle and its hinge, while all other objects in the kitchen remain unlabelled collision geometry. The sketch is also kinematic. The door is a link on a revolute joint with an axis, an origin and a goal angle, so that a planner can move the gripper along the door’s arc. Such a sketch is small enough for a VLM to draw from a handful of measurements, yet complete enough for a planner to act on. We treat drawing the sketch as an agentic task ( Figure 2 ). The VLM requests targeted perception, such as “the 3-D points of the door’s corners and of the handle’s ends” or “a straight-on view of the stove’s knob labels”. It assembles a sketch from the measured primitives, sees this sketch rendered back into the images and corrects it. Perception thus becomes a set of tools that the VLM calls, instead of a segmentation of the whole scene. Properties that the images cannot settle, such as which way a faucet lever turns on the water, remain in the sketch as joint hypotheses with prior probabilities. On the sketch, a planner chooses the grasp jointly with the whole-body motion along the joint, and a verifier confirms each outcome from changes in depth and appearance. When execution contradicts the sketch, the robot tries the next hypothesis or the VLM revises the sketch, and after a failed attempt a fresh VLM session draws it anew. We evaluate WorldSketch on 30 RoboCasa kitchen tasks with a mobile manipulator, where it succeeds in 91% of episodes with about five VLM calls per episode and without any robot data. With the same VLM, the CaP-X coding agent succeeds in 33% and the other zero-shot baselines in at most 9%, while two VLAs trained on demonstrations of these tasks succeed in 39% and 44%. Success changes little with the VLM or its reasoning effort (86–91%). The first sketch is correct in 76–81% of episodes, and in the runs we analyzed most failures happen in execution. On LIBERO-PRO, whose tabletop tasks perturb object positions and instructions, WorldSketch succeeds in 97% of episodes with about three VLM calls each, compared with 15% for the CaP-X coding agent. The idea also carries over to instruction-following navigation, where a sketch of the doors, passages and stairs that the instruction mentions guides a path planner to the goal. On R2R-CE, WorldSketch succeeds as often as agents that ask the same VLM for each action (76%), with a fifth of the VLM calls. We make three contributions: • The world sketch, a minimal kinematic world model with joint hypotheses, as the interface between a VLM and a planner ( Section 3 ). • WorldSketch, an agentic system in which the VLM draws the sketch by requesting perception and checking the result, and a planner optimizes contact and motion on it. Execution evidence tests the sketch and triggers revisions ( Section 4 ). • An evaluation on RoboCasa kitchen tasks against code-as-policies agents, keypoint constraints and two VLAs, on LIBERO-PRO tabletop tasks and on navigation, with analyses of the accuracy of the sketches and of where success is lost ( Section 5 ).
