arxiv: 2611.01815 (2611.01815v1)
title: When Does Latent Prediction Error Support Physical Surprise Claims in Video World Models?
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01815

§Abstract
Prediction errors from video world models are often treated as physical surprise, but they can also reflect appearance, static content, motion, or ordinary prediction difficulty. We present an evaluation framework that separates what frozen representations contain, how they organize appearance and physical differences, and what changes a model's own prediction error. On IntPhys2, a benchmark of physically possible and impossible events, we compare representations from latent prediction, masked pixel reconstruction, and simple visual statistics. Appearance remains easy to decode, while V-JEPA2 exposes modest information about whether events are possible or impossible. Looking at the latent geometry, appearance changes become smaller compared with differences between videos, their directions line up more consistently across scenes, and their overlap with physical changes decreases in deeper layers. Across action-conditioned models, incorrect actions raise prediction error, while appearance changes also produce substantial responses. In Physion++ video clips, repeating a single frame can produce higher error than the original clip, while changing color throughout the clip can lower it. Using Kubric, a simulation and rendering framework, we find that impossible events can produce lower error than possible events. A fixed error threshold also incorrectly flags many possible events as impossible. A model can therefore represent physical information and respond to incorrect actions without its native prediction error reliably measuring physical surprise.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Video world models are expected to capture regularities that make visual futures predictable. Prediction errors and related scores are consequently used to assess physical plausibility and identify unexpected events ( Smith et al., 2019 ; Piloto et al., 2022 ; Riochet et al., 2018 ; Dai et al., 2023 ; Garrido et al., 2025 ; Bordes et al., 2025 ; Maes et al., 2026 ) . Such scores also guide the selection of physically plausible generated videos ( Baldassarre et al., 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2026 ) . Understanding what they measure matters when errors are used to locate events, compare videos, or reward trajectories. Let f_{C} and f_{T} construct context and target latent representations, also called features, from observations x . Both functions include the model’s encoding and token-selection steps. For a predictor p , optional actions a , and a scoring distance d , the prediction-error score is S(x,a)=d\!\left(p(f_{C}(x),a),\,f_{T}(x)\right). This score measures prediction difficulty in the chosen representation. A large value may reflect physical inconsistency, appearance, static content, motion, or ordinary model error. Physical information may also be accessible to a classifier even when the predictor’s error responds primarily to other properties of the video. We first test whether linear classifiers can distinguish physically possible from impossible events using frozen representations. We also test how well appearance information can be recovered from the same representations. IntPhys2 is a synthetic video benchmark pairing physically possible events with violations such as an object passing through a solid obstacle ( Bordes et al., 2025 ) . We compare V-JEPA2-G, trained to predict latent representations, with VideoMAE2-G, trained to reconstruct masked pixels, and simple visual statistics. Linear classifiers readily recover appearance labels from all three. V-JEPA2-G provides a modest advantage when classifying events as possible or impossible. We then examine how appearance and physical differences are organized across network depth. Changes in hue, brightness, and object color let us measure how far representations move, how consistently they move in the same direction across scenes, and how much those directions overlap with differences between possible and impossible events. In both V-JEPA2-G and VideoMAE2-G, appearance shifts become smaller relative to distances between unrelated videos. At deeper layers, the shifts line up more than those from randomly paired videos and overlap less with physical changes, though these trends sometimes reverse between layers. Appearance labels nevertheless remain easy to recover at the evaluated probe layers. We also test whether representation distances help predict changes in V-JEPA2-G’s prediction error. In a regression analysis, they add little beyond scene conditions and simple visual statistics. Finally, we test prediction error under controlled changes to actions, appearance, motion, and physical validity. Across three action-conditioned world models, incorrect actions raise error while the observed frames are held fixed. Color changes also alter error without changing the simulated physical state. In Physion++ videos of physical interactions, repeating a single frame can increase error, whereas changing color throughout a clip can lower it ( Tung et al., 2023 ) . Using Kubric, a simulation and rendering framework, we test stopping and falling with and without support beneath an object ( Greff et al., 2022 ) . Mean error is higher for stopping in both settings, even though stopping is lawful only when support is present. A fixed error threshold also flags many lawful events as impossible. These results show why recoverable physical information and action sensitivity alone are insufficient to interpret prediction error as physical surprise.
