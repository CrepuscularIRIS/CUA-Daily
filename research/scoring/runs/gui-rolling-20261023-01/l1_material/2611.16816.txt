title: Outcome-Mass Alignment: Controlling Length-Dependent Trajectory Influence in Multi-Turn LLM Agent Reinforcement Learning
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16816

§Abstract
To keep long interaction contexts tractable, stepwise group-relative training for multi-turn LLM agents materializes each source trajectory into multiple response rows and valid tokens. This expansion creates an outer-accounting problem distinct from within-trajectory credit assignment: row-based outcome statistics can count one episode outcome multiple times, while token-level reduction can assign unequal aggregate objective mass across trajectories. We term these effects outcome-statistics multiplicity and objective-mass imbalance. We introduce Outcome-Mass Alignment (OMA), a two-component method that makes both accounting choices explicit. Outcome Alignment computes outcome statistics over unique source trajectories, while Mass Alignment controls aggregate objective mass after within-trajectory aggregation of optimizer-native token objectives. Neither redesigns the base optimizer's credit assignment, importance ratios, or clipping. Across matched ALFWorld GRPO comparisons at two model scales, where failed trajectories typically expand much more than successful ones, OMA improves matched-horizon endpoint success by 7.0-9.4 percentage points. Ordinary WebShop exhibits weak or reversed expansion-outcome coupling and correspondingly mixed effects, whereas in a success-oriented WebShop setting, WebShop-SOL, OMA improves success nAUC from 0.399 to 0.511. Component ablations and GiGPO and GSPO comparisons further support component separability and cross-optimizer compatibility. These results show that trajectory expansion always defines an implicit weighting rule, but its practical effect depends on how expansion couples to outcomes and optimizer-native updates.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language models are increasingly used as agents that perceive, reason, and act through repeated interaction with external environments. Representative settings include embodied household tasks in ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) and goal-directed web interaction in WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) . These tasks require long-horizon planning under sparse feedback: an agent may make tens of decisions before receiving a verifiable episode-level outcome. Reinforcement learning is therefore a natural post-training paradigm, and group-relative methods such as GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) offer a scalable, critic-free way to learn from multiple attempts at the same task. Extending this success to multi-turn agents, however, requires deciding how a long environment interaction is presented to the language-model optimizer. Multi-turn pipelines use two main materialization patterns. Trajectory-wise optimization concatenates all states and model outputs into one sequence, as in RAGEN ( Wang et al., 2025b ) , preserving full history but causing the effective context to grow with the interaction horizon. Stepwise optimization instead materializes each turn as a response row conditioned on a managed history, keeping individual contexts tractable but expanding each source trajectory into a variable number of optimizer-facing units (Figure 1 (b)). A direct stepwise adaptation of GRPO broadcasts the same trajectory-level relative advantage to every row, obscuring which decisions helped or harmed. GiGPO localizes credit through anchor-state groups ( Feng et al., 2025 ) , while HGPO further organizes steps by historical-context consistency ( He et al., 2026 ) . These methods address which decisions receive credit within a trajectory. Stepwise expansion, however, creates a separate outer-accounting problem: after a source trajectory expands into multiple rows and tokens, how much total statistical and optimization influence should it receive? OMA targets this trajectory-level influence rather than redesigning within-trajectory credit assignment. Figure 1 illustrates how stepwise materialization can reweight source trajectories: an ALFWorld task yields a 14-turn success with 1,663 response tokens and a 50-turn failure with 6,446 tokens, both sampled from the same policy checkpoint. Although each source trajectory yields one outcome, row-based outcome statistics count the outcomes with different multiplicities, while token-proportional reduction assigns different aggregate objective mass (Figure 1 (c)). For this pair, the failed trajectory therefore accounts for 78.1% of the row-expanded outcome population and 79.5% of the token-weighted objective mass. This expansion-induced trajectory reweighting comprises outcome-statistics multiplicity and objective-mass imbalance . Stepwise materialization makes trajectory influence depend implicitly on row and token expansion, without an explicit trajectory-level weighting choice. We introduce Outcome–Mass Alignment (OMA), an outer accounting framework that makes these two choices explicit and controllable. Outcome Alignment constructs the designated outcome statistics over unique source trajectories before assigning the resulting learning signals to response rows. Mass Alignment first aggregates the optimizer-native objective contributions within each source trajectory and then controls its contribution to the outer objective. OMA therefore changes neither environment interaction nor the base optimizer’s native within-trajectory credit, importance-ratio, and clipping mechanisms. Its two components can be enabled independently or jointly and instantiated with multiple group-relative policy optimizers. We evaluate OMA on ALFWorld and WebShop with GRPO, together with GiGPO and GSPO adapter comparisons. ALFWorld exhibits strong success–failure expansion asymmetry: across two model scales, OMA improves matched-horizon success by 7.0–9.4 percentage points. Ordinary WebShop exhibits much weaker or reversed expansion–outcome coupling and correspondingly mixed effects, whereas a success-oriented WebShop setting, WebShop-SOL, improves success nAUC from 0.399 to 0.511. These results support a condition-dependent account: trajectory expansion always defines an implicit weighting rule, but its practical effect depends on how expansion couples to outcomes and optimizer-native updates. Our contributions are: • We identify expansion-induced trajectory reweighting in stepwise group-relative agent training, separating outcome-statistics multiplicity from objective-mass imbalance (Sections 3.1 – 3.3 ). • We introduce Outcome–Mass Alignment, a two-component framework for explicit trajectory-level outcome accounting and objective weighting (Sections 3.2 – 3.4 ). • We validate this condition-dependent account through component ablations on ALFWorld and WebShop and GiGPO and GSPO comparisons (Section 4 ).
