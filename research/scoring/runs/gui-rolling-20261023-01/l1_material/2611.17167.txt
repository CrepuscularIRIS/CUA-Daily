title: Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17167

§Abstract
Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent's actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions. As a teacher, JEV raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy over GRPO from 72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. These results suggest that a poor actor can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Decision models are pretrained models that answer a question about a context with a typed output, such as a choice among given options with a probability for each, instead of generated text, and they do so quickly and cheaply ( Almeida, 2026 ; OpenAI, 2026 ; Perplexity AI, 2026 ) . An LLM agent can therefore query the same decision model in two ways: to choose its next action, or to evaluate an action that has already been taken. Its evaluations can in turn teach a policy during reinforcement learning, by scoring each action of every rollout. This use matters because outcome rewards give every action in a trajectory the same credit ( Harutyunyan et al., 2019 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) , while step-level judgments are otherwise costly, requiring labels for each environment ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) or an LLM judge queried at every action ( Tan et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026a ) . Yet decision models have been evaluated mainly as actors ( Zhu et al., 2026b ; Ling et al., 2026 ) , and in that role they often fail: they are drawn to commands that mention task objects, skip prerequisite subgoals, and complete subgoals in the wrong order ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Ling et al., 2026 ) . These failures do not settle how useful a decision model is when it evaluates rather than acts. On the same ALFWorld evaluation tasks, JEV completes 42.9% of the tasks when it chooses the actions itself. When it instead scores every action of a 1.5B policy during training, after seeing the whole trajectory, the policy completes 82.9% of the tasks if JEV is asked whether each action helped achieve the goal, compared with 72.8% for training with outcome rewards alone. Asked instead whether the task will succeed after each action, JEV yields a policy that completes only 69.3%. The same model can thus act poorly yet teach well, but only when it is asked the right question. This paper asks why a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well, and which queries make this possible . We answer these questions by querying the same decision models in each way on the same tasks (Figure 1 ). As an Actor , a model chooses each action before seeing its effect. As a Judge , it evaluates actions in recorded trajectories, and we vary two choices: what it sees, either the trajectory up to the action ( Prefix ) or the whole trajectory ( Hindsight ), and what it is asked, either whether the task will succeed after the action ( Value ) or whether the action helped achieve the goal ( Progress ). As a Teacher , it applies the same queries to the actions of a policy being trained, and its scores, centered within each rollout group, are added to the outcome advantage of GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) . Each analysis changes one factor, such as the goal, the question, or the context, and holds the rest fixed. We evaluate three decision models and three general LLMs given the same questions, two of them the base LLMs of decision models, on ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) and WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , where some subgoals must be completed in order. Acting fails when an easier subgoal draws the model away from the required one. All six models succeed far less often when one subgoal must be completed before another, such as heating a mug before placing it on a table; JEV, for example, completes 70.8% of tasks with two independent subgoals but only 14.7% of tasks with ordered ones. The models do not lack knowledge of the required step. In states where a single action would complete the easier subgoal, removing that subgoal from the goal moves most of each model’s probability to the required step; for JEV, the probability on the required state change rises from 14.6% to 77.6%. A reminder about the dependency changes success by only -2.8\% to +5.2\% . To act correctly, the model must anticipate that the easier action leaves the task incomplete before any consequence is visible, whereas a judge evaluates the action after its consequences have occurred. Whether a judge avoids this failure depends on what it sees and what it is asked. Seeing the whole trajectory helps Judges recognize contributions that the same models overlook as Actors: these contributions can be unclear from the prefix alone but become apparent from what follows. Seeing the consequences is not enough, however. With the whole trajectory visible, asking whether each action helped separates actions that complete a subgoal from actions that change nothing, with JEV scoring the former 0.36 higher within a trajectory, whereas asking whether the task will succeed gives a gap of only 0.05, because every action in the trajectory then has the same answer, the outcome. All six models show both effects, and the two decision models with a disclosed base LLM judge more accurately than that LLM given the same question, at 28% to 43% lower cost per score.
