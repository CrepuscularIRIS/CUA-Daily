arxiv: 2611.16107 (2611.16107v1)
title: Trained on What Screen Readers Cannot Read: Accessibility Debt in the GUI Corpora Behind Computer-Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16107

§Abstract
Computer-use agents learn to operate mobile interfaces from corpora of captured screens and accessibility trees. The same trees are what a screen reader consumes, so an agent trained on them inherits whatever labelling gaps the underlying applications carry. This paper measures one of those gaps, content-description coverage, in two Android corpora: Rico, collected in 2017, and AndroidControl, collected in 2024. Across 17,373,614 interface elements, 17.74% of the Rico elements that need a content description under our coding rule carry one, against 57.27% in AndroidControl. The difference of 39.5 percentage points holds under five alternative coding rules and when whole apps are resampled. It describes two datasets that also differ in app selection, capture method and platform version; it does not measure change over time. Input controls are the least labelled role in both corpora (2.20% and 27.21%, or 30.99% in AndroidControl if hint text counts as a name). Descriptions in the older corpus are also five times more likely to be placeholders (10.25% against 1.95%), so the difference is not an artefact of developers typing filler. Resolving 26,205 task actions to the elements they touched shows that, among tappable graphics with no text, demonstrations operate elements described 9.0 points more often than the others on the screens they visit, about 3.5 points of it from the choice of element within a screen. That is a selection effect inside the benchmark. A two-coder reliability check (kappa = 0.91 and 0.96) and the full pipeline accompany the paper.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
An agent that operates a phone on someone’s behalf has to know what is on the screen. The usual way to tell it is the platform accessibility tree: a structured record of every element, its class, its bounds, whether it responds to a tap, and its content-desc , the short name a developer supplies for elements that show no text of their own. The large mobile GUI corpora used to train and evaluate these agents are built from that tree. Rico [ 1 ] and AndroidControl [ 2 ] both expose it, and agents built on AndroidControl, AndroidWorld and their relatives consume it directly [ 2 , 3 ] . The accessibility tree was built for screen readers, not for agents, and developers have populated it poorly for as long as anyone has measured it [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] . That dependency has drawn little attention in the agent literature. A missing content description makes an application harder to use with TalkBack. It also removes information from the observation an agent is trained on, evaluated on, and deployed against. We refer to this shared gap as accessibility debt. The framing changes who the problem belongs to. Missing descriptions have been treated as an accessibility defect and addressed through developer tooling and guidelines. If the corpora that train computer-use agents are drawn from the same field, the defect is also a data-quality problem, and the agent research community has both a reason and the resources to address it. This paper measures the debt and asks four questions. • RQ1. What share of the interface elements that require a content description carry one? • RQ2. How large is the difference in that share between a 2017 corpus and a 2024 corpus? • RQ3. Do the elements that task demonstrations touch carry descriptions at a different rate from the other elements on the same screens? • RQ4. Is the share of described elements on an episode’s screens associated with the length of its demonstration? The paper makes four contributions. It provides a measurement of content-description coverage across 17,373,614 elements in two corpora, using a coding scheme that two coders applied independently with \kappa\geq 0.91 . It shows that the difference between corpora holds under five alternative specifications, including one that gives the older corpus the benefit of richer metadata and one that discounts uninformative descriptions, and it reports the second of those as a finding in its own right. It resolves task actions to the elements they touched and shows that demonstrations favour better-described targets, which has a direct consequence for how agent benchmarks should report results. Finally, it releases the codebook, the pipeline and the statistical reports so that the same figures can be computed for any corpus that exposes an accessibility tree. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section II places the work in the context of GUI corpora, accessibility measurement and benchmark validity. Section III describes the two corpora and Section IV the coding scheme, its reliability check and the statistical approach. Section V answers the four questions and reports the sensitivity analysis. Sections VI to VIII discuss what the results mean for three audiences, state the limitations, and conclude.

## III Data
This section describes the two corpora and the population of elements the analysis draws from. The corpora were chosen because both expose the accessibility tree and because they were collected seven years apart. Rico [ 1 ] contributes 66,261 screens from a 2017 crawl of more than 9,300 free Android applications across 27 Play Store categories, with view hierarchies and app-level metadata including category and average rating. Rico is distributed under a research-use agreement that forbids redistribution, so we release parsing code and checksums rather than data. AndroidControl [ 2 ] contributes 7,623 task episodes from ten of its twenty published shards (00000 to 00009), covering 49,822 screen observations, of which 26,228 received a click or long press (hereafter a click) that could be resolved to an element. The data name an episode’s app only when the demonstration opens it, so we identify apps from the package prefix of resource identifiers. This yields 700 application packages; 251 of the episodes with elements needing a label carry no usable identifier. Each step records an accessibility forest and an action. Actions carry no element reference, only coordinates, so we resolve every click to an element by point-in-bounds hit testing over the visible window stack, taking the deepest node that contains the point and breaking ties on drawing order and then on smallest area. When that node is not clickable, the click is assigned to its nearest clickable ancestor; this applied to 43.71% of resolved clicks. A further 18.42% stayed on a node that is not clickable, because none of its ancestors was. The rule resolved 99.92% of clicks. Ten shards exceed every sample-size threshold in the analysis plan by more than an order of magnitude, so the remaining shards were not parsed. The release does not group shards by app, split or date: the ten parsed shards hold between 48.95% and 50.82% of every tenth of the released episode-identifier range and 50.75% of the released test episodes, and label coverage (Section IV-C ) ranges from 55.84% to 59.23% across them. ## IV Method
This section defines the unit of analysis, the two coded variables, the strict subpopulation used for the headline claims, the reliability procedure, and the statistical approach. The scheme is implemented as code and stated in prose in a codebook that ships with the pipeline. IV-A Unit of analysis and inclusion The unit is a single node in the view hierarchy (Rico) or accessibility tree (AndroidControl). A node enters the analysis population when it is flagged visible to the user and has positive area. Both conditions matter. In Rico, 48.7% of nodes have zero or negative area, and 1,716 of those are still flagged visible, so the visibility flag alone would admit elements that are not on screen in any useful sense. The enabled attribute is not part of the primary rule, because a disabled control is still announced by a screen reader and still needs a name. Section V-E reports the alternative. IV-B Element role Roles are assigned from the class name alone, matching on normalised suffixes so that the rule survives AppCompat, Material, androidx and vendor subclasses. Seven roles are used: input , button , image , web , text , container and other . Rules are evaluated in that order and the first match wins, because several input classes end in -button and one button class ends in -imagebutton . Rico hierarchies carry an ancestors field with the full superclass chain; AndroidControl trees do not. Classifying Rico with the richer signal and AndroidControl with the poorer one would make any cross-corpus difference partly an artefact of metadata rather than a fact about applications. The primary classifier therefore uses the class name for both, and the superclass variant is reported as a robustness check. IV-C Label status Each included element receives exactly one of four values, assigned by the decision procedure in Fig. 1 . Let d(e) be the element’s content description after whitespace and the placeholder strings none , null and nan are removed, and let t(e) be its visible text treated the same way. An element needs a description when \begin{split}\mathrm{needs}(e)={}&\neg\mathrm{exempt}(e)\wedge t(e)=\varnothing\\ &{}\wedge\big(\mathrm{image}(e)\vee\mathrm{interactive}(e)\big),\end{split} (1) where \mathrm{exempt}(e) holds for roles container and web , and \mathrm{interactive}(e) holds when the element is clickable or long-clickable or has role input or button . Containers are structural and are traversed rather than announced; a web view hides its contents from the tree; an element that shows text is announced by that text; and an image always needs a description because a screen reader has nothing else to say. An element is labelled when d(e)\neq\varnothing , whether or not it also shows text. It is unlabelled when d(e)=\varnothing and \mathrm{needs}(e) holds; this is the debt. Otherwise it is named_by_text if it shows text and not_applicable if it shows nothing. Coverage for any population P is then C(P)=\frac{\left|\{e\in P:\mathrm{needs}(e)\wedge d(e)\neq\varnothing\}\right|}{\left|\{e\in P:\mathrm{needs}(e)\}\right|}. (2) The denominator is the needs-a-label population, never the count of elements coded labelled or unlabelled . The latter would report exempt roles at 100% by construction, since no container is ever coded unlabelled . The construct measured throughout is therefore content-description coverage under the stated rule. It is not the same as whether a screen reader can name an element, because TalkBack can also take an accessible name from other fields, most often the hint text of an input field; Section V-A reports coverage with hint text counted. IV-D The icon-like subpopulation We define an element as icon-like when it is a tappable graphic with no text of its own: role image or button , t(e)=\varnothing , and \mathrm{interactive}(e) . This is the strictest unit in the paper and the population where a missing description is a defect rather than a judgement call. Every headline claim is restated on it. IV-E Reliability Two authors independently hand-coded a stratified sample of 300 elements (60 screens per corpus, up to 10 elements per screen, stratified by role and by automated label status so that rare cells are represented), each blind to the other and to the automated assignments. Agreement was measured with Cohen’s \kappa [ 21 ] , \kappa=\frac{p_{o}-p_{e}}{1-p_{e}}, (3) where p_{o} is observed agreement and p_{e} is agreement expected by chance, with percentile intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the coded items and the Landis and Koch conventions for interpretation [ 22 ] . The two coders reached \kappa=0.96 , 95% CI [0.94,0.98] and 97.0% raw agreement for element role, and \kappa=0.91 , 95% CI [0.87,0.95] and 93.3% raw agreement for label status. The 28 items on which they differed were not adjudicated: the human codes serve only to check the scheme and enter no analysis, and every result below rests on the automated assignment. Agreement was lowest at the boundary between not_applicable and unlabelled , which accounted for 11 of the 20 label-status disagreements. That boundary is where the decorative-versus-meaningful judgement sits, and Section VII returns to it. Because the automated coder is deterministic and cannot disagree with itself, its agreement with each human coder is reported as a validity check rather than as reliability: \kappa=0.98 and 0.99 for role, and 0.96 and 0.95 for label status. Eleven automated validation checks on vocabularies, screen geometry and internal contradictions passed without exception. IV-F Statistical approach Proportions carry Wilson score intervals [ 23 ] , which stay well-behaved at the low rates seen in the older corpus. Differences between proportions carry Newcombe hybrid score intervals [ 24 ] and are accompanied by Cohen’s h [ 25 ] , h=\left|\,2\arcsin\sqrt{p_{1}}-2\arcsin\sqrt{p_{2}}\,\right|, (4) reported as a magnitude with direction given in the text and read against the conventions of 0.20, 0.50 and 0.80 for small, medium and large. Contingency tables carry Cramér’s V with bootstrap intervals, and ordinal comparisons carry Cliff’s \delta [ 26 ] . RQ3 uses permutation tests with 10,000 label shuffles and the (r+1)/(m+1) convention, alongside parametric companions. Because elements are nested in screens and screens in apps, the headline proportions, the cross-corpus difference and the RQ3 differences also carry percentile intervals from 5,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples of whole screens and, for coverage and the difference, of whole apps. RQ3 adds a paired within-screen difference and a permutation test that shuffles the touched flag only within screens. The null of that test is centred on the difference that screen composition alone would produce, so it tests the part of each difference that arises within screens. Where a family of tests is reported, p -values are adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [ 27 ] . RQ4 fits a negative binomial model of demonstration length, \log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{steps}_{i}]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\,\mathrm{density}_{i}+\beta_{2}\log n_{i}, (5) where \mathrm{density}_{i} is the share of labellable elements on episode i ’s screens that carry a description and n_{i} is the total number of included elements across those screens, so that \exp(\beta_{1}) is an incidence rate ratio for label density at a fixed element count. Because n_{i} is summed over the screens an episode visits, it grows with the number of steps. Model 2 adds AndroidControl’s published split (train, validation, test) and clusters standard errors by app. Model 3 keeps the split and the clustering but takes both density and element count from the episode’s first in-app screen, defined as observation 1 after an open_app action and observation 0 otherwise, for episodes where that screen has at least five elements needing a label. Exposure and control are then fixed before the demonstration unfolds. All analyses are seeded, and every number in this paper can be traced to a generated report.
