arxiv: 2609.22798 (2609.22798v2)
title: Expert-Play Contouring Control: Faster-than-Demonstration Planning from Slow Expert and Fast Play
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22798

§Abstract
Expert demonstrations often specify what a robot should do, but not how fast it can do it. Imitation Learning (IL) inherits demonstration timing, while directly accelerating the learned motion can fail when faster execution changes the robot-object dynamics. We study faster-than-demonstration execution as a dynamics-aware control problem and introduce Expert-Play Contouring Control (EPCC), which combines slow expert demonstrations with fast, non-expert play. Expert demonstrations train a latent trajectory generator whose predictions are reparameterized into a time-independent contour of successful task progression, while play trains a world model (WM) of fast-action outcomes. At deployment, our proposed planner uses the WM to optimize actions that makes maximize progress along the expert-derived contour while penalizing deviation from the intended task evolution. Averaged across three visuomotor manipulation tasks, EPCC achieves a $2.0\times$ the throughput of the IL baseline, including $2.2\times$ that of the throughput of the strongest acceleration baseline on a task with interaction-sensitive object dynamics. Our analysis shows that the gains concentrate where faster execution changes robot-object evolution. Together, our results highlight a simple yet effective principle: demonstrations provide task intent, while play data provides the dynamic coverage needed to execute that intent faster.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Imitation Learning (IL) provides a scalable framework for acquiring complex manipulation skills by avoiding hand-designing task objectives or system dynamics [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . However, policies trained from human demonstrations often inherit their slow and cautious execution, limiting throughput [ 4 ] . This limitation has motivated the study of faster-than-demonstration execution [ 5 , 6 ] . A key challenge is that faster execution can induce robot-object interactions that are absent from slow demonstrations, pushing closed-loop rollouts outside the demonstrations’ support. Naïvely executing an IL policy’s predicted motion faster can therefore degrade task performance: the same motion need not induce the same task evolution at higher speed [ 7 , 8 ] . For example, a careful push that stops an object at its target goal on a low-friction surface may instead cause it to overshoot the target when executed faster ( ). Because slow demonstrations provide no direct evidence of fast-action outcomes, most prior work mitigates the failure by identifying phases during which the motion generated by the policy can be executed faster without changing the outcome. They use proxy cues for precision, such as motion complexity or semantic phase, and temporally compress the identified segments through action downsampling, faster tracking or retiming [ 5 , 6 , 9 , 7 , 8 ] . This strategy assumes that low-precision segments remain task-valid when the same underlying motion is executed faster. However, proxy-based schedulers do not directly predict the resulting robot-object evolution, making them either conservative or unreliable and thereby limiting throughput [ 10 ] . More fundamentally, they can miss speedup opportunities in interaction-sensitive phases, where faster execution invalidates the original motion and preserving success requires adapting the motion itself. In this paper, we investigate whether cheap non-expert play can provide the missing fast-regime action-outcome evidence, without requiring hand-designed analytic dynamics, thus preserving the scalability of IL. Our key insight is to separate task intent from fast-execution dynamics : slow expert demos define the successful task evolution to preserve, whereas fast play reveals how actions change the robot-object evolution at higher speed ( ). We integrate these complementary sources through a single planning objective that maximizes progress along the demonstrated path while keeping predicted rollouts close to it. Instead of prescribing when and how much to accelerate, our method advances as far as the learned dynamics predict is feasible, enabling speedup during contact-sensitive interactions. We call our approach Expert-Play Contouring Control ( EPCC ) . Given the current observation, a latent contour generator trained from expert demonstrations produces a time-independent path of successful task progression, while a world model [ 11 , 12 ] learned from fast play predicts how candidate actions change the robot-object system. Our Latent Contouring Controller uses these predictions to plan actions that maximize progress along the expert-derived contour. By replanning online, EPCC accelerates whenever faster execution remains consistent with the intended task evolution and adapts when it does not. We evaluate EPCC against an IL policy and three faster-than-demonstration baselines on three visuomotor manipulation tasks. Averaged across tasks, EPCC achieves 2.0\times the throughput of the IL policy, the largest relative improvement among all evaluated methods. Its advantage is greatest when faster actions substantially alter robot-object interactions: on an interaction-sensitive task, it achieves 2.3\times the throughput of the strongest acceleration baseline. Across the three tasks, its contact-phase speedup is, on average, 1.15\times that of the strongest baseline. Our ablations suggest that these gains arise from using play-trained dynamics to predict fast-action outcomes and optimizing actions beyond temporal rescaling of IL policy outputs. We further characterize how the speed and amount of play data affect acceleration, finding that more data improves reliability. To summarize, our contributions are: 1. We introduce Expert-Play Contouring Control, which combines an expert-derived latent task contour generator with a visual world model learned from non-expert play to optimize fast, task-progressing actions. 2. Across three tasks, EPCC achieves the largest macro-averaged throughput gain over DP ( 2.0\times ), including 2.3\times the throughput of the strongest baseline on the interaction-sensitive Slippery-PushT task. 3. We characterize when interaction-aware acceleration is beneficial, when synthesizing actions beyond temporally transforming policy outputs is important, and how the distribution and amount of play data affect performance.

## III Problem Formulation
We consider a visuomotor manipulation system with observation space \mathcal{O} and action space \mathcal{A} . The robot is given two offline datasets. The first is a set of successful expert demonstrations, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}=\left\{\tau_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}},\qquad\tau_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}^{(i)}=\left\{\left(o_{t}^{(i)},a_{t}^{(i)},o_{t+1}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{t=0}^{T_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}^{(i)}-1}. (1) Each expert trajectory completes the task, but need not execute it at the fastest speed attainable by the robot. The second dataset consists of play interactions, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}=\left\{\tau_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}},\qquad\tau_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}^{(j)}=\left\{\left(o_{t}^{(j)},a_{t}^{(j)},o_{t+1}^{(j)}\right)\right\}_{t=0}^{T_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}^{(j)}-1}. (2) Play need not complete the task or exhibit expert behavior; it provides experience of robot-object interactions under actions that cover the target fast-execution regime. We do not assume access to successful fast expert demonstrations. Our objective is to synthesize a policy that rewards task success and short task completion time: \displaystyle\pi^{\star}\in\argmin_{\pi} \displaystyle c_{1}\,\mathbb{E}\left[T(\tau_{\pi})\mid\mathsf{S}(\tau_{\pi})=1\right] (3) \displaystyle-c_{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{S}(\tau_{\pi})\right],\qquad c_{1},c_{2}>0. Here \tau_{\pi} is a closed-loop rollout, \mathsf{S}(\tau_{\pi})\in\{0,1\} indicates task success, and T(\tau_{\pi}) is completion time for a successful rollout. The two terms respectively favor shorter successful rollouts and higher success rates, with c_{1} and c_{2} controlling the success-speed trade-off. A target policy is faster than demonstration if it completes successful rollouts faster than an imitation policy trained on expert demonstrations, while maintaining a comparable success rate. The central challenge is that the two datasets provide different pieces of this objective. Expert data identifies task evolutions that lead to success, whereas play data identifies how the robot-object system responds to actions in the fast regime. Our method composes these complementary sources to infer fast actions without requiring fast successful demos.

## IV Method
In this section, we introduce EPCC to solve ( 3 ). We first learn a latent world model from fast play to predict robot-object dynamics, and a contour generator from expert demonstrations to produce an expert-derived latent contour from the current observation. We then combine them through receding-horizon contouring control, which optimizes actions to maximize progress along the latent contour while keeping predicted rollouts close to it. Please see Figure 2 for overview. IV-A Learning Latent World Model from Play Data Following action-conditioned latent world models [ 11 , 12 ] , we encode visual observations into a compact latent state using h_{\phi}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z} , z_{t}=h_{\phi}(o_{t}), (4) and learn a latent dynamics model f_{\theta}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z} , \hat{z}_{t+1}=f_{\theta}(z_{t},a_{t}). (5) The world model is trained on play transitions using a next-latent prediction objective, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WM}}(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(o_{t},a_{t},o_{t+1})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{\textnormal{p}}}}\left\|f_{\theta}\!\left(h_{\phi}(o_{t}),a_{t}\right)-h_{\phi}(o_{t+1})\right\|_{2}^{2} (6) The encoder may be fixed or learned jointly with the dynamics, with representation-specific regularization when needed. At deployment time, an action sequence A_{t:t+H-1}=\left(a_{t\mid t},a_{t+1\mid t},\ldots,a_{t+H-1\mid t}\right) (7) induces the predicted latent rollout \displaystyle\hat{z}_{t\mid t} \displaystyle=h_{\phi}(o_{t}), (8) \displaystyle\hat{z}_{t+k+1\mid t} \displaystyle=f_{\theta}\left(\hat{z}_{t+k\mid t},a_{t+k\mid t}\right),\qquad k=0,\ldots,H-1. (9) Because the encoder observes both the robot and the manipulated scene, the learned dynamics capture task-relevant robot-object evolution. IV-B Latent Contour Generation from Expert Demonstration We construct a latent contour generator \mathcal{C_{\psi}} that maps the current latent state to a time-independent reference path in latent space. The generator consists of two components: a learned latent trajectory generator G_{\psi} and a path reparameterization. The former predicts the sequence of latent states that should be reached, while the latter removes their timings. Learning a latent trajectory generator. We freeze the world-model encoder h_{\phi} and use it to encode expert demonstrations into the same latent space used by WM. For an expert trajectory segment of length L+1 , we define Z_{t:t+L}=\left(z_{t},z_{t+1},\ldots,z_{t+L}\right),\qquad z_{t+k}=h_{\phi}(o_{t+k}). (10) We then train a latent trajectory generator G_{\psi} to predict the future latent sequence from the current latent state: \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LTG}}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{Z_{t:t+L}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{\textnormal{e}}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{traj}}\left(G_{\psi}(z_{t}),Z_{t+1:t+L}\right)\right], (11) where \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{traj}} denotes the training objective of the trajectory generator (e.g., diffusion, flow). At inference, the current observation is encoded as z_{t}=h_{\phi}(o_{t}) , and the generator predicts G_{\psi}(z_{t})=\left(\tilde{z}_{t+1\mid t},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{t+L\mid t}\right). (12) Anchoring the prediction at the current latent state gives the generated latent trajectory \tilde{Z}_{t:t+L\mid t}=\left(z_{t},\tilde{z}_{t+1\mid t},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{t+L\mid t}\right). (13) Here, L denotes the reference horizon. We choose L to be sufficiently long to expose enough future task progression for meaningful acceleration. Path reparameterization. The generated latent trajectory \tilde{Z}_{t:t+L\mid t} captures the desired future task evolution, but remains time-indexed and therefore inherits the timing of the expert demonstrations. We convert it into a time-independent latent contour \bar{z}_{t}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{Z} that preserves the ordering of the intermediate states while removing timing. Using the latent distance d_{\mathcal{Z}}(z,z^{\prime}):=\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{2} , we assign each generated state a normalized cumulative path coordinate \displaystyle s_{t,k} \displaystyle:=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{\mathcal{Z}}\left(\tilde{z}_{t+j\mid t},\tilde{z}_{t+j-1\mid t}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}d_{\mathcal{Z}}\left(\tilde{z}_{t+j\mid t},\tilde{z}_{t+j-1\mid t}\right)}, \displaystyle k=1,\ldots,L, (14) with \tilde{z}_{t\mid t}=z_{t} and s_{t,0}=0 . For any continuous progress s\in[s_{t,k},s_{t,k+1}] , we define the contour by piecewise-linear interpolation, exploiting latent straightening property [ 12 ] , \bar{z}_{t}(s)=\bigl(1-\alpha\bigr)\tilde{z}_{t+k\mid t}+\alpha\tilde{z}_{t+k+1\mid t},\quad\alpha=\frac{s-s_{t,k}}{s_{t,k+1}-s_{t,k}} (15) Thus, \bar{z}_{t}(s_{t,k})=\tilde{z}_{t+k|t} , with \bar{z}_{t}(0)=z_{t} and \bar{z}_{t}(1)=\tilde{z}_{t+L\mid t} . The resulting contour preserves the geometry and ordering of the generated task evolution while leaving its traversal speed to the controller. We denote this deterministic path reparameterization by \bar{z}_{t}(\cdot)=\mathcal{P}\left(\tilde{Z}_{t:t+L\mid t}\right) (16) Together with the learned trajectory generator, this defines the latent contour generator \mathcal{C_{\psi}}(z_{t}):=\mathcal{P}\left(z_{t},G_{\psi}(z_{t})\right)=\bar{z}_{t}(\cdot), (17) which produces the time-independent reference contour used by EPCC for progress-aware control. IV-C Latent Contouring Control Given the current latent state z_{t}=h_{\phi}(o_{t}) , the latent contour generator produces a time-independent reference \bar{z}_{t}(\cdot)=\mathcal{C_{\psi}}(z_{t}). (18) EPCC then uses the play-trained world model to select actions that make maximal progress along this contour while limiting deviation from it. This follows the central principle of MPCC [ 20 , 19 ] . We formulate this objective using contouring, progress, and action regularization costs. Contouring cost. Let s_{t+k\mid t}\in[0,1] denote the progress variable at prediction step k . It selects the corresponding reference state \bar{z}_{t}(s_{t+k\mid t}) along the latent contour. We define the latent contouring error and its finite-horizon cost as \displaystyle e^{c}_{t+k\mid t} \displaystyle:=\hat{z}_{t+k\mid t}-\bar{z}_{t}(s_{t+k\mid t})\ \mathrm{\textnormal{and}} (19) \displaystyle J_{\mathrm{cont}}(A_{t},S_{t}) \displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{H}\left\|e^{c}_{t+k\mid t}\right\|_{Q_{c}}^{2},\ \mathrm{\textnormal{where}} (20) \displaystyle A_{t} \displaystyle:=\left(a_{t\mid t},\ldots,a_{t+H-1\mid t}\right) (21) is the candidate action sequence and S_{t}=(s_{t\mid t},\ldots,s_{t+H\mid t}) is the corresponding progress sequence. Progress cost. Let \nu_{t+k\mid t}\geq 0 denote the progress increment, with s_{t+k+1\mid t}=s_{t+k\mid t}+\nu_{t+k\mid t}. (22) We encourage rapid traversal using J_{\mathrm{prog}}(V_{t}):=-w_{p}\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\nu_{t+k\mid t}, (23) where V_{t}=(\nu_{t\mid t},\ldots,\nu_{t+H-1\mid t}) . Since each generated contour is anchored at the current state and s_{t\mid t}=0 , this is equivalent to J_{\mathrm{prog}}=-w_{p}s_{t+H\mid t} . Regularization cost. We penalize large action magnitudes and abrupt action changes: J_{\mathrm{act}}(A_{t}):=\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\left\|a_{t+k\mid t}\right\|_{R_{a}}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{H-1}\left\|\Delta a_{t+k\mid t}\right\|_{R_{\Delta}}^{2}, (24) where \Delta a_{t+k\mid t}=a_{t+k\mid t}-a_{t+k-1\mid t} . The smoothness weight R_{\Delta} is task-dependent. Optimization. At each control step, EPCC jointly optimizes the action sequence and progress evolution: \displaystyle A_{t}^{\star},V_{t}^{\star}\in\underset{A_{t},V_{t}}{\arg\min} \displaystyle J_{\mathrm{cont}}(A_{t},S_{t})+J_{\mathrm{prog}}(V_{t})+J_{\mathrm{act}}(A_{t}) (25) \displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.} \displaystyle\hat{z}_{t\mid t}=h_{\phi}(o_{t}), \displaystyle\hat{z}_{t+k+1\mid t}=f_{\theta}\left(\hat{z}_{t+k\mid t},a_{t+k\mid t}\right), \displaystyle s_{t+k+1\mid t}=s_{t+k\mid t}+\nu_{t+k\mid t}, \displaystyle s_{t\mid t}=0,\qquad 0\leq s_{t+k\mid t}\leq 1, \displaystyle a_{t+k\mid t}\in\mathcal{A},\qquad 0\leq\nu_{t+k\mid t}\leq\nu_{\max}, \displaystyle k=0,\ldots,H-1. The contouring cost preserves the expert-derived task evolution, while the progress cost drives the predicted rollout forward as quickly as permitted by WM. After solving Eq. ( 25 ), the controller executes the first T_{a} optimized actions, A_{t}^{\mathrm{exec}}=\left(a_{t\mid t}^{\star},\ldots,a_{t+T_{a}-1\mid t}^{\star}\right),\qquad 1\leq T_{a}\leq H, (26) and replans from the resulting observation. At each replanning step, both the latent contour and the optimized action-progress sequence are updated from the latest visual observation. The full algorithm is in Algorithm 1 .
