title: CoDA: Co-Evolution of Data Engines and Action Policies
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22362

§Abstract
Generative simulation can automate robot environment construction, but physically valid environments are not necessarily useful for training. The challenge is to identify environments that address a policy's failures and supervision that helps it overcome them. We introduce CoDA, a framework in which a simulation data engine and an action policy co-evolve through a shared heterogeneous memory of environment construction, policy failures, and learning progress. The memory compiles validated diagnoses from a vision-language critic into generation programs that recreate difficulties and training programs that help the policy overcome them. We further propose Text2potential to convert these diagnoses into staged potentials for action-chunk value learning, providing dense supervision for policy updates while preserving the task's optimal policy under standard shaping assumptions. Measured learning progress guides further generation and program refinement. Across four manipulation task families, CoDA improves out-of-distribution success from 5.3% after supervised fine-tuning to 27.6%, exceeding the strongest closed-loop baseline by 9.5 percentage points with the same generation budget. On LIBERO-Pro, 15 rounds of generation and training raise success from 25% to 84% under position perturbations and from 1% to 76% under instruction perturbations.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Simulation has become a primary source of robot manipulation data, but building simulated environments has long been the bottleneck: each one requires a task design, semantically appropriate assets, a plausible layout, and a training signal, often all specified by hand [ 38 ] . Generative simulation removes much of that cost. Coding agents now synthesize scenes, articulations, and task specifications directly [ 38 , 39 , 43 , 37 , 25 ] , and language models author the reward functions that accompany them [ 42 , 48 ] . Environments are no longer scarce. A central challenge remaining is deciding which environments to build next and what supervision will help the policy to learn from them. Existing engines sample variation from hand-specified ranges [ 29 ] , refine a scene until it is physically valid [ 39 , 43 ] , or allocate budget by per-task success rate [ 10 ] . These signals indicate whether an environment is valid and how well the current policy performs, but do not by themselves measure how much the policy improves through training. It records repairs needed to make scenes valid, together with policy failure diagnoses grounded in rollout evidence and localized to one generative factor and one trajectory phase. The memory tracks learning progress for recurring difficulty modes to guide subsequent generation. This evidence is compiled into separate programs for scene generation and policy training, with the latter specifying subgoals and learning tools [ 38 , 42 ] . As tasks involve longer sequences of dependent subtasks, sparse success signals can slow value learning, while designing effective dense rewards can require manual effort. We therefore introduce Text2potential , which compiles a failure diagnosis into a potential over the simulator state and uses it to densify value targets for action chunks [ 15 , 7 ] . The resulting value estimates guide policy updates through weighted regression on the policy’s own rollouts, favoring action chunks with higher estimated value. Across four manipulation tasks, CoDA achieves 27.6% out-of-distribution success, compared with 5.3% for the supervised fine-tuning baseline. With the same budget for generating environments, a loop guided by per-task success rates achieves 18.1%, while open-loop variants yield 8.5%. Extending our loop to more rounds increases out-of-distribution success to 42.1%. Moreover, on LIBERO-Pro, training on source-task variants generated by CoDA increases success from 25% to 84% on position generalization and from 1% to 76% on task generalization. Finally, we observe that reusing memory across policy backbones transfers accumulated experience, enabling a second backbone to increase out-of-distribution success from 3.8% to 21.1%. In summary, the contributions of our work are: • Closed-loop co-evolution. We introduce CoDA, a framework that co-evolves a simulation data engine with a trainable action policy. Failure diagnoses grounded in policy rollouts guide environment generation and training, while measured learning progress determines which difficulties to revisit. • Shared heterogeneous memory. We introduce a memory shared by the generator and policy that combines episodic evidence, reusable difficulty modes, and procedural programs. The same diagnosis compiles into a generation program that recreates the difficulty and a training program that helps the policy overcome it. Both programs are indexed by difficulty mode, making them reusable across tasks. • Dense supervision through Text2potential. Text2potential compiles failure diagnoses into potentials over simulator states that provide dense targets for learning the value of action chunks [ 15 ] . Reward shaping requires potential evaluations only at chunk endpoints and preserves the policies that maximize the original task return. • Improved out-of-distribution generalization. Across four manipulation task families, CoDA achieves 27.6% out-of-distribution success, compared with 5.3% after supervised fine-tuning and 18.1% for success-rate-guided generation using the same accepted-environment budget.
