title: Kolab: Benchmarking Human-Agent Collaboration in Long-Horizon Workflows
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21109

§Abstract
When humans collaborate with AI agents on long-horizon tasks, what happens to the agents' contributions? For solving long-horizon tasks, humans place greater importance on ongoing interaction with AI agents (e.g., revising plans) than on an agent's ability to complete the task in a single attempt. However, most agentic benchmarks have focused more on automation. Strong performance on such benchmarks does not imply effective real-world collaboration. Professional workflows offer a useful setting, as they often involve interdependent steps. To address this gap, we introduce Kolab, a new dataset of 115+ hours of screen recordings of 50 professionals from 40 occupations collaborating with agents on realistic, long-horizon problems. Kolab also contains participants' collaboration and output quality judgments. Our analysis reveals diverse collaboration patterns, highlighting the importance of when and how professionals coordinate with agents. A collaborative agent must do two things that automation benchmarks never test: anticipate whether experts will adopt, modify, or reject its contribution once it leaves the chat window to guide its next steps, and assess collaboration quality from the unfolding workflow without constant expert feedback. Our benchmark operationalizes both: (1) forecasting how professionals use agent contributions at diverse workflows, and (2) verifying their judgments of collaboration satisfaction and output quality. Findings show that frontier models, both agentic and non-agentic, overestimate collaboration with experts and struggle to distinguish between high- and low-quality collaboration. Longer workflow context does not consistently improve these predictions. As frontier agents cannot yet understand long-horizon collaboration with experts, we position Kolab as a valuable dataset for studying human-agent collaboration grounded in real professional workflows.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
As AI agents become more capable of complex reasoning and knowledge work, recent research has examined whether they can complete such tasks autonomously in a single attempt, given a single instruction ( Patwardhan et al., 2026 ; Vidgen et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026b ; Merrill et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) . However, real-world problem solving may not follow this autonomous setting: human goals can evolve, earlier decisions shape subsequent work, and errors may require intervention before they propagate ( Tack et al., 2026 ) . In professional work, for example, workers formulate requirements, produce intermediate artifacts, revise decisions, and move between agents and domain-specific tools. Thus, strong autonomous task performance may not translate into effective human-agent collaboration ( Zou and others, 2025 ; Mozannar et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2026 ) . Despite the need for studying effective human-agent collaboration, existing datasets provide limited grounding in real collaborative workflows. They are often not publicly released, rely on predefined tasks constructed or sourced by researchers, or focus on a single professional domain such as software development ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Shao et al., 2026b ) . As a result, benchmarking long-horizon human-agent collaboration workflows remains underexplored. To address this gap, we introduce Kolab , a dataset of screen recordings capturing human-agent collaboration on long-horizon professional problems. We represent each workflow as a graph of semantically meaningful human and agent actions and their dependencies (Figure 1 ), allowing us to understand when and how collaboration occurs in the workflow. In this example, an architect completes a long-horizon task in five steps, and agent outputs can influence later steps. A task delegated in Step 2 (source node) produces information (target AI node) that is later modified and used in Step 3 ( Modify ). Human responses to agent outputs may also occur outside the chat interface. The architect ultimately adopts and executes the resulting plan in Revit, an action not directly observable by the agent. Our analysis reveals that human-agent collaboration workflow varies in when and how professionals engage with AI agents. Burstier workflows are associated with longer periods of independent work before interacting with AI, suggesting that professionals may organize their collaboration around periods of independent work rather than continuous AI interaction. Workflows with more concurrent human-agent activities are associated with lower collaboration satisfaction, suggesting that parallel activities may introduce additional coordination costs. Beyond characterizing collaboration, we ask whether frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and agents can understand their own place in a professional’s workflow. We frame this as two capabilities that a deployed collaborative agent needs but that autonomous benchmarks never measure. Anticipating the fate of a contribution (RQ1). In long-horizon work, an agent’s output is rarely the end of the story: it is adopted, modified, or rejected, often outside the chat interface ( e.g., an architect incorporating an agent’s plan in Revit). An agent that can recognize what happened to its output after the fact (1a) could turn naturalistic screen recordings into feedback signals for training and for auditing deployed agents, without asking the expert to label anything. An agent that can forecast this response at delivery time (1b)—before the human reacts—could act proactively: asking a clarifying question when rejection is likely, flagging the parts it expects to be edited, or escalating to the human when its contribution carries risk that would propagate to later steps ( Oh et al., 2026 ) . Because such forecasts depend on how much of the collaboration the agent remembers, we vary the available history from the preceding steps (1c) to the entire workflow so far (1d), which directly informs how much context a long-running or computer-use agent must retain to model its collaborator. Judging the collaboration itself (RQ2). Expert ratings of collaboration satisfaction (2a) and output quality (2b) are the ground truth for whether human-agent collaboration works, yet they are expensive to collect and unavailable at deployment. If an MLLM could recover these ratings from the workflow alone, it could serve as a process-level reward model for training collaborative agents, as an automatic monitor that detects friction or low-quality outcomes in deployed systems, and as a scalable evaluator for professional tasks where no reference answer exists. Crucially, such a verifier must separate genuinely successful interactions from dissatisfying ones, rather than echoing the predominantly positive ratings.
