title: SkillHorizon: Constructing Long-Horizon Agent Tasks through Skill Composition
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14966

§Abstract
Long-horizon agents must complete workflows in which later activities depend on earlier outputs and changes to shared state. A key challenge for evaluation is to expand task requirements and their dependencies while keeping tasks executable and outcomes verifiable. We introduce SkillHorizon, a benchmark that constructs long-horizon tasks through skill composition in seven business environments. Its generators use domain-specific rules to turn reusable skill procedures into concrete business activities. These activities are connected through input-output dependencies, shared resources, and state updates, allowing workload and coordination demands to grow together. Successful reference executions validate task feasibility and measure interaction workload, while programmatic evaluators assess local completion and whole-task success. We evaluate five language models on 1,050 tasks with reference interaction workloads ranging from 64K to 1M tokens. Across these scales, mean local-goal completion falls from 90.4% to 46.8%, as completed work fails to keep pace with growing requirements. At larger workloads, agents more often submit unsuccessful final answers with substantial work unfinished and tool-call allowance remaining. Trace analysis also identifies cases where scripts execute successfully but their outputs fail downstream business checks. SkillHorizon provides a configurable testbed for studying sustained progress, dependency coordination, and complete task delivery in growing workflows. Our code is available at https://github.com/KnowComp/SkillHorizon.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Language agents increasingly carry out business workflows that involve multiple activities and sustained interaction with tools and data ( Xu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025 ) . The outputs of one activity become inputs to later stages, while resource commitments shape subsequent choices. Changes in the environment may also require completed work to be reviewed or revised. Agents must therefore maintain valid intermediate results and coordinate activities as they work toward the final goal. Studying this ability requires executable tasks that can grow in required work and dependencies, with persistent state and verifiable outcomes. Existing research provides foundations for long-horizon evaluation and skill reuse. WorkArena++ combines atomic tasks into enterprise workflows with reference executions and validation functions ( Boisvert et al., 2024 ) . TheAgentCompany evaluates work across business applications, code execution, and communication in a simulated workplace ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , while AgentLongBench studies information use over extended interaction histories ( Fang et al., 2026 ) . In parallel, Voyager develops an executable skill library, and SkillsBench evaluates packaged skills across tasks ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2026c ) . AgentSkillOS further studies skill retrieval and graph-based coordination ( Li et al., 2026a ) . These studies support complex workflows and procedural reuse. Despite this progress, existing evaluations offer limited insight into how agents perform as more business activities are added and linked to earlier work. Studying this scaling requires a construction method that expands task goals and dependencies together. Each new activity must specify its required inputs, expected outputs, and use of shared resources. State updates must also define when earlier results need to be revised. At the same time, resource allocations and completion checks must match the expanded task. The key challenge is to build these larger workflows from reusable skills while keeping them executable and their outcomes verifiable. We introduce SkillHorizon, a benchmark that constructs long-horizon tasks through skill composition in seven business environments spanning finance, field maintenance, social campaigns, logistics, research, documentation, and manufacturing. Domain-specific generators turn reusable skill procedures into business modules with concrete objects and goals. They connect modules through input–output dependencies and configure shared resources and state updates. Adding modules increases required work, while their connections determine which earlier results must be reused, combined, or revised. The generators create task goals and initial states together. Each environment provides public tools, skill resources, and programmatic evaluators. Successful reference executions validate task feasibility and measure interaction workload; evaluators check local-goal completion and whole-task acceptance. Figure 1 summarizes the pipeline. We evaluate five language models on 1,050 tasks with reference interaction workloads ranging from 64K to 1M tokens, yielding 5,250 model–task executions. Mean local-goal completion falls from 90.4% to 46.8% across these scales, as completed work fails to keep pace with growing requirements. We also analyze failed final answers with recorded completion and tool use. At 1M, 54.0% of these runs complete less than 25% of local goals while retaining over 50% of the tool-call allowance. The corresponding share is 2.4% at 64K. Trace analysis further identifies cases where scripts execute successfully but their outputs fail downstream business checks. These findings reveal difficulties in sustaining progress and using local results to complete larger workflows. Our contributions are threefold: 1. Skill-based task construction. We introduce a method that builds business activities from reusable skills and connects them through input–output dependencies, shared resources, and state updates. The method supports configurable expansion of required work and coordination demands. 2. An executable and scalable benchmark. We build seven persistent business environments with task generators, public tools, skill resources, reference executions, and programmatic evaluators. These resources support reproducible evaluation across task instances and workload scales. 3. An empirical study of long-horizon execution. We evaluate five models across increasing workloads and identify gaps between required and completed work, including unfinished execution with unused tool allowance and downstream failures of script outputs.
