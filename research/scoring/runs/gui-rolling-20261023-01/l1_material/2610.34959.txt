title: GUIFlow: Temporal Redundancy-Aware Incremental Inference for GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34959

§Abstract
GUI agents built on vision-language models interact with continuously evolving interfaces, yet conventional inference repeatedly recomputes representations of largely overlapping observations and histories. This mismatch makes multi-step interaction costly, while changes in context, interface content, and action arguments prevent straightforward cache reuse. We introduce GUIFlow, a framework for full-pipeline incremental inference in multi-image GUI interaction. Our central insight is that advancing a decision need not require rebuilding all supporting representations: each interaction step can instead selectively update computational states maintained across the trajectory. To support new decisions as the context evolves, GUIFlow adapts how past computation is reused: historical KV states are position-aligned and injected into the current context; change signals guide selective updates of current-observation states in the vision encoder and language model; and earlier action responses serve as speculative drafts verified by the target model. Together, these mechanisms enable cross-step reuse throughout visual encoding, prefill, and decoding without additional training, a separate draft model, or changes to the model-visible inputs and action interface. Experiments demonstrate up to 3.39x geometric-mean speedup across offline multi-image benchmarks and up to 3.49x in online, closed-loop interaction on OSWorld, while maintaining task performance broadly comparable to the dense baseline.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
AI agents are moving beyond answering questions toward executing tasks in digital environments, including everyday applications on computers and mobile devices. Graphical user interfaces (GUIs) provide a broadly applicable way to interact with existing software without relying solely on application-specific APIs. Advances in vision-language models ( Bai et al., 2025 ) have enabled GUI agents to interpret screens and act on them through repeated observation and action ( Qin et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2026a ; Xie et al., 2024 ) . However, completing multi-step tasks requires sustained model inference, whose cumulative latency limits the responsiveness and practical usability of these agents. The continuity of interaction offers an opportunity to reduce this cost. Successive decision steps share supporting information: historical screenshots recur across overlapping context windows, neighboring observations share persistent interface regions, and action responses often repeat structural patterns. For example, editing a text field changes only part of a screen, while the surrounding interface and much of the interaction history remain available from the preceding step. Figure 1 provides evidence at both the image and representation levels: across the evaluated tasks, more than 70\% of observation pairs have RGB-image error no greater than 5\% at each temporal gap from one to four steps. The illustrated KV-drift maps show similar spatial patterns of change in an early and the final layer, while the trajectory example shows substantial state stability between major interface changes. These observations suggest that advancing a decision can build on persistent computational states and selectively update what changes, rather than rebuild all supporting representations at every step. Recent efficiency methods exploit temporal structure through KV-cache compression, as in GUI-KV and ST-Lite ( Huang et al., 2025 ; Zhou et al., 2026 ) , or learned visual-patch reduction, as in ReVision ( Abaskohi et al., 2026 ) . Reducing visual context, however, does not by itself make past computational states reusable under new conditions or ensure benefits across the full inference pipeline, raising two broader challenges. The first is maintaining reusable computation under evolving interactions : past computational states must support new decisions even as their context changes, requiring a distinction between what remains reusable and what needs updating. The second is turning local reuse into pipeline-wide acceleration : savings within individual stages may be limited by computation elsewhere and by the overhead of reuse itself. Addressing this challenge requires extending reuse across visual encoding, prefill, and decoding while translating computational savings into actual latency reductions. These challenges motivate our central question: Can GUI agents accelerate real-world interaction through full-pipeline incremental inference, selectively updating existing computational states as the interaction evolves? To address these challenges, we introduce GUIFlow , a temporal redundancy-aware framework for full-pipeline incremental inference in GUI agents. GUIFlow treats each interaction step as a selective update to computational states maintained across the trajectory, rather than an independent inference pass. For historical inputs, it injects position-aligned KV states for recurring screenshots into the current context, enabling approximate reuse beyond unchanged prefixes. For current observations, change signals guide selective updates in the vision encoder and deeper language-model layers, while source tracking and reuse limits control state staleness. For action generation, earlier responses serve as speculative drafts rather than fixed answers, with the target model verifying draft tokens and correcting mismatches. Together, these mechanisms extend reuse across visual encoding, prefill, and decoding, reducing repeated computation beyond any single stage. GUIFlow requires neither additional training nor a separate draft model, and leaves the model-visible inputs and action interface unchanged. We evaluate GUIFlow on AITW ( Rawles et al., 2023 ) and Mind2Web ( Deng et al., 2023 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ) for offline multi-image navigation and on OSWorld ( Xie et al., 2024 ) for online closed-loop interaction, covering both fixed trajectories and evolving environments. GUIFlow achieves up to 3.39\times geometric-mean speedup across the offline benchmarks and up to 3.49\times speedup on OSWorld, while maintaining task performance broadly comparable to the dense baseline. Across these settings, mean per-step processing latency is reduced by factors of 1.94 – 2.55 . These results show that selective state updates can turn temporal redundancy into both computational savings and lower execution latency.
