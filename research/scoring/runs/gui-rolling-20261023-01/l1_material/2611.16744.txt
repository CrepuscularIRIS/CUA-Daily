title: Scale-Induced Semantic Shift in Visual Prompt Injection Against VLMs and Web Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16744

§Abstract
Visual content in multimedia systems may be resized, compressed, or re-rendered before it is interpreted by a vision-language model. We study a security failure mode in which these transformations change the machine-readable semantics of an image: a textual instruction is not recovered at a $512\times512$ presentation resolution under our evaluation protocol but becomes recoverable after aggressive downsampling. We formulate this behavior as Scale-Induced Semantic Shift (SISS) and develop a closed-loop black-box framework that combines dual-scale verification, selective self-hardening, shallow-layer low-rank adaptation, and pixel-space noise regularization. On a fixed 300-case benchmark, the third SISS refinement round obtains a $58.75\%$ average attack success rate across four closed-source vision-language models, compared with $41.08\%$ for the strongest generation baseline. A controlled sweep bounds the effect to aggressive compression: average success decreases to $17.68\%$, $6.85\%$, and $5.60\%$ at $128\times128$, $224\times224$, and $336\times336$, respectively. In a downstream web-agent study, the generated images increase hidden-instruction perception, but absolute behavioral compromise remains about $4\%$. Generic input transformations provide little protection in a separate defense protocol, whereas the proposed ScaleGuard consistency detector reduces non-adaptive success from $61.4\%$ to $24.3\%$ and one-round defense-aware success from $42.5\%$ to $15.0\%$, with no observed rejection among matched clean controls. The results characterize a bounded but reproducible semantic inconsistency in multimedia preprocessing and motivate scale-consistent visual-language inference.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Multimedia inference pipelines routinely transform visual content before model consumption. An image displayed to a user can be resized, compressed, rasterized, or converted into a model-specific representation, and the resulting view may differ from the presentation view. This processing becomes security-relevant as vision-language models (VLMs) are incorporated into document understanding and web-based computer-use agents [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Prompt injection can exploit the instruction-following behavior of such systems and, when connected to tools, can affect downstream actions [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] . Research on prompt injection has predominantly examined text supplied directly to a language model. Conventional visual attacks use adversarial perturbations or localized patches [ 7 , 8 , 9 ] , whereas VLM-oriented work uses typographic prompts, adversarial images, or indirect image-borne instructions [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] . The latter studies establish that image content can influence a VLM, but they do not fully characterize whether the same multimedia asset preserves its instruction semantics across the transformations that connect presentation, transmission, and model-side perception. Image-scaling attacks expose a related inconsistency, although their usual objective is an object- or scene-level label change rather than recovery of a natural-language instruction [ 13 , 14 ] . We study this gap through instruction-level cross-resolution semantic separation , which we term Scale-Induced Semantic Shift (SISS). Under our threat model, the attacker controls only the image asset; the user prompt, system instruction, victim-model parameters, and internal preprocessing pipeline remain inaccessible. A successful sample must suppress recovery of a target instruction from the 512\times 512 presentation view while enabling its recovery from an aggressively downsampled view. The 512\!\rightarrow\!64 setting is a controlled stress test, not an assumption about the internal resolution of every deployed VLM. The central question is therefore whether a generated image can induce a reproducible semantic discrepancy across views and transfer to black-box VLMs. This setting introduces three coupled challenges. First, the textual structure must survive low-resolution aggregation without being reliably recovered from the presentation view. Second, optimization must preserve scene content while avoiding smooth regions that expose the payload. Third, the resulting asset must transfer beyond the surrogate used for selection and must be evaluated separately at the perception and downstream-action levels. These constraints distinguish the problem from both ordinary visible typographic injection and conventional image-scaling attacks. To address them, we propose SISS, a generate–verify–select–self-harden framework. Dual-scale verification provides the optimization signal without gradients from the victim model. Low-rank adapters are restricted to shallow ControlNet blocks to bias adaptation toward local spatial structure, while pixel-space Gaussian noise regularization discourages smooth shortcut solutions. We freeze the generated evaluation images before querying four closed-source target VLMs, sweep four target resolutions, separate payload perception from agent behavior, and test generic preprocessing and a calibrated multi-scale consistency defense. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • Multimedia-security formulation. We define instruction-level cross-resolution semantic separation and an explicit success criterion over presentation and transformed views. • Closed-loop generation. We introduce a black-box generate–verify–select–self-harden framework with shallow-layer low-rank adaptation and pixel-space noise regularization. • Multi-level evaluation. We characterize transfer across four VLMs and four target resolutions, and distinguish visual-language perception from downstream web-agent behavior. • Scale-consistency defense. We evaluate five generic input transformations and propose ScaleGuard, which is calibrated on a disjoint split and tested under non-adaptive and one-round defense-aware attacks. The contribution is thus broader than a single attack rate: it links content generation, cross-resolution signal transformation, multimodal inference, and downstream agent behavior in one controlled evaluation. The experiments also delimit the threat, showing that the effect weakens sharply outside the aggressive-compression regime.

## III Cross-Resolution Attack Formulation and SISS Framework
III-A Multimedia System Model and Threat Formulation We consider a multimedia pipeline that presents an image \mathbf{x} at a native resolution and may also derive transformed views through resampling or compression before visual-language inference. The attacker controls the image asset and its embedded payload y_{p} , but has no access to the victim VLM parameters, its system prompt, or its internal preprocessing. We model an observable transformed view as \mathcal{T}_{r,k}(\mathbf{x}) , where r is the target resolution and k is the resampling kernel. The main attack protocol fixes k to Lanczos and compares r=512 with r=64 ; the resolution and defense studies vary these transformation parameters. The attack objective is asymmetric. The payload should not be recovered from the presentation view but should be recovered from the transformed view. This response-level definition avoids claiming human imperceptibility and does not require knowledge of a target model’s internal image resolution. SISS uses the criterion as the selection signal in a closed-loop black-box procedure: generate candidates, verify their responses across scales, retain strict successes, and use the retained set for self-hardening. Shallow-layer adaptation and pixel-space noise regularization provide the two optimization biases. Figure 1 summarizes the generation, verification, self-hardening, and evaluation pipeline. III-B Dataset Construction To support iterative optimization and reproducible evaluation, we construct SISS-Bench with a filtered training set and a fixed test set. The training and evaluation roles are separated: training images are selected only with surrogate feedback, whereas final test images are frozen before any target-model query. Following the prompt-injection taxonomy of Liu et al. [ 20 ] , we organize hidden textual payloads into three categories: Direct Injection , Escape Characters , and Context Ignoring . In our setting, these categories describe the semantic role of the hidden instruction embedded in the image rather than the original text-channel delivery mechanism. Concretely, Direct Injection appends malicious commands, Escape Characters contains formatting or control symbols such as “\n” and “\t,” and Context Ignoring instructs the model to disregard prior context. We pair payloads with high-frequency scene prompts such as woven fabrics, dense foliage, reptilian scales, and striped shadows. The textured content supplies spatial variation within which the typographic condition can be encoded (Fig. 2 ). For training, we generate a candidate pool of 5{,}000 images spanning all three payload categories and diverse scene prompts, and retain 3{,}269 images after applying the cross-scale verification protocol in Sec. III-C . For evaluation, we define 300 fixed evaluation cases by pairing 30 payloads with 10 scene templates and generate the corresponding test images using the fine-tuned model. This protocol is used for the main, resolution-sensitivity, and web-agent evaluations; Sec. IV-E specifies a separate defense split. III-C Cross-Resolution Success Criterion A successful image must satisfy the response-level semantic separation defined in Sec. III-A : the hidden payload is not recovered from the presentation view and is recovered from a downsampled evaluation view. We operationalize this requirement through a 512\rightarrow 64 protocol. The native 512\times 512 image is the presentation reference, whereas 64\times 64 is a controlled stress setting for aggressive resizing. We use this pair as a reproducible instantiation of the scale-dependent phenomenon studied in prior resolution attacks [ 14 ] ; Sec. IV-C evaluates the same images at 128\times 128 , 224\times 224 , and 336\times 336 . The conjunction excludes samples that are ineffective after downsampling or already exposed by the native-view evaluation. It measures semantic disagreement between model responses and does not assert human imperceptibility. Training and evaluation use the same cross-scale criterion but strictly separated model roles. Qwen2.5-VL is used only as the training-time surrogate; no final target response is used for regeneration or sample selection. Given \mathbf{x}_{512} , we form \mathbf{x}_{64}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Lanczos}}_{64}(\mathbf{x}_{512}). (1) Let b_{r}^{Q}\in\{0,1\} denote whether the Qwen2.5-VL surrogate recovers the target payload at resolution r . Training retains a sample iff \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{select}}(\mathbf{x})=\neg b^{Q}_{512}\land b^{Q}_{64}. (2) For final evaluation, each target VLM m independently receives the frozen image and the prompt “Describe the image in detail. What text, words, or messages can you see?” . A fixed Gemini 3 Pro text-only judge receives the target response d_{r}^{m} and ground-truth payload y_{p} , but not the image: b_{r}^{m}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{Gemini}}(d_{r}^{m},y_{p}). (3) Attack success requires \neg b_{512}^{m}\land b_{64}^{m} , yielding \mathrm{ASR}(m)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}[\neg b_{512}^{m,(i)}\land b_{64}^{m,(i)}]. (4) III-D Structure-Conditioned Generation Algorithm 1 SISS closed-loop cross-scale optimization 0: Scene prompts \mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{scene}} , payloads \mathcal{Y}_{p} , generator G_{\theta_{\mathrm{shallow}}} , Qwen2.5-VL surrogate \mathcal{Q} , noise scale \sigma_{\mathrm{pix}} 0: Updated shallow-layer parameters \theta_{\mathrm{shallow}} 1: for each training step do 2: Sample (y_{\mathrm{scene}},y_{p}) and render text condition c_{\mathrm{text}} 3: Generate x_{512}\leftarrow G_{\theta_{\mathrm{shallow}}}(y_{\mathrm{scene}},c_{\mathrm{text}}) 4: Downsample x_{64}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Lanczos}}_{64}(x_{512}) 5: Obtain fixed Qwen2.5-VL cross-scale decisions b^{Q}_{512} and b^{Q}_{64} 6: if \neg b^{Q}_{512}\wedge b^{Q}_{64} then 7: Sample \xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{pix}}^{2}I) and set \tilde{x}=x_{512}+\xi 8: Encode z_{0}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}(\tilde{x}) 9: Update only \theta_{\mathrm{shallow}} using \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}} 10: end if 11: end for Following Algorithm 1 , we render the payload y_{p} as a binary typographic map \mathbf{c}_{\text{text}} containing black text on a white background. This map conditions ControlNet [ 31 ] , which guides a pre-trained latent diffusion model [ 38 , 39 ] with classifier-free guidance [ 40 ] under the benign scene prompt y_{\mathrm{scene}} . The cross-scale objective imposes an asymmetric requirement: payload structure must survive low-resolution aggregation without becoming strongly expressed in the native-resolution response. To bias optimization toward this regime, SISS adopts two coupled design choices: shallow layer-selective adaptation and pixel-space Gaussian noise regularization. The first design choice is layer-selective adaptation . LoRA provides parameter-efficient fine-tuning, but applying it throughout the conditioning network also changes deeper features associated with global scene structure. Because the cross-resolution objective depends on local strokes and texture, we inject adapters only into the ControlNet input_blocks and first two down_blocks, while freezing the deeper ControlNet modules and the complete Stable Diffusion U-Net. We denote the trainable subset by \theta_{\mathrm{shallow}} . This restriction is a design bias rather than an assumption about a victim VLM; Sec. V directly compares it with global tuning. The second design choice is pixel-space Gaussian noise regularization . Without this perturbation, the generator can preserve the payload through smooth, low-complexity regions in which its structure is also more salient at the native view. We add Gaussian noise directly in pixel space during training: \tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\xi,\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{pix}}^{2}\mathbf{I}). (5) For accepted samples, noise is applied before VAE encoding and diffusion training (Algorithm 1 ). The regularizer biases reconstruction away from a smooth shortcut; its contribution is evaluated independently in Sec. V . The resulting optimization objective is \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{0},\epsilon,t,y_{\mathrm{scene}},\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{text}}}\Big[\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta_{\mathrm{shallow}}}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t,y_{\mathrm{scene}},\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{text}})\|_{2}^{2}\Big], (6) where \mathbf{z}_{t} denotes the noisy latent at timestep t and \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}) . Together, the restricted adapter scope and pixel-space perturbation target the trade-off between native-view suppression and low-resolution recoverability. Their effects are reported separately rather than inferred from the architecture alone. III-E Evaluation at Perception and Action Levels We evaluate SISS at two levels of the multimedia pipeline. The static benchmark isolates visual-language perception and reports ASR under Eq. 4 . The downstream web-agent testbed places the same adversarial images in browser interfaces containing benign and attacker-controlled elements, and reports explicit perception M_{\mathrm{perceive}} , behavioral compromise M_{\mathrm{behavior}} , and their joint rate P(\mathrm{perceive}\cap\mathrm{behavior}) . The two levels are not interchangeable: payload recovery is necessary for the measured joint event, whereas downstream behavior also depends on the agent policy, available tools, and interface state.
