title: PEAM: On-device Communication Agent for Personalized, Efficient and Autonomous Mobile Network Configuration
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14855

§Abstract
The growing deployment of compact large language models on smartphones is enabling autonomous on-device decision-making, such as emerging mobile GUI agents. However, many mobile decisions, such as network configuration when users switch apps or connectivity conditions change, must satisfy stringent latency constraints to preserve user experience. In this paper, we propose PEAM, an on-device LLM-based communication agent that autonomously generates personalized network-configuration rules executable within milliseconds. Specifically, PEAM first fine-tune the on-device LLM with a dataset constructed from manually annotated real-user data to integrate the communication knowledge into the local model. PEAM then operates in two phases. In the rule-generation phase, which is performed when the device is idle, such as overnight, PEAM analyzes historical network-configuration records in batches and reflects across batches to consolidate consistent evidence, reduce local bias, and derive robust configuration rules. To enable more accurate and stable rule generation over time, PEAM further constructs a user profile from high-confidence rules to capture the user's network preferences and incorporates this profile into each rule-generation cycle. In the rule-execution phase, PEAM is invoked when the communication context changes, such as when users switch apps or connectivity conditions change, and searches the rule library to identify and execute the appropriate rule. Experiments demonstrate that PEAM, based on a fine-tuned 4B model, achieves 99.13% correct network configuration decisions, while 92.50% of the rules generated by PEAM are valid, outperforming the traditional expert-rule based method by 4.41% and 84.50%, respectively.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have significantly improved their reasoning, planning, and autonomous decision-making capabilities. Meanwhile, the growing deployment of compact models on mobile and edge devices has made LLM-based intelligence increasingly accessible in resource-constrained environments ( Jiang et al. 2020 ; Chen et al. 2025 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ) , as demonstrated by emerging mobile GUI agents ( Wen et al. 2025 ; Zhang et al. 2025b ; Yang et al. 2025 ) . These trends open up new opportunities for using on-device LLMs to support complex decision-making tasks in mobile systems. One representative task is mobile network configuration, which dynamically selects and adjusts communication resources and settings, such as access networks, WiFi access points, and SIM cards, according to application requirements, network conditions, device states, and user contexts. Unlike many offline decision-making tasks, mobile network configuration directly affects user experience and often lies on a latency-critical execution path. A practical solution should therefore be personalized , because different users may prefer different configurations under similar communication conditions; efficient , because configuration decisions must often be completed within stringent latency constraints; and autonomous , because the device should learn and apply user preferences without repeated manual intervention. Existing network-configuration methods mainly fall into three categories: expert-rule (ER)-based, supervised-learning (SL)-based, and reinforcement-learning (RL)-based methods. ER-based methods remain widely used in practical systems. They encode communication knowledge into hand-crafted constraints over metrics such as signal strength, uplink/downlink latency, and quality of service (QoS), and then map such conditions to switching actions ( Sunita et al. 2015 ; Miyim et al. 2021 ; Wu et al. 2022 ) . Although ER-based methods are efficient at runtime, their strategies are primarily determined by expert-designed heuristics, making them difficult to personalize according to individual user behavior. To improve adaptability, recent studies have explored SL-based and RL-based methods. SL-based methods learn correlations between communication observations and user actions from labeled data ( Masi and Wac 2020 ; Paropkari et al. 2022 ; Peng et al. 2023 ) , whereas RL-based methods formulate network switching as a sequential decision process and optimize policies through reward-driven interaction ( Tan et al. 2010 ; Wang et al. 2024 ; Xie et al. 2023 ; Lu et al. 2025 ; Wu et al. 2024 ; He et al. 2023 ) . However, in these methods, user preferences are still implicitly encoded in hand-crafted rules, training labels, reward functions, or model parameters. As a result, they are difficult to interpret, update, and continually adapt as user behavior evolves. More recently, researchers have begun to apply LLMs to network-configuration tasks. Existing studies have explored LLM-based solutions for WiFi roaming ( Lee et al. 2025 ; Nagai et al. 2026 ) and cellular handover ( Li et al. 2026 ; Jeong et al. 2026 ) , demonstrating the potential of LLMs to interpret heterogeneous communication observations and generate corresponding decisions. However, these approaches largely employ LLMs as reactive decision engines that directly map current observations to configuration actions. They do not systematically distill long-term user behavior into persistent, interpretable configuration rules, and they still rely on LLM inference during the decision-making path. Consequently, existing LLM-based methods struggle to simultaneously achieve personalization, efficiency, and autonomy for on-device mobile network configuration. To address these limitations, we propose PEAM, an on-device LLM-based communication agent for personalized, efficient, and autonomous mobile network configuration. The key idea of PEAM is to decouple complex LLM reasoning from latency-critical execution. Instead of invoking the LLM for every online configuration decision, PEAM performs LLM-based reasoning offline when the device is idle, distills historical user behavior into persistent condition–action rules, and executes these rules online through lightweight retrieval. As illustrated in Figure 1 , PEAM models personalized network configuration as two coupled problems: understanding why a user prefers a particular configuration from historical records and determining what configuration action should be executed under the current communication context. This design introduces two key challenges on resource-constrained devices. First, how can a compact on-device LLM reliably infer user intents and generate configuration rules from long and heterogeneous historical records? ## Methodology
As illustrated in Figure 2 , PEAM consists of four modules: Record Acquisition, Periodic Update, User-Level Local Memory, and Real-Time Execution. We consider personalized mobile network configuration over a sequence of update periods indexed by t . In each period, PEAM first collects user-initiated configuration records, then performs offline LLM-based rule generation when the device is idle, updates the local rule and profile memories, and finally executes matched rules online without invoking the LLM. Record Acquisition The Record Acquisition module runs continuously in the background and monitors user-initiated network-configuration operations. Once an operation is detected, PEAM records the communication context immediately before the operation, the configuration action selected by the user, and the resulting context after the operation. The records collected during the t -th update period are represented as D_{t}=\left\{d_{i}^{t}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}},\qquad d_{i}^{t}=\left(x_{i}^{t,-},a_{i}^{t},x_{i}^{t,+}\right), (1) where N_{t} denotes the number of collected records; x_{i}^{t,-} and x_{i}^{t,+} denote the communication contexts immediately before and after the i -th operation, respectively; and a_{i}^{t} denotes the configuration action selected by the user. Each context contains information such as time, location, foreground application, device configuration, and network conditions. All records are stored locally until the next periodic update. The collected record set D_{t} , together with the user profile p_{t-1} generated in the previous period, serves as the input to Periodic Updating, which is then used to infer the user’s underlying decision intents and generate personalized configuration rules. Periodic Updating Data Clustering Records collected within an update period are heterogeneous and may exceed the context capacity of the on-device LLM. PEAM therefore groups \mathcal{D}_{t} into batches using feature-weighted HDBSCAN ( Campello et al. 2015 ) . For two records d_{a} and d_{b} , the dissimilarity of the k -th feature and their overall weighted Gower distance are defined as \displaystyle\delta_{k}(d_{a},d_{b}) \displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{|x_{a}^{k}-x_{b}^{k}|}{R_{k}},&k\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{num}},\\[5.0pt] \mathbb{I}(x_{a}^{k}\neq x_{b}^{k}),&k\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cat}},\end{cases} (2) \displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbf{W}}(d_{a},d_{b}) \displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\delta_{k}(d_{a},d_{b})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}}. where R_{k} is the range of the numerical feature k , \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{num}} and \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cat}} denote the numerical and categorical feature sets, and w_{k} controls the contribution of each feature. The feature weights \mathbf{W} and HDBSCAN hyperparameters \Theta are jointly optimized once on labeled training data using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator ( Akiba et al. 2019 ) . Let \mathbf{c}^{*} denote the ground-truth intent labels and \hat{\mathbf{c}}\equiv\hat{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{W},\Theta) denote the clustering assignments. The optimization objective is \displaystyle Q(\mathbf{W},\Theta) \displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\operatorname{ARI}\bigl(\mathbf{c}^{*},\hat{\mathbf{c}}\bigr)+\lambda_{2}\operatorname{NMI}\bigl(\mathbf{c}^{*},\hat{\mathbf{c}}\bigr), (3) \displaystyle(\mathbf{W}^{*},\Theta^{*}) \displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathbf{W},\Theta}Q(\mathbf{W},\Theta). where \lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1 . The optimized parameters are fixed after training and reused during deployment. Applying them to \mathcal{D}_{t} yields a cluster set \mathcal{C}_{t}=\{C_{t,j}\}_{j=1}^{M_{t}} , where each cluster forms an independent analysis batch and sparse observations are treated as noise. Record Analysis Although clustering reduces the input length and groups records with similar contexts, raw device logs remain difficult for a compact LLM to interpret directly. PEAM therefore converts each cluster into a concise semantic representation before inference. Stable device attributes, such as SIM information and data-usage settings, are presented once as shared configuration context. Dynamic attributes, such as time, location, and network conditions, are summarized using the mean for numerical features and the mode for categorical features. Each contextual transition is then represented as K:V^{-}\rightarrow V^{+} , where K denotes an attribute and V^{-} and V^{+} denote its values before and after the configuration operation, respectively. As illustrated in Figure 3 , the record-analysis prompt combines semantic records with the content constraint \Gamma_{c} , the structural constraint \Gamma_{s} , and the previous user profile p_{t-1} . The content constraint guides the model to identify shared behavioral patterns, infer the most plausible user intent and rationale, determine corresponding decision conditions, and verify the compatibility of the resulting configuration action. The structural constraint \Gamma_{s} enforces a consistent output comprising the inferred intents, confidence scores, rationales, condition-action rules, and a summary. For the j -th cluster, the record-analysis process is formulated as \displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{t,j},\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{t,j}\right)=\operatorname{LLM}\bigl(\Gamma_{c},\Gamma_{s},\mathbf{p}_{t-1},\operatorname{Sem}(C_{t,j})\bigr). (4) where \operatorname{Sem}(\cdot) denotes semantic transformation, and \hat{\mathcal{I}}_{t,j} and \hat{\mathcal{R}}_{t,j} denote the locally inferred intents and candidate configuration rules, respectively. Reflection Clustering cannot guarantee that all records associated with the same underlying intent fall into a single batch. As a result, batch-level outputs may contain incomplete intents, redundant rules, or conflicting actions caused by limited local evidence. PEAM address this problem through cross-batch reflection ( Shinn et al. 2023 ; Madaan et al. 2023 ) . Guided by a fixed reflection constraint \Gamma_{r} , the LLM identifies intents and rules with shared semantics across batches, merges recurring strategies and complementary conditions to consolidate distributed evidence. It further examines candidate rules with overlapping conditions but incompatible actions, suppressing conclusions supported only by isolated batches. Semantically duplicated rules are also eliminated to prevent fragmented observations from being misinterpreted as independent user preferences. The cross-batch reflection process is formulated as \displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{t},\mathcal{R}_{t})=\operatorname{LLM}\Big(\Gamma_{r},\big\{(\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{t,j},\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{t,j})\big\}_{j=1}^{M_{t}}\Big), (5) where \mathcal{I}_{t} and \mathcal{R}_{t} denote the consolidated user intents and configuration rules for the current update period. User-Level Local Memory PEAM maintains a user-level local memory consisting of a personalized rule base and a semantic user profile. Each rule r contains a condition–action pair and a confidence score q_{r} . When a rule is retrieved for execution, PEAM asks the user whether to accept the proposed configuration. Acceptance increases the rule confidence, whereas rejection decreases it. Rules whose confidence falls below a predefined threshold are discarded. This feedback mechanism allows PEAM to retain reliable strategies while removing rules that no longer match the user’s preferences. Detailed rule states and transition mechanisms are provided in the Appendix. To summarize user preferences beyond individual condition-specific rules, PEAM periodically constructs a semantic user profile from high-confidence rules. The profile captures both scenario-specific preferences, such as preferred configurations under particular applications, times, locations, and network conditions, and broader behavioral tendencies, such as sensitivity to data usage and preference for connection quality. Let B_{t}^{hc} denote the high-confidence rules maintained during the t -th update period. Guided by a fixed profile-generation constraint \Gamma_{p} , PEAM generates the profile as p_{t}=\operatorname{LLM}\left(\Gamma_{p},B_{t}^{hc}\right), (6) where p_{t} denotes the updated user profile. PEAM regenerates the profile from the latest reliable rules and incorporates it into the next offline rule-generation cycle, providing higher-level preference context for subsequent record analysis. Real-Time Execution When the communication context changes, PEAM captures the current application, temporal, spatial, device-configuration, and network conditions, and retrieves applicable active rules from the local rule base. Since these rules are generated in advance during periodic updates, the real-time execution path does not invoke the LLM. Instead, PEAM directly compares the current context with the conditions specified by each rule and retrieves the rules whose conditions are satisfied. If multiple rules match, PEAM resolves the conflict using a two-level priority scheme. It first selects the rule with the largest number of matched condition predicates, since this rule provides the more specific description of the current context. If multiple rules contain the same number of matched conditions, the rule with the highest confidence is selected. Finally, PEAM applies the action associated with the selected rule to the device. This retrieval-based design avoids costly real-time model inference and enables millisecond-level online decision-making.
