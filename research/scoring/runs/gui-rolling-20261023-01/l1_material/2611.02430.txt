title: InteractionBench: Measuring Timing, Task Accuracy, Whisper and Volume, and Grounding in Real-Time Voice Systems
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02430

§Abstract
Most benchmarks for real-time voice systems focus on one aspect, such as turn-taking, instruction following, paralinguistic control, or conversational memory, each with its own data and set of systems. We present InteractionBench, a benchmark and evaluation harness that measures four dimensions on the same systems from the same recorded user audio: conversational timing, spoken-task accuracy, whisper and volume, and groundedness in conversations of more than twenty turns. The benchmark contains 221 items per system in thirteen scenarios. Before any system runs, we fix the expected floor-control actions for spoken tasks by rule and take the expected answers from scripted dialogues. We score whispering from the audio signal. We evaluate GPT-Live, GPT-Realtime, Gemini Live, Moshi, PersonaPlex, Freeze-Omni, and a turn-based cascade in 1,519 graded runs. Among the six full-duplex systems, timing and spoken-task accuracy are rank-correlated ($ρ= 0.83$), while the cascade has the highest spoken-task score (38/50) and the second-lowest timing score. No system corrects a user's grammar within the required window. Only Gemini Live whispers on most requests (8/10). Hosted systems pass 67-88% of groundedness probes, compared with 14-35% for open full-duplex models. Timing is measured where the client hears each system, so the timing of hosted systems includes network delay: for GPT-Live, whose server reports its own clock, removing a delay of about 1.5 s lowers its timing score from 86% to 56%. We also document where the protocol differs across systems, including the text instructions.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Full-duplex voice systems listen while they speak. They must handle events that turn-based assistants can ignore: pauses inside a user’s sentence, short backchannels during the system’s reply, and interruptions that replace the current request. Hosted services such as GPT-Realtime, Gemini Live, and GPT-Live now offer streaming interfaces for this kind of interaction. Most benchmarks for these systems focus on one aspect: turn-taking [ 15 , 14 , 1 , 9 ] , instruction following over multiple turns [ 17 , 39 , 31 , 22 ] , task completion [ 27 , 16 ] , paralinguistic control [ 40 , 18 , 11 ] , or conversational memory [ 10 , 8 ] . Because each uses its own data, protocol, and systems, results on different aspects of the same system are rarely compared. The newest hosted systems are also hard to include. Instruct-FD, for example, left out GPT-Realtime because its interface expects the client to signal interruptions [ 31 ] . GPT-Live raises a different problem: its streaming audio carries no timestamps and omits silent frames, so its timeline has to be rebuilt before any timing can be measured. We built InteractionBench to measure four aspects on the same seven systems: conversational timing, spoken-task accuracy, whisper and volume, and groundedness in long conversations. It scores both how a system manages the conversation and what it says. Every system hears the same recorded user audio, and one harness drives hosted APIs, locally served models, and an offline cascade through the same recording and grading path (Figure 1 ). The benchmark has 221 items per system in thirteen scenarios. Spoken-task scorecards fix the expected floor-control action by rule and the expected answer from a scripted dialogue, and groundedness probes are asked inside live conversations (§ 3 , § 4 ). We evaluate seven systems, including GPT-Live, for which we found no earlier published benchmark results, and compare the results with findings from prior benchmarks (§ 5 , § 6 ). Because every system is timed where the client hears it, the timing of hosted systems includes network delay. For GPT-Live, the only system whose server reports its own clock, removing this delay lowers its timing aggregate from 86% to 56% (§ 6.3 ). Scores are computed automatically, except for seven verdicts we corrected by hand and five probes we left out after reading them (§ 5.1 and § 7 ). Section 7 also lists where the protocol differs across systems, and Appendix G shows how the scores change under other thresholds.
