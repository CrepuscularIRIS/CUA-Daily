title: REA: A Routed Embodied Agent Runtime for Asynchronous Brain-Cerebellum Control
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42053

§Abstract
Foundation models are becoming remarkably reliable at high-level reasoning, yet they remain too slow and costly to serve as fine-grained controllers. Conversely, fast policies like VLAs and whole-body controllers offer more efficient reflexes, but struggle to generalize. While pairing a deliberative brain (foundation models) with a reactive cerebellum (fast policies) seems natural for embodied systems, two obstacles remain: methods are built and scored on incompatible stacks, and their asynchronous coordination is difficult. For example, an agent that calls the VLA in the agentic loop can stall the fast execution, while a VLA that calls the agent on errors tends to re-trigger fallbacks repeatedly or completely miss silent failures. To address this, we propose REA (Routed Embodied Agent), a unified runtime where a deliberative System 2, an embodied router System 1 and reactive System 0 policies cooperate under a single golden rule - no running policy waits on the brain. Instead of querying the brain at each failure, REA employs a route memory that stores each policy's measured success ahead of time, a boundary-triggered judge gate that consults the brain on policy execution, and a shared-budget escalation ladder for bounded recovery. Evaluated on 3,000 test episodes across 60 LIBERO-Pro tasks, REA reaches 95.9% success and beats the best single-agent baseline in its own policy pool (94.7%) while reducing control steps by 25% and control time by 17%. In a separate 100-episode RoboTwin 2.0 study, REA achieves 84% success with VLA policies and 86% with VLA+code, compared with 80% for the strongest single VLA baseline MEDex. These results demonstrate learned and programmatic policies operating within the same runtime. REA also transfers to BEHAVIOR-1K and a real Unitree G1 humanoid, achieving the highest observed success among the compared methods.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Foundation-model agents have become capable embodied brains : a coding agent can write a manipulation program that calls segmentation, grasp planning and inverse kinematics [ 1 , 2 ] , and a language or vision–language model can decompose a command [ 3 , 4 , 5 ] or tell whether a task succeeded [ 5 , 6 ] . These abilities are expensive: in our runs ASPIRE ’s repair loop [ 2 ] spent a median of 43 min per LIBERO task, and a Codex + GPT-6 pipeline consumed 89M input tokens to generate programs for 60 LIBERO-Pro tasks. Nor can the brain simply act as the controller: in our probes, models emitting one-token or Cartesian IK actions solved 1 of 1,987 and 0 of 600 LIBERO-Pro episodes ( Section C.4 , Figure 6 ). Vision–language–action models (VLAs) [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] and whole-body controllers such as SONIC [ 11 ] act at a few to tens of hertz, far faster than an agent deliberates, but VLAs that exceed 90% on LIBERO collapse under LIBERO-Pro’s perturbed test set [ 12 ] . Embodied intelligence should therefore separate a deliberative brain from a reactive cerebellum , as recent dual-system robot models do [ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] . However, two obstacles still stand in the way. First, there is no common runtime for comparing agents and VLAs : agent- and VLA-based systems are each built on their own simulator, robot, observation format and success criterion, so it is hard for them to test on the same episodes. Second, asynchronous coordination is hard ( Figure 3 ). When the agent drives and calls the fast policy, the fast loop waits on every agent decision: an agent advisor that the router waits for leaves the robot idle for 2.6s per LIBERO-Pro episode, 20% of its control time ( Figure 4 ). When the fast policy drives and calls the agent on errors, a persistent error re-triggers the brain at every recurrence, while most failures raise no error at all: an exception-triggered router escalated only 6 times in 590 LIBERO-Pro episodes although 166 failed. Re-execution is not always reliable either: if the task has already succeeded, running it again may undo the result. We address the above issues with REA , a Routed Embodied Agent runtime ( Figure 1 ) designed to unify agent and VLA runtime across simulators and real robots. REA implements a three-level system: System 2, the brain , is an agent that generates policies offline or online, and provides advises or judges; System 1, the embodied router , decides which policy to run and when to consult the brain without blocking a running policy; System 0, the cerebellum , holds fast policies (frozen programs, VLA policies or whole-body controllers) that return control to the router at segment or policy boundaries. REA also provides a unified evaluation framework for any policies including code-as-policies and VLAs. Figure 2 shows REA across simulators and on a real robot. Our central idea is to let the brain prepare policies offline and intervene online only when there is a need, so that no running policy has to wait for it. Besides the conventional serial execution mode, our REA also offers asynchronous mode: policies run at their own rate, with System 2 running in parallel to provide advice or judge. Our proposed method achieves this via three designs. To keep the brain off the dispatch path , REA contains a route memory of each policy’s per-task success and cost on development episodes, so choosing a policy can be isolated from the System 2’s reasoning process and call System 2 on dilemma. To catch silent failures without re-firing on errors , a boundary-triggered judge gate consults System 2 concurrently and decides whether a task is finished: if not done then escalates, if done then stops and protects completed work.
