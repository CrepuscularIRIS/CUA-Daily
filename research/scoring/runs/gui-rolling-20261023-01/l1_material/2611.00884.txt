arxiv: 2611.00884 (2611.00884v1)
title: When 200 OK Is Not an Answer: Silent Fetch Failures in Agentic Web Content Acquisition
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00884

§Abstract
Web agents and answer engines fetch pages at question time, and liveness checks of cited sources treat an HTTP 200 response as success. We measure how often that assumption fails and what it does to measurements built on fetched pages. We fetched 10,307 URLs in three days with a polite static client and a rendered browser client: pages from 1,507 sites in five strata, random non-existent paths on every site, and the 4,795 URLs cited by expert-verified claims in ExpertQA. We define a taxonomy of what a 200 response contains, label the static 200 responses automatically, and check the labels against 300 items reviewed by an LLM-based assistant; a human check of 60 of them retained every reviewed usability label after discussion. By the automatic labels, 20.9% of the pages that a static client receives with status 200 do not contain the page's main content: app shells, bot challenges, block pages, error and placeholder pages, and partial content. On the held-out test split, the reviewed sample lowers this estimate from 25.5% to 20.0%. 13.1% of the sites whose random-path probe was answered returned 200. The error propagates: for the cited sources, treating 200 as content overstates the share of claim-source pairs that can be checked against live content by 12.2 points and inflates the apparent silent citation-drift rate from 5.8% to 21.9%. Cheap heuristics catch most of it. Text length, probe comparison and redirect-to-root, combined and tuned on held-out sites, recover 86% of unusable reviewed responses at a false-alarm rate near 5%. Content-reading detectors perform comparably: the best, the text classifier, gains five to seven points of recall at more than twice the false-alarm rate. We argue that agents and their evaluations should report fetch outcomes explicitly instead of inferring them from the status line and will release the labelled data and code.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language model (LLM) agents and answer engines increasingly read the Web live. A search-augmented assistant issues queries, fetches the top results, and writes an answer with citations ( Nakano et al., 2021 ; Sayyid-Ali et al., 2026 ) ; evaluation pipelines check whether cited URLs are still live ( Rao et al., 2026 ) , and attribution datasets pair claims with the evidence of the cited pages ( Malaviya et al., 2024 ) . Status-based liveness checks treat a fetch as a simple event: a response with status 200 is content, anything else is a failure ( Rao et al., 2026 ) , and the same shortcut is easy to take whenever pages are fetched at scale. This paper asks how often that equivalence is wrong on today’s Web, whether the wrong cases can be detected from the response itself, and how much the error moves the numbers that Web measurements and agent evaluations report. The Web relevance is direct: the populations are the pages agents are pointed at (llms.txt files, popular sites, cited sources), and the quantities affected (reachability, dead-link and drift rates) are standard Web measurements. The status line is a weak signal for three reasons, each documented in isolation. Servers answer missing pages with a 200 “soft 404” or a redirect to the homepage ( Bar-Yossef et al., 2004 ; Prieto et al., 2013 ) . Bot-management services often serve challenge and block pages, and refusals use status codes inconsistently ( Ansar et al., 2025 ; Gundelach et al., 2026 ) . Many pages ship an application shell whose content arrives only after JavaScript runs, a long-standing problem for web archiving ( Brunelle et al., 2016 ; Goel et al., 2022 ) . A static fetch, the cheapest way to obtain a page, sees all three as a successful download. The recent measurement of answer engines’ real-time fetching by Sayyid-Ali et al. ( Sayyid-Ali et al., 2026 ) shows that engines fetch pages live at question time; it measures how much they fetch, not what the fetches return. Gundelach et al. ( Gundelach et al., 2026 ) measure client-dependent blocking with explicit error codes and state that “content degradation within HTTP 200 responses” is outside their scope. That gap is the subject of this paper. We make four contributions. • A fetch-outcome taxonomy for 200 responses (Section 3 ) that separates main content from block pages, challenges, shells, error or substitute pages, parked pages and consent walls, together with a usability rule that also catches partial content by comparing a static fetch with a rendered one. • A measurement (Sections 4 – 5 ) of 10,307 fetches across six agent-relevant populations (8,800 URLs fetched with both a static and a rendered client, plus 1,507 static-only reference probes), automatic labels for the static 200 responses, 300 reviewed labels, a human check of 60 of them and a browser check of the rendered reference. By the automatic labels a static 200 is unusable for 20.9% of pages (the reviewed sample lowers the test-split estimate from 25.5% to 20.0%), with large differences between populations. • A distortion analysis (Section 7 ) on 6,156 claim–source pairs from ExpertQA: reading every 200 as content inflates the apparent silent-drift rate of cited sources from 5.8% to 21.9%, and status-based dead-link counts miss content that is gone behind a 200. • A detector comparison (Section 6 ) on site-disjoint splits, from the status code and five heuristics from crawling toolkits and the soft-404 literature to a zero-shot LLM, a text classifier and a fine-tuned small language model. A tuned combination of cheap heuristics recovers 86% of unusable responses at a false-alarm rate near 5%; the best content reader adds five to seven points of recall at more than twice the false-alarm rate, so cheap checks are the practical default for agent fetch tools. All collection respected robots.txt (with one stated exception), rate limits and challenges; we never solved or bypassed a challenge (Section 9 ).
