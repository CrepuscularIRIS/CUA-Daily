title: StreamEmbed: Real-Time Indexing from Pixels for Screenshot Retrieval
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18516

§Abstract
Screen history can serve as searchable visual memory for people and agents, but unlike an offline corpus, it must be encoded and indexed as each screenshot arrives. OCR-based indexing is fast but loses visual information; visual encoding takes over two seconds. Yet screens change incrementally: most pixels repeat within and across screenshots. This raises a question: do visual encoders need to recompute their representations from scratch for every new screenshot? We present StreamEmbed, a system for incremental visual embedding that exploits this redundancy. Guided solely by pixel differences, it combines spatial token pruning, which skips blocks that repeat a neighbor, with temporal KV reuse, which reuses cached keys and values for blocks unchanged across screenshots. A small learned adapter compensates for pruning and reuse to preserve fidelity to full-screenshot embeddings. On ScreenMemEval, our benchmark of 10,426 questions over 184 hours of screen histories from two humans and a computer-use agent, StreamEmbed sets a new accuracy-latency Pareto frontier on an M2 Pro GPU: it encodes up to 3.7x faster than full-screenshot encoding without sacrificing accuracy and improves accuracy over OCR-based indexing by up to 6.3 Recall@10 points at matched latency. A memory-efficient vector index stores a simulated year of screenshots in 0.13 GB of RAM. Together, these results make real-time indexing of screen history in its native visual form practical, laying a foundation for fully local visual assistants.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Much of daily computer work passes through laptop screens: reading papers, browsing products, and messaging colleagues. Yet people remember few of the details and may later ask: “Which paper had the pink and blue Pareto frontier curves I saw last week?” A searchable screen history could help people recover these details, and also provide persistent memory for locally running agents ( Packer et al., 2023 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . Because screenshots capture every application without specialized integration, they offer a unified interface across text and visual contents ( Hu and Lee, 2020 ) . Answering questions about past activity then becomes a retrieval problem over a continuously growing screenshot history. The key challenge is maintaining this visual memory continuously without exhausting system resources. Unlike a fixed corpus that is indexed offline ( Karpukhin et al., 2020 ) , this setting requires real-time indexing : encoding and indexing each screenshot as it arrives, ideally locally to keep private content on device, while consuming only a small fraction of the laptop’s capacity. Converting each screenshot into OCR text, as existing open-source tools do ( OpenRecall, 2024 ; McGhee, 2023 ) , is fast but loses what only pixels capture ( Wang et al., 2026c ) . Direct visual embedding preserves this information but takes 2.3 s per screenshot on the GPU. At one screenshot every 5 s, this uses the GPU for 28 minutes per hour of the user’s work, and at one every 2 s, it cannot keep pace. Even commercial systems, such as Microsoft Recall ( Microsoft, ) , require dedicated hardware like NPUs and at least 16 GB of RAM. Thus, the central question is: can we retain visual retrieval quality while keeping continuous encoding lightweight enough to run silently in the background? Screen histories, however, offer a particular opportunity: they change incrementally and are largely redundant (Figure 2 ). Background elements often repeat within and across screenshots, and typing or moving the cursor changes only a few regions, leaving the rest untouched. This raises a question: do we need to compute representations from scratch for every screenshot? We propose StreamEmbed , which encodes screenshots as a stream rather than as isolated images, exploiting this redundancy. Within a screenshot, it drops pixel blocks that merely duplicate a neighboring block (spatial pruning), cutting the computation by 33–41%. Across screenshots, it recomputes only the changed blocks and reuses cached keys and values for the rest in both the vision encoder and the language model (temporal reuse), cutting the remaining computation by a further 34–57%. These two techniques are largely complementary, and combining them further reduces the computation per screenshot by 57–71%. A small adapter merged into the weights compensates for pruning and reuse, keeping the resulting embedding close to that of the full screenshot. Evaluating StreamEmbed presents another challenge, since no suitable benchmark is available. Such a benchmark needs a screenshot history, questions about it, and the screenshots that answer each question, but existing benchmarks lack a screenshot history ( Wu et al., 2026 ) , questions about the past ( Xie et al., 2024 ) , or relevance labels and public data ( Wang and Chen, 2025 ) . We therefore build ScreenMemEval ( SME ), consisting of 10,426 questions drawn from three screenshot histories, each labeled with the screenshots that answer it. Two histories come from human workers on Windows in a public dataset of computer-use recordings ( Howland et al., 2025 ) : SME-H1 spans 117 days with 88 hours of screen time, and SME-H2 spans 57 days with 18 hours. The third, SME-Agent , spans 43 days with 78 hours, recorded on Ubuntu from a computer-use agent (the frontier model GPT-6.1 Sol ( OpenAI, 2026b ) ) performing the tasks of myPCBench ( Jang et al., 2026 ) . On SME , StreamEmbed on Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B ( Li et al., 2026 ) sets a new accuracy–latency Pareto frontier (Figure 1 ). Against baselines on the same backbone, fine-tuned on the same data, it is up to 6.3 points more accurate than an OCR-based index at similar latency, and up to 3.7 \times faster than a screenshot index while retaining over 98% of its accuracy. To cut latency, one can lower the input resolution or use a smaller encoder. When we apply these to both StreamEmbed and the baselines, StreamEmbed is more accurate at every latency, by up to 11.4 points (§ 6.4 ). We also show that binary codes of the embeddings reduce memory usage without sacrificing accuracy on SME . With them, an index of five simulated years of screenshots fits in under 1 GB of RAM, and search takes under 0.1 s. Together, this work makes real-time indexing of screen history in its native visual form practical and efficient, paving the way for fully local, visual computer-use assistants.
