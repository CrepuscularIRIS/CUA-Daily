title: T2T Technical Report: Synthesizing GUI Agent Trajectories at Scale from Video Tutorials
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18715

§Abstract
Training generalist GUI agents requires large volumes of interaction trajectories comprising screenshot sequences paired with grounded UI actions, yet collecting such data through human demonstration is expensive and difficult to scale. Although public software tutorial videos contain rich operational demonstrations, their continuous pixel streams lack discrete action boundaries, semantic intents, and precise spatial coordinates, making them difficult to use directly for model training. To address this, we present T2T (Tutorial-to-Trajectory), a fully automated pipeline for synthesizing GUI agent trajectories at scale. T2T spans desktop, web, and mobile platforms to convert unannotated video tutorials into structured, executable interaction trajectories. To guarantee trajectory quality, T2T combines active segment filtering, keyframe identification via localized UI change, shortcut-free observation frame selection, presence-aware grounding, and two-stage action-space normalization. These refined trajectories are then converted into multi-task training views spanning navigation, planning, forward dynamics, and grounding. We fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B on a subset of the more than one million human-annotation-free training samples generated by this pipeline. Evaluations show that the model achieves consistent gains across multiple benchmarks covering desktop, web, and mobile environments, including OSWorld-G, ScreenSpot-Pro, AndroidControl, and AgentNetBench, indicating improved core capabilities in grounding, step-level instruction execution, and trajectory-level decision-making. Notably, all gains are achieved without any in-distribution training data from target domains. Learning exclusively from trajectories extracted from public videos via the T2T pipeline, the model exhibits cross-application and cross-task generalization capabilities.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language models and vision-language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding natural language, generating code, and reasoning over visual content ( OpenAI, 2024 ; Qwen Team, 2025a ; Chen et al., 2024 ) , increasingly serving as general-purpose foundations for a wide range of intelligent applications. A natural next step is to translate these capabilities into concrete task execution in the digital world. Unlike web APIs or command-line interfaces that expose structured endpoints, the vast majority of software, from productivity suites and creative tools to enterprise systems and mobile applications, is designed primarily for human interaction through graphical user interfaces. This makes GUI the de facto universal interface to the digital world, but also one that demands perceptual and motor skills traditionally reserved for human users: recognizing UI elements from pixels, understanding spatial layouts, tracking state changes across screens, and executing precise sequences of clicks, keystrokes, and gestures ( Cheng et al., 2024 ; Gou et al., 2025 ; Xie et al., 2026a ) . GUI agents, autonomous systems built on top of LLMs and VLMs that perceive screen content and perform such actions on behalf of users, bridge this gap by operating directly at the interface level, automating tasks across arbitrary applications without requiring dedicated APIs or developer-side instrumentation. A substantial body of work has advanced this direction in recent years ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Nguyen et al., 2025 ) , producing increasingly capable agents across desktop, mobile, and web platforms ( Lin et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2025 ; ByteDance Seed, 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) . Despite rapid progress in model architecture and training recipes, the availability of high-quality trajectory data remains a central bottleneck. Training a capable GUI agent requires large collections of expert demonstrations: sequences of screenshots paired with precise actions that together capture realistic, multi-step interactions with diverse applications and interfaces. Curating such data through human annotation is inherently expensive and slow, and the cost grows sharply with task complexity and horizon length. A natural alternative source of such data is video tutorials. Platforms such as YouTube host millions of screen recordings in which human experts demonstrate complex software workflows step by step, spanning a broad range of applications across desktop, mobile, and web platforms. These videos are inherently long-horizon, reflect realistic human operating patterns, and are available at a scale that manual annotation cannot easily match. Several recent efforts have recognized the potential of web-sourced tutorials and videos, and explored converting them into agent trajectories ( Lu et al., 2025 ; Xiong et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Song et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2026b ) . However, bridging the gap from raw video to structured, training-ready trajectories presents several technical challenges: (1) Computational and Contextual Limits. Feeding unsegmented raw videos into vision-language models (VLMs) is computationally prohibitive and practically infeasible due to strict API limits and context windows. Pruning the vast majority of frames that contain non-interactive content (e.g., introductions, static slides, or idle pauses) and identifying informative frames are essential for efficient and effective video processing. (2) Visual Target Leakage and Shortcut Learning. Naively sampled screenshots often suffer from severe visual shortcuts. High-production tutorials frequently overlay colored boxes or arrows, training models to locate annotations rather than comprehend UI pixels. Similarly, taking screenshots at the exact action timestamp leaves the cursor directly hovering over the target, encouraging models to learn a trivial “read-the-cursor” shortcut. (3) Coordinate Hallucination under Target Absence. Standard grounding models may instead rely on layout priors (e.g., close buttons in corners) and hallucinate coordinates even when the intended target element is temporarily absent or occluded, injecting noisy coordinates into the training distribution. (4) Action Space Heterogeneity and Misalignment. Speech narration and screen activity are temporally asynchronous. Furthermore, raw video extraction produces a heterogeneous vocabulary of non-standardized action names, which must be mapped into unified, platform-conditioned tool-call schemas. In this report, we present T2T (Tutorial-to-Trajectory), a fully automated pipeline designed to overcome these challenges. Starting from raw public videos, the pipeline proceeds through three stages: (1) Video Filtering and Preprocessing.
