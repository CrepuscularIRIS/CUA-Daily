title: Kierston & KierstonBench: Simulation of Allentown, PA and Evaluation of Language Models in Crime Investigation and Surveillance Tasks
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04785

§Abstract
Large language models are candidates for collecting and analyzing records in criminal investigations and mass surveillance. Their capability and accuracy in this work cannot currently be measured. Real deployments lack the ground truth needed to score a model's conclusions, and no benchmark evaluates these capabilities in a simulated environment. We present Kierston, a city-scale simulation engine, and KierstonBench, a benchmark that tests these capabilities against known simulated events. Kierston models approximately 125,000 residents of Allentown, Pennsylvania, generating their daily activities and the telecommunications, financial, administrative and license-plate records that surveillance and investigative systems collect about them. These records derive from a common behavioral timeline and are distributed across sources representing separate providers. Models investigate through a restricted records interface, linking observations across sources without access to the underlying simulation. The benchmark comprises 32 task families covering record linkage, routine reconstruction, incident investigation and assessment of deceptive accounts. In an evaluation of sixteen language models, most could not perform records investigation and submitted identifications the records did not support. A small number of frontier models solved most of the tasks and abstained when evidence was insufficient. In some cases these models still fell for common deceptions and assumptions, and none replaced an investigator.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Investigators combine records held by different organizations to establish what happened, where and when it happened, and who was involved. A vehicle registration, a cellular-network event and a bank transaction each describe a different part of an activity. To answer an investigative question, an analyst finds the relevant records, connects their identifiers and tests whether the resulting account fits the available observations. Language models can query these sources and write analysis code. How well they turn retrieved records into supported conclusions cannot currently be measured. Measuring it requires linked records with known underlying events. Real investigative data is difficult to access and rarely provides complete ground truth. Independently generated tables do not show whether records from different sources describe the same behavior. Kierston solves this by simulating a population’s activities first and deriving the provider records from those activities. The current simulation places approximately 125,000 residents in Allentown, Pennsylvania, using public geographic and census data. The records Kierston produces have the structure of the data available to law-enforcement and intelligence analysts: carrier network events and call records, plate reads, bank transactions, and state and federal administrative files, linked only through the identifiers each holder keeps. The engine is therefore useful outside language-model evaluation. It can supply test data with known ground truth for records-fusion and analytics software, for planning plate-reader and cell-site deployments, and for analyst training. The benchmark measures the capability these systems are built to provide, which is identifying and locating people from provider records. Both the engine and the benchmark are dual-use. KierstonBench presents the resulting records through an interface representing separate telecommunications, financial, administrative and surveillance providers. The model receives a question and initial evidence, then chooses which sources to query and how to analyze the returned records. It has no access to the simulation’s activity histories or incident roles. Technical Tasks test the ability to link records and reconstruct ordinary routines. Real-Time Investigations introduce incidents and restrict records to those available shortly afterward. Deception Tasks add accounts that must be checked against independent records. The three groups test the retrieval and analysis needed to investigate an event. They also test the judgment needed to decide whether a conclusion is supported. Contributions. We present: 1. Kierston , a simulation engine that generates linked investigative records from a shared model of daily behavior, retaining the activities that produced them as ground truth. It includes provider formats, incomplete observations and measurement errors. 2. KierstonBench , 32 task families that test record linkage, reconstruction of activities, investigation of staged incidents and assessment of deceptive accounts through a restricted records interface. 3. An evaluation of language models , with analysis of their recorded queries, scripts and answers to examine how evidence collection and interpretation affect their conclusions.
