title: Deep Search in Real-World Web Environments
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30181

§Abstract
Despite substantial progress in Deep Search agents, their ability to conduct end-to-end research in real-world open Web environments remains insufficiently evaluated. Unlike static retrieval settings, information on the real-world Web is distributed across diverse sources, modalities, and heterogeneous formats, often involving complex interaction mechanisms that require agents to autonomously search for, access, and integrate relevant evidence during execution. To address this gap, we introduce the OpenDS task, designed to evaluate agents' ability to perform multi-step deep search in the live, open Web. This task requires agents to autonomously explore the real Web, discover and connect relevant evidence from multiple independent sources through Web search, browser interaction, and evidence processing, and ultimately determine a unique factual answer. Building on this task formulation, we construct OpenDS-World, a benchmark comprising 200 challenging tasks spanning diverse information modalities, heterogeneous file formats, and interactive access requirements, with independent human verification to ensure evidence validity and answer uniqueness. We evaluate 12 frontier models under a unified tool environment and execution protocol. The best-performing model achieves 39.0% accuracy, while human evaluation across 1,000 independent attempts yields 21.1% accuracy. Further analyses of tool usage, search cost, termination behavior, and failure recovery reveal substantial differences in research strategies across models, as well as persistent challenges in tool coordination and long-horizon search management. OpenDS-World provides a unified evaluation platform for systematically assessing and advancing agents' autonomous deep search capabilities in real-world open Web environments.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
A central objective of Deep Search is to enable AI agents to autonomously acquire, analyze, and integrate information to solve complex information-seeking and research tasks ( Xi et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ; Zhang et al., 2025a ) . Recent advances in large language models and tool-augmented agents have substantially improved their capabilities in multi-round search, Web browsing, and cross-source reasoning ( Jin et al., 2025b ; Qiao et al., 2025 ) , enabling them to address increasingly complex information needs ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Li et al., 2026c ) . Meanwhile, reliably evaluating these agents’ research capabilities in practical environments has become an increasingly important issue. Existing studies have explored various aspects of agent capabilities, including general tool use, complex information retrieval, persistent Web search, and interactive Web navigation, providing an important foundation for understanding their search, reasoning, and tool-use capabilities ( Wei et al., 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2025a ; Xue et al., 2025 ) . Despite substantial progress in open Web search, multi-round information retrieval, and cross-source reasoning, existing Deep Search evaluations remain insufficient for systematically assessing the complex evidence acquisition requirements of real-world Web environments. The complexity of the real-world Web is primarily reflected in three aspects: Diverse Modalities, where information is presented in various forms, including text, images, audio, and video ( Li et al., 2025b ; Liu et al., 2026b ) ; Heterogeneous Formats, where information is distributed across resources with different structures and processing requirements ( Jin et al., 2025b ) , such as HTML, PDF, XML, and Parquet; and Interactive Access Requirements, where certain evidence cannot be obtained through direct retrieval but requires interactive operations such as Web navigation, interface manipulation, parameter updates, or artifact transfer ( Liu et al., 2026a ; Xu et al., 2026 ) . Together, these attributes constitute the complex evidence acquisition process in real-world Web environments, requiring agents to coordinate information discovery, interactive access, resource processing, and cross-source reasoning throughout multi-step research. However, existing benchmarks ( Li et al., 2025b ; Java et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026a ; Xu et al., 2026 ) have not sufficiently covered these interconnected challenges, limiting the systematic analysis of agents’ deep search capabilities in real-world environments. To address this gap, we introduce the OpenDS (Open-Web Deep Search) task , designed to systematically evaluate agents’ ability to conduct deep search across heterogeneous information sources in real-world Web environments. This task requires agents to autonomously search, access, and connect evidence across Web sources with diverse information modalities, resource formats, and interactive access requirements to complete complex multi-step searches. Building upon the OpenDS task, we construct OpenDS-World , a benchmark comprising 200 challenging tasks designed around real-world Web information sources, spanning eight domains: culture, daily life, science, economy, history, nature, society, and technology. Each task requires agents to start from a video containing real-world information and independently discover evidence from multiple sources across the open Web to support the answer. As illustrated in Figure 2 , solving a task may involve accessing online videos, interactive virtual tours, research papers, and structured datasets, and acquiring and connecting relevant evidence through different access mechanisms. To systematically characterize the challenges of evidence acquisition in real-world Web environments, OpenDS-World covers diverse information modalities, heterogeneous file formats, and seven types of access requirements. We employ independent human verification and task filtering to ensure evidence validity, factual accuracy, and answer uniqueness, and further conduct human testing to assess practical research difficulty. We systematically evaluate 12 frontier models under a unified tool environment and execution protocol. The results reveal that current agents still face substantial challenges when conducting deep search in real-world Web environments. The best-performing model, GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026b ) , achieves only 39.0% accuracy across all 200 tasks. By comparison, human participants achieve an overall accuracy of 21.1% in independent solving attempts (Figure 1 ), with 42.5% of tasks remaining unsolved by all assigned human participants. Further analysis reveals substantial differences across models in tool usage, search cost, termination behavior, and failure recovery.
