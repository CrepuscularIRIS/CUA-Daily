title: CANDID: Candidate-Driven Intervention for Disciplined Execution in Local Computer-Use Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24977

§Abstract
We present CANDID (Candidate-Driven Intervention for Disciplined Execution), an execution-control framework designed to enhance the reliability of local computer-use agents (CUAs) in resource-constrained settings. While local CUAs feature robust baseline proficiency in single-step action prediction, they frequently fail at critical junctions due to rigid action loops or premature DONE decisions. Rather than imposing imprecise cognitive loads through textual reflection, CANDID repurposes parallel test-time scaling into a disciplined, execution-level intervention at sparse, failure-prone points. We introduce a loop-recovery mechanism to filter candidates away from repeated behaviors and a conservative, unanimous DONE gating policy that requires absolute consensus to terminate. Experiments on OSWorld demonstrate that CANDID's structural control significantly improves success rates, outperforming feedback-based alternatives with minimal execution overhead.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Computer-use agents (CUAs) have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for autonomous interaction with graphical user interfaces (GUIs), enabling multimodal agents to execute real-world desktop and web tasks by understanding screenshots and executing GUI actions. Recent advances in multimodal foundation models Qin et al. (2025) ; Wang et al. (2025) ; Xue et al. (2026) ; Xu et al. (2025) ; Lin et al. (2024) and agentic frameworks Agashe et al. (2024) ; Agashe et al. (2025) ; Gonzalez-Pumariega et al. (2026) ; Yang et al. (2025) have significantly improved long-horizon GUI interaction capabilities across increasingly realistic environments Xie et al. (2024) ; Zhou et al. (2024) ; Bonatti et al. (2024) ; Deng et al. (2023) . In particular, modern CUA systems leverage hierarchical planning Agashe et al. (2024) , memory retrieval Liu et al. (2025) , and test-time scaling Yang et al. (2025) strategies to improve execution robustness and task success rates. Deploying CUAs under local, resource-constrained settings is practically important, and small language model (SLM)-based agentic systems have been increasingly proposed toward this goal Belcak et al. (2025) . Yet such deployment remains highly challenging: unlike large cloud-based systems that rely on expensive multi-agent orchestration Song et al. (2026) ; Yang et al. (2026) ; Han et al. (2026) or proprietary reasoning models Anthropic (2026a) ; Singh et al. (2026) , local CUAs operate with limited model sizes and weaker multimodal reasoning. Under these conditions, simply increasing execution horizons or enlarging multimodal histories often fails to reliably improve performance. The central obstacle is that local agents fail in two coupled ways: they fall into repetitive action loops, or terminate prematurely before completing the task. Moreover, naively scaling the step budget or contextual history merely shifts the failure pattern between the two, rather than resolving both. Prior methods for recovering failed agent trajectories primarily rely on textual reflection or verifier feedback Shinn et al. (2023) ; Han et al. (2026) , where the agent inspects its own recent trajectory, generates natural-language feedback, and incorporates it before revising its next action. However, we argue that such feedback-based approaches provide limited benefit for lightweight local CUAs, whose reasoning capabilities are often insufficient either to generate meaningful textual feedback about the failure or to convert given feedback into distinct, executable GUI actions. These limitations prevent agents from escaping repetitive action loops or improving termination decisions under ambiguous completion states, often causing the model to still repeat its failed behavior under a restated rationale. To overcome these limitations, we instead build on a key observation about local CUA behavior: although they struggle to assess trajectory progress, they remain capable of generating multiple plausible actions—which we refer to as candidates —from the same interface state. This suggests that the bottleneck lies not in the availability of good candidates, but in committing to one when progress or completion is uncertain. We therefore propose CANDID, a candidate-based control framework that treats candidate disagreement as an execution-control signal, rather than asking the model to reflect on its own failure. CANDID instantiates this principle through two modules: (1) a loop-recovery module that identifies repetitive behavior through rule-based detection and selects an alternative candidate, and (2) a unanimous DONE gating module that accepts termination only when all sampled candidates agree. CANDID applies this sparse intervention only at these failure-prone points, concentrating computation where it is most beneficial while keeping overhead low. We evaluate CANDID on the OSWorld benchmark Xie et al. (2024) using multiple local CUA models, including Qwen3-VL Bai et al. (2025) and OpenCUA Wang et al. (2025) variants. Experimental results show that CANDID consistently improves task success rates across all evaluated models, outperforming both feedback-based interventions and a test-time scaling baseline. Further analyses show that its two control modules address complementary failure modes, and that sparse intervention is more effective than applying candidate selection uniformly throughout execution.
