title: StepBench: Diagnosing Real-World Procedural Understanding in AI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26679

§Abstract
Evaluating an agent's planning ability remains challenging for open-domain tasks, where verifying procedures through execution is often impractical. Existing evaluations typically assess only the final plan and do not distinguish procedural errors such as missing, incorrect, or misordered actions. We introduce STEPBENCH, a benchmark of 6,885 verified procedures crawled from wikiHow across 81 open-domain categories. From each procedure, we derive 5 diagnostic tasks: four with deterministic answers involving withheld, incorrect, or shuffled steps, and one requiring next-step generation. We additionally evaluate full-plan generation to examine how diagnostic performance correlates with end-to-end plan validity. Using the same procedures across tasks lets us directly compare different planning capabilities. For generation, STEPEVAL identifies critical failures that prevent goal completion. Across 14 configurations of 12 models, we find substantial differences across planning capabilities, with step ordering proving the most difficult: the strongest model correctly orders only 63.0% of procedures, dropping to 33.1% for those with nine or more steps. Incorrect-step detection also predicts judged plan validity across models, suggesting a lower-cost alternative for evaluating generation quality. On STEPBENCH-Val, comprising 214 procedures published in 2026, comprehension scores and rankings remain stable, suggesting limited influence from memorisation.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents are on behalf of humans to perform long-horizon tasks, with access to tools and actions that go beyond text generation. These tools and actuators that can link AI systems beyond cyber-digital spaces will emerge, enabling LLM agents to tackle open-domain cyberphysical tasks (tasks that require manipulating both digital and physical environments). AI agents capable of goal-based planning and tool calling ( Shen et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2024 ) on physical, open-domain tasks lead to synergistic teaming with humans (who have access to physical environments) at present and, in time, take autonomy over executing and completing these tasks. Having a deep understanding of task decomposition, sequencing and gap identification in open-domain tasks plays a foundational role in creating reliable agents that can succeed in both knowledge-based and (much broader) cyber-physical tasks. Open-domain task-planning benchmarks primarily focus on generating task decompositions ( Zhang et al., 2021 ; Shen et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2024 ) or predicting inter-step temporal dependencies ( Zhang et al., 2020a ; Yuan et al., 2023a ; Lal et al., 2024 ) . Fine-grained error analysis exists in formal ( Valmeekam et al., 2023b ; Kokel et al., 2025 ; Kokel et al., 2026 ) and embodied ( Li et al., 2024 ) environments, where an executor supplies ground truth, but is harder to obtain for open-domain written procedures. Our goal is to diagnose the capabilities underlying open-domain agent planning without executing the proposed steps. For open-domain tasks, generated plans generally cannot be physically executed or formally verified at benchmark scale, while evaluating only the final plan or task outcome provides little insight into which planning capabilities models struggle with. We aim to identify these strengths and weaknesses through targeted diagnostic tasks and examine how they relate to end-to-end plan generation performance. We introduce StepBench , comprising 5 tasks derived from 6,885 curated wikiHow procedures across 81 categories. StepBench ’s 5 diagnostic tasks probe distinct aspects of procedural understanding: last-step selection, missing-step recovery, incorrect-step detection, reordering, and next-step generation. The source articles have documented editorial provenance, with 96.1% co-authored by subject experts, and distractors are retrieved from other procedures to create plausible errors. Using the same procedures across tasks enables direct comparison of different aspects of agentic planning ability. For next-step and full-plan generation, StepEval adopts the critical-failure formulation of Chang et al. (2026) , judging whether an output contains an error severe enough to prevent goal completion while allowing valid alternative plans. Across 14 configurations of 12 models, we find that performance varies substantially across capabilities. Models with similar incorrect-step detection scores can differ markedly in reordering. Even when all correct steps are provided, the strongest configuration recovers the reference order for only 63.0% of procedures, falling to 33.1% for procedures with nine or more steps. Reordering accuracy also correlates strongly with judged full-plan validity ( \rho=0.96{} ), suggesting that diagnostic tasks can provide a lower-cost signal of plan quality. Our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce StepBench , a planning diagnosis benchmark suite for evaluating plan recovery, error detection, ordering, and generation on 6,885 open-domain procedures. We additionally evaluate on 214 procedures published in 2026 (§ 3 –§ 4 ). • We apply critical-failure evaluation to next-step and full-plan generation through StepEval , an 8B judge distilled from GPT-5 and validated against human annotations (§ 5 ). • We evaluate 12 models across 14 configurations and find substantial differences across planning abilities. Diagnostic performance also correlates with judged full-plan validity, providing a lower-cost signal of generation quality (§ 7 ).
