title: FORCA: A Force-Aware Coding Agent for Contact-Rich Robot Manipulation
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31841

§Abstract
Coding agents can translate language instructions into robot programs. For contact-rich manipulation, a key challenge is to generate and refine control logic that responds to physical interaction. We present FORCA, a coding agent that takes visual observations and wrist force/torque (F/T) measurements as input to write Python programs for contact probing and force-responsive motion. Between executions, the agent inspects force records alongside visual outcomes to diagnose failures and revise both motion and force-feedback control logic. FORCA requires no demonstrations, task-specific training, or simulation. Across five real-robot tasks, we demonstrate its effectiveness in three forms of contact-rich manipulation: improving manipulation accuracy, adapting to changes in object or surface properties, and regulating contact under surface variation and object motion. Compared with a vision-only variant of the same agent, FORCA improves grasp accuracy and task success and reduces contact loss in wiping and peeling.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Coding agents based on foundation models turn open-ended instructions into executable robot programs that compose perception, grasping, and motion skills [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . They ground their decisions mainly in vision and proprioception, which is often insufficient for contact-rich manipulation: occluded or reflective surfaces yield uncertain geometry, mass and friction are not visible, and maintaining contact requires responding to surface variation and object motion. Force and contact measurements complement vision by providing evidence about contact states and mechanical responses that are difficult to infer from visual observations alone. Learned policies exploit force and tactile signals for reactive control but require demonstrations or robot data [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . Foundation-model agents have used force through compliant or force-thresholded primitives [ 9 , 10 ] , inferred physical properties largely from priors [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] , and revised force targets after failed attempts [ 14 ] . Supporting these diverse tasks requires agents to determine when contact information is needed, how it should guide ongoing motion, and how unexpected contact outcomes should inform subsequent attempts. We present Forca , a training-free coding agent that uses wrist force/torque (F/T) sensing in both program execution and program revision (Fig. 1 ). It writes complete Python programs with access to the measured wrench. During a rollout, the program uses measurements from contact probes to guide subsequent actions and uses live force feedback to regulate its motion. Between rollouts, the agent relates the recorded force traces to visual outcomes, diagnoses failures, and revises the program. Forca addresses three types of contact-rich manipulation: (i) precision manipulation , where force cues help refine grasp poses and improve manipulation accuracy; (ii) physical adaptation , where actions must be adjusted to changes in object or surface properties; and (iii) contact regulation , where motion must respond to surface variation and object movement. We evaluate Forca on five real-robot tasks spanning these categories: drawer opening, door-handle operation, cube-to-target knocking, vase wiping, and cucumber peeling. Compared with a vision-only variant of the same agent, Forca increases task success from 40% to 100% for drawer opening and from 10% to 100% for door-handle operation, while reducing contact-loss rates in wiping and peeling from 40.6–55.9% to at most 2.2%. Our contributions are twofold. First, we introduce a coding-agent framework that generates task-specific motion and force-feedback control logic from language instructions. The resulting programs use measured contact to refine grasp poses and regulate motion during execution. Second, we extend program refinement to the force-feedback logic itself, enabling coding agents to revise how their programs respond to contact based on evidence from real-robot execution.

## III Method
Given a language instruction and RGB-D observations, Forca writes a Python code with access to wrist force/torque (F/T) measurements. The program executes on the robot without querying the foundation model. After execution, the agent reviews the recorded interaction and revises the code (Fig. 1 ). We refer to one execution of a program as a rollout . III-A Force-Aware Program Synthesis For each task, the agent generates a Python task program that specifies the action sequence and the force-dependent motion logic. To construct this program, the agent has access to an initial skill codebase for perception, grasping, motion planning, and pick-and-place, assembled from open-source components under human supervision. These predefined modules provide object pose estimates, grasp candidates, and collision-free trajectories computed with PyRoKi [ 30 ] . Figure 2 illustrates these modules as the robot acquires a whiteboard eraser for the instruction “Wipe the vase.” The agent writes the force-dependent logic for each task using wrist wrench measurements exposed by the robot interface. For example, this logic can specify when an approach should stop or how motion should respond to a force error. To implement these behaviors, the agent can modify existing routines or add measurement and control code as needed. III-B Contact-Responsive Execution During a rollout, the generated program uses F/T measurements to guide subsequent actions and adjust ongoing motion. The conditions and feedback rules governing these responses are encoded in the program and executed without querying the foundation model. Contact measurements can inform actions by refining the geometric estimates on which they depend. For example, the program can probe a surface before grasping, specifying both the probing motion and the force condition used to detect contact. The robot pose at contact, together with the tool geometry, provides an updated estimate of surface height or grasp position. Subsequent motions use this estimate within the same rollout. For actions that require sustained contact, the program uses live force feedback to regulate motion. A host-side 100 Hz loop reads the wrench and robot state over RTDE and updates Cartesian velocity commands according to the generated control logic. This logic can adjust motion based on the difference between measured and target force, or pause forward motion when contact is insufficient and resume it once contact is restored. III-C Execution-Grounded Program Revision After each rollout, the agent inspects visual observations alongside the recorded force and robot-state traces. The agent can write analysis code to examine relevant time windows and relate force changes to robot motion and visible outcomes. The agent uses these records to identify possible causes of failure and revise the relevant code. Changes can affect motion parameters or contact conditions, and can also modify the control logic or action sequence. Figure 3 illustrates one such revision in cucumber peeling. Skin remains exactly where the recorded force shows that the blade lost contact, so the agent makes the blade wait for contact before moving on. Before execution, checks screen the revised code and planned motions for errors, reachability, and workspace violations. Revision stops when the agent declares the program converged or when either the rollout or wall-clock budget is exhausted. Development budgets and evaluation protocols are described in Sec. IV-A .

## IV Experiments
We consider three ways in which force/torque feedback can benefit manipulation: (i) improving manipulation accuracy , by complementing visual estimates with contact-based geometric information to refine grasp poses and guide subsequent actions; (ii) adapting to physical changes , by providing evidence about how object properties affect action outcomes to adjust manipulation accordingly; and (iii) regulating contact , by using live force measurements to adjust ongoing motion and maintain contact as surfaces vary or objects move. Our experiments evaluate how effectively Forca uses contact information across these three settings, combining comparisons against a vision-only agent with case studies of the generated programs. We additionally examine how Forca revises its programs across iterations and the time and robot interactions required. IV-A Experimental Setup and Protocol Hardware and agent. Experiments use a UR5e arm with wrist force/torque sensing, a WSG-50 gripper, and a fixed RealSense D455 RGB-D camera. The coding agent uses Claude Fable 5 for all analysis and code writing. The initial skills are described in Sec. III . Execution and evaluation. The agent generates a complete task program, executes it, inspects the resulting records, and revises the program. One rollout consists of the agent writing or revising the task program and executing it once on the real robot. Each run is limited to 20 rollouts and 4 hours of wall-clock time. The run stops when the agent declares the program converged, after which we freeze the final program and run it over repeated trials and assess the physical task outcome; otherwise, it ends when either limit is reached. A rollout whose execution reaches the hard-coded 30 N force limit is stopped and counted as a failure. Human involvement is limited to execution authorization, scene operations, and final acceptance assessment. Baseline policy. We compare Forca with a vision-only agent that uses the same foundation model, task instructions, and basic skills, but has no access to force/torque data in program generation, revision, or execution: it receives no live or recorded wrench and no force-responsive control routines. Its programs guide motion with visual observations and robot state, and the agent can write visual feedback code and inspect visual outcomes when revising them. Robot-level safety protections remain active but do not expose force data to the agent, and the force recordings used for offline evaluation are withheld from it. IV-B Results and Analysis IV-B 1 Tasks Requiring Precise Manipulation We evaluate whether force cues improve manipulation accuracy and task success on drawer opening and door-handle operation . Both tasks require the robot to locate and grasp a handle before actuating the mechanism. The drawer is transparent, making its handle difficult to localize precisely from vision alone. Contact probing provides additional geometric information to refine the grasp pose. We compare Forca with the vision-only agent using translational and rotational grasp error and task success rate (Table I ). For each task and method, we execute the frozen program for 10 trials and report the success rate and grasp errors across these trials. Forca achieves lower translational and rotational grasp errors and succeeds in all 10 trials on both tasks (100%), whereas the vision-only agent succeeds in only 4/10 trials on drawer opening and 1/10 on door-handle operation. The robot must adjust its knocking motion to send the object far enough to reach the target without overshooting. The object’s mass and contact geometry affect its response to the impact, while friction with the supporting surface influences its subsequent deceleration and stopping distance. Consequently, the same knocking motion can produce different final positions when the object or surface changes. We ask whether a program developed under one physical condition can be reused when the object’s response changes, and how many additional interactions it needs to complete the task again. We vary the object, using a cube and a cylinder that differ in both shape and mass (61.9 g and 663.6 g), and the surface, using a whiteboard and a woodboard that differ in friction (Fig. 5 (b)). These variations change how far the object travels in response to a knock while preserving the task objective. The evaluation has two stages. In the development stage, Forca builds a knocking program from scratch for the cube on the whiteboard, the reference configuration (Table IV ). In the reuse stage, we change the object or the surface and let Forca continue from the developed program. After each attempt, it reviews the measured interaction forces together with the observed final object position and revises the program. Figure 6 shows the distance from the object to the target band after each attempt, and Fig. 5 (a) shows illustrative landing positions across attempts. In each of the three new configurations, the retained program reached the target band within a few additional iterations, far fewer than developing it from scratch required (Table IV ). The interaction experience of Forca is thus kept in executable code, which remains usable, and can be revised, when physical conditions change. IV-B 3 Tasks Requiring Online Contact Regulation We evaluate force-responsive execution on vase wiping and cucumber peeling . Wiping tests whether programs refined on a stationary surface remain effective when the object moves. Peeling tests whether the robot can maintain contact along an uneven surface while avoiding excessive force. Vase wiping. The robot uses a whiteboard eraser to remove a marker line from a vase. We consider two settings: a static vase resting on a table and a hand-held vase that a person translates and rotates during wiping. We evaluate both methods after iterative program refinement in the static setting, then deploy the statically refined programs in the hand-held setting without further program revision. Table II reports the marker-removal ratio , contact-loss rate , and maximum force for both methods in each setting. The marker-removal ratio is the percentage of the original mark removed, measured from before/after images. After iterative refinement, both methods achieve high marker removal on the static setup. The vision-only program improves by repeatedly adjusting trajectory offsets to the fixed surface, while Forca uses force feedback to correct motion during contact. When transferred to the hand-held setting, the tuned offsets become inadequate as the vase moves, reducing removal and increasing contact loss. Forca adjusts its motion to the moving surface and maintains more consistent contact. Figure 7 shows the wiping outcomes and corresponding force traces. IV-D Iterative Program Revision During iterative revision, the agent uses execution feedback to diagnose failures and update its program between rollouts. To illustrate this process, we present door-handle operation as a case study and analyze one development run of each agent. Door-handle case study. Figure 9 shows representative histories of iterative program development for Forca and the vision-only agent. Each marker sits at the stage where that iteration’s execution failed, so reading the markers in order shows how far each revision got. The dominant failure modes differ. Forca failed mostly at press down : the handle turned but the latch did not release. It resolved this from the force recorded while pressing, where a sharp rise in resistance marks the end stop, and then fixed the remaining failures in pulling and releasing. The vision-only agent failed mostly at grasp and pull open . It adjusted the grasp and commanded press angle using images alone and could press the handle down, but the handle repeatedly slipped out of the gripper before the door opened. Table V documents the failures and corresponding program revisions in both histories. The revision after execution 16 adds an angle condition for accepting an end stop and a force-drop condition before pulling. These checks change how contact evidence governs transitions between actions, illustrating refinement of the force-feedback logic itself.
