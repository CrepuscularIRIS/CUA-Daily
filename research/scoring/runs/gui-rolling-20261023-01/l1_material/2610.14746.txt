title: BridgeWAM: Bridging Video and Action Experts via Latent Bridge Queries
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14746

§Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) commonly couple video and action experts through dense, layer-wise Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) attention, exposing action prediction to irrelevant visual features while enforcing matched expert depths. This coupling hinders selective visual-feature use and introduces redundant computation. We introduce Bridge-of-Experts (BoE), instantiated as BridgeWAM, which aggregates visual, language, and proprioceptive priors through 32 Latent Bridge Queries (LBQs) into a shared, limited latent space. Joint video-action supervision lets action gradients guide the Video Expert toward representations better suited for action prediction, while the Action Expert learns to selectively retrieve task-relevant information through cross-attention. Decoupling expert depths and reusing LBQs during action denoising yield a 5.06B model with a 30-layer Video Expert and a two-layer Action Expert, achieving 98.25% success on LIBERO and 72.24% pooled success on LIBERO-Plus, demonstrating effective task execution and strong generalization with an ultralight action head. Ablations support joint representation adaptation and unified LBQ conditioning. Relative to Fast-WAM, BridgeWAM reduces single-sample inference FLOPs by 28.72% in simulation profiling and real-robot latency to 77.1 ms (a 2.47-fold speedup). Qualitative experimental results further indicate preferential attention to target objects and task destinations across perturbations.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
World Action Models (WAMs) aim to predict robot actions from visual observations, language instructions, and proprioceptive state while retaining the broad visual priors of video-generation models. Useful WAMs must therefore simultaneously predict precise action chunks, remain robust when scenes and objects change, and run with low enough latency for closed-loop control. Recent WAMs have improved these properties by coupling a Video Expert and an Action Expert with Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) attention Ye et al. (2026) ; Bi et al. (2025) ; Li et al. (2026b) ; Yuan et al. (2026) . However, information transfer between the two modalities remains largely unchanged. WAM designs progressively simplify inference from joint video–action generation to lighter visual conditioning. Joint models process current and future video, action, and state tokens together Ye et al. (2026) . IDM-style models use asymmetric attention: the video branch operates independently while the action branch reads the visual sequence, but future-video generation remains necessary Li et al. (2026b) ; Zhang et al. (2026) . Fast-WAM then removes future-video generation at inference to reduce latency, retaining MoT’s direct video K/V interface to action attention Yuan et al. (2026) . However, these layer-wise MoT designs retain central limitations. First, each Video Expert layer directly exposes its dense visual features to the corresponding action layer by concatenating video and action keys/values, making task-relevant information and redundant details compete for attention. Prior work has found that action generation may allocate excessive attention to task-irrelevant visual regions, interfering with the use of task-relevant information and impairing task execution Li et al. (2026a) . Furthermore, reliance on task-irrelevant scene cues may weaken policy generalization under visual distribution shifts Sun et al. (2026) . Second, one-to-one layer coupling forces both experts to have equal depth, introducing redundant parameters and inference overhead. Excessive action-side capacity may favor memorizing specific scene patterns over learning generalizable environmental regularities, weakening generalization. WAMs therefore need a learnable interface that operates in a compact latent space and enables the Action Expert to allocate more attention to task-relevant visual features while allowing action supervision to shape visual representations. It should decouple expert depths, reducing redundant parameters and improving inference efficiency. We introduce Bridge-of-Experts (BoE), a new architecture that overcomes these limitations, and instantiate it as BridgeWAM , with an overview shown in Fig. 1 . As illustrated in Fig. 2 , BoE preserves modality-specific computation and connects the two experts through a compact set of Latent Bridge Queries ( LBQs ), rather than directly mixing video and action tokens. Within the Video Expert, 32 LBQs aggregate the visual, language, and proprioceptive priors needed for action generation into a shared, limited latent space, providing the Action Expert with latent features optimized for action generation through iterative learning. During end-to-end optimization, action-loss gradients flow through the LBQs into the Video Expert, guiding it to produce representations better suited to the Action Expert’s learning and prediction. BoE decouples the depths of the two experts. BridgeWAM combines a 30-layer Video Expert with a two-layer alternating cross–self-attention Action Expert: cross-attention retrieves information from the LBQs , while self-attention coordinates the action chunk. By retaining the backbone’s representational capacity with a lightweight action head, this design balances efficiency and performance. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce BoE, a video–action interface built on a limited latent space. BoE uses 32 LBQs to construct a limited latent space that aggregates visual, language, and proprioceptive priors into a unified latent representation tailored to the Action Expert. Joint video–action optimization sends action gradients through the LBQs to shape action-relevant video representations and trains the Action Expert to prioritize task-relevant visual information. • We develop BridgeWAM with decoupled expert depths to balance efficiency and performance. BridgeWAM pairs a 30-layer Video Expert with a two-layer alternating cross–self-attention Action Expert for LBQ readout and action-chunk coordination. The lightweight action head and reusable LBQ interface reduce redundancy in parameters and the cost of inference, yielding a 5.06 B-parameter BridgeWAM that retains the video backbone’s representational capacity while balancing performance and efficiency. • We demonstrate that BridgeWAM overcomes the above limitations of MoT through simulation and real-world task evaluations and precise ablation studies.
