title: CarryOn: A Dual-Loop Harness for Personalized and Reliable Robot Assistance
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21453

§Abstract
While generalist vision-language-action (VLA) models have enabled diverse open-world manipulation, real-world robotic assistance requires two critical capabilities they currently lack: personalization and reliable execution. First, executing an instruction like "make me a sandwich" requires grounding subtask decomposition in persistent user preferences rather than relying on a single generic solution. Second, reliable deployment demands explainable safety assurance that proactively prevents hazards and gracefully recovers from occasional physical failures. To address these gaps, we present CarryOn, a dual-loop harness designed to augment a frozen VLA policy. The planning loop maintains persistent user-level and scene-level context that is continually grounded against new observations and revised through interaction, enabling personalized task selection and execution grounding across conversations and changing scenes. The execution loop actively supervises policy rollouts, shielding actions from predicted hazardous contact. When execution fails, the system invokes reusable control-based recovery skills that restore task progress and are refined through reflection on earlier failures. On a real robot and in simulation, CarryOn dispatches preference-consistent subtasks across users and scenes, reduces the violation rate of an already safety-finetuned VLA from 52.6% to 6.1%, and increases recovery success from 13% to 80% when refined skills are reused.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Robot assistance ultimately aims to serve people in their everyday lives. Generalist vision-language-action (VLA) models have advanced this goal by learning to execute diverse manipulation instructions across tasks and environments [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Large-scale robot datasets further support learning across tasks and embodiments [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] . However, progress in instruction-conditioned manipulation alone does not bridge the gap to everyday assistance. A robot must know both what to do to meet the user’s needs in the current environment and how to carry it out reliably as physical conditions change. Bridging this gap requires a system that connects context-sensitive task decisions with reliable execution. A harness is the system surrounding a base model that orchestrates execution and decides how the model plans, perceives and manages context, stores artifacts, and evaluates results [ 7 ] . Language-agent systems illustrate how memory and reflection can support decisions across interactions [ 8 , 9 ] . In the field of robotics, hierarchical VLA systems connect high-level requests to manipulation instructions [ 10 ] . Recent concurrent works on embodied harnesses use memory to steer frozen VLAs and coordinate heterogeneous policies [ 11 , 12 ] , or evolve runtime critics and recovery skills from execution experience [ 13 ] . These works mainly focus on how to improve VLA models’ performance in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. However, for everyday assistance, reliable execution must also be coupled with deciding what is appropriate for the user in the current situation. We study this connection through personalized robot assistance, which exposes two challenges. First, task decisions must remain aligned with the user’s needs as interactions and scenes change, and the same request can require different actions. Learning preferences or identifying personal objects [ 14 , 15 ] provides context, while in reality the context can be dynamically changed and thus requires revision in stored memory. Second, the resulting plan must be supported by reliable physical execution: impending hazardous contact calls for intervention [ 16 ] to ensure safety, while a failure that has already occurred calls for repair [ 17 ] to ensure robustness. The harness must connect these responses to the intended task and retain failure experience to improve later recovery. Consider a user who asks a robot to “serve me food” (Fig. 1 ). The robot should respect an earlier vegetarian preference without requiring it to be repeated in the query. It may look beneath a remembered cover for a carrot, but finding meat instead must change its original plan. Even when the ingredient is suitable, an obstacle or a lost grasp can interrupt delivery to the bowl. The harness must preserve the user’s dietary requirement when observations change the plan, restore progress when a grasp fails, and use that failure to improve later recovery. We present CarryOn, a dual-loop harness that connects adaptive task decisions with reliable execution for personalized robot assistance. Its central design makes task context and recovery behavior persistent, revisable parts of the runtime around a frozen VLA. The planning loop determines what to do for the user and scene, and the execution loop protects and repairs the active subtask. Visual feedback connects them, informing plan revision and determining whether execution should advance or invoke recovery while retaining the task goal. The planning loop supports contextual adaptation through memories. User context expresses needs and preferences, while scene context helps determine where and how they can be fulfilled. The planner combines both with the current multimodal request and available observations to select an endpoint and supported subtasks. Dialogue revises user context, and observations trigger explicit scene-memory updates before subsequent plans proceed. Memory thus participates in a feedback loop: it guides action, while the resulting evidence updates what later plans can rely on. The execution loop supports physical reliability through fast proactive shielding and slower reactive recovery. A fast proactive shield predicts gripper and carried-object motion and filters commands against geometric contact constraints. After a failure, slower, event-triggered recovery skills repair the physical state so the interrupted task can continue. Offline reflection then uses failures of the current recovery version to revise its parameters and strategies, retaining successive versions in a skill library for later attempts. Immediate protection and reactive repair operate within an episode, and accumulated experience changes recovery behavior across attempts. This design keeps the VLA fixed while allowing the recovery pathway to improve through repeated reflection. Contributions.
