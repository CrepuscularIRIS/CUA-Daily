§Abstract
Generating accurate CAD models requires substantial engineering expertise and careful control of geometry, topology, and mechanical constraints. Text-to-CAD aims to simplify this process, but current LLM-based methods often generate CAD programs directly. Language models remain unreliable when producing long code while coordinating coupled geometric, topological, and dimensional constraints. Consequently, executable programs may still produce structurally incorrect models, which execution rate and rendered appearance may fail to reveal. To address these limitations, we propose GOPR-CAD, an agentic Text-to-CAD pipeline based on Graph-of-Operations Progressive Reasoning. A language model acts as a decision agent over encapsulated CAD operations and progressively constructs an executable operation graph, which a deterministic compiler converts into the final CAD model. This separation requires no additional training and supports different language models. To evaluate structural fidelity, we construct MHCAD, a benchmark of 300 standard mechanical part requirements derived from Machinery's Handbook. Their explicit structures enable reference-free STEP/B-rep evaluation. GOPR-CAD achieves 82.7% Structural Yield on MHCAD, and 87.2% Structural Yield on an external CADPrompt subset.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Computer-aided design (CAD) models are fundamental to mechanical design, manufacturing, and engineering analysis. Compared with rendered images and polygonal meshes, CAD models encode exact geometry within an explicit topological structure and retain an editable construction history. This representation supports reliable downstream processing throughout the engineering workflow. A useful CAD model must therefore go beyond a recognizable shape. It must be geometrically precise, topologically valid, and editable as an engineering artifact. Recent research has increasingly automated CAD construction from geometric observations and large CAD collections. Reconstruction methods recover analytic geometry from point clouds, then assemble it into a valid topological structure ( Liu et al. 2024 ) . Sequence models learn ordered construction histories built from sketches followed by feature operations ( Wu et al. 2021 ; Willis et al. 2021 ; Xu et al. 2022 ) . Other methods directly synthesize native boundary representations or parametric surfaces ( Xu et al. 2024 ; Usama et al. 2026 ) . These advances have improved geometric recovery and made editable CAD generation more practical. Most still require geometric input or prior CAD examples, leaving users to translate design intent into specialized representations. A more accessible interface should allow the intended model to be described directly in natural language. Text-to-CAD provides such an interface by translating natural language requirements into editable CAD models. It simplifies the modeling process by allowing users to express design intent without mastering specialized CAD commands. Existing methods map language to parametric command sequences or structured CAD representations ( Khan et al. 2024 ; Wang et al. 2025a ; Wang et al. 2025b ; Usama et al. 2026 ) . More recent work connects large language models (LLMs) with scriptable interfaces such as CadQuery, using executable programs to bridge language understanding and parametric modeling ( Xie and Ju 2025 ; Guan et al. 2026 ; Alrashedy et al. 2025 ) . These programs can be executed by mature CAD kernels and remain available for subsequent editing. Together, these advances make natural language a practical interface for creating parametric CAD models. However, direct program generation remains unreliable for complex mechanical parts, as an LLM must preserve geometric, topological, and dimensional constraints throughout a long sequence of dependent instructions. Errors introduced early can propagate through later construction, while the CAD kernel may still accept the resulting program and export a valid solid that does not satisfy the requirement. Existing evaluation protocols often fail to expose this gap. Execution-based metrics verify artifact validity, while rendered views may miss errors in internal structure. Comparison with a single reference is also restrictive, as the same requirement can admit different valid constructions. Reliable Text-to-CAD therefore requires a more dependable construction process and an evaluation protocol that measures structural fidelity directly on the generated CAD model. To address these challenges, we propose GOPR-CAD , an agentic Text-to-CAD pipeline based on Graph-of-Operations Progressive Reasoning . A language model acts as a decision agent over encapsulated CAD operations and progressively constructs an executable operation graph. A deterministic compiler then realizes the graph as the final CAD model. This separation lets the language model focus on construction decisions while the CAD operation library handles geometric realization. It also decouples the agent backbone from the CAD system, allowing different language models to be substituted without additional training or modifications to the remaining pipeline. We further construct MHCAD to evaluate the structural failures that conventional metrics overlook. The benchmark contains 300 requirements for standard mechanical parts derived from Machinery’s Handbook . Standard parts specify precise features, topology, and dimensional relations, making them suitable for testing whether a generated model preserves the intended mechanical structure. MHCAD derives structural checks from each requirement and applies them to reimported STEP/B-rep models without assuming a unique reference construction. Structural Fidelity (SF) measures structural correctness among valid B-reps, whereas Structural Yield (SY) measures correct structural delivery across the full benchmark. ## Method
Overview GOPR-CAD formulates Text-to-CAD as Graph-of-Operations Progressive Reasoning . Given a natural language requirement x , a language model acts as a decision agent over a fixed library of executable CAD operations. The agent develops an explicit operation graph, while the geometric implementation of each operation and the final compilation process remain deterministic. Let \mathcal{O}=\{o_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M} denote the operation library. Each operation is associated with a contract o_{j}=\left(d_{j},\mathcal{R}_{j},\Theta_{j},\kappa_{j}\right), where d_{j} describes its mechanical role, \mathcal{R}_{j} specifies the relations in which it may participate, \Theta_{j} defines its required parameter interface, and \kappa_{j} binds the operation to a deterministic CAD implementation. The agent selects and organizes operations without generating their internal geometry. The graph is completed through four successive transformations: x\xrightarrow{\Phi_{D}^{\theta}}D\xrightarrow{\Phi_{O}^{\theta}}G_{O}\xrightarrow{\Phi_{R}^{\theta}}G_{R}\xrightarrow{\Phi_{P}}G_{P}. Here D represents construction intent, G_{O} contains the selected operation nodes, G_{R} adds typed mechanical relations, and G_{P} contains complete parameter bindings. The superscript \theta denotes decisions made by the language model. Parameter completion \Phi_{P} follows deterministic rules defined by the operation contracts. Each stage introduces one type of commitment: intent, operations, relations, and parameters. The first three stages form a constrained agent process. Let H_{t} denote the current structured state and define s_{t}=(x,H_{t},\mathcal{O}). At stage t , the agent selects a structured decision and updates the state: a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}\!\left(\cdot\mid s_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t}(s_{t})\right),\qquad H_{t+1}=\mathcal{U}_{t}(H_{t},a_{t}), where \mathcal{A}_{t}(s_{t}) is the set of decisions allowed by the current stage and the operation contracts. The contracts restrict each stage to choices supported by the operation interfaces, removing type-incompatible or non-executable decisions before CAD realization. This interface constraint does not guarantee that the agent selects a prompt-consistent design; its practical effect is evaluated through the typed-interface ablations. Because the contracts and compiler are independent of the policy, the agent backbone can be replaced without retraining or changes to the CAD system. Design: Representing Construction Intent The Design stage establishes a persistent representation of what the requirement asks the CAD model to contain: D=\Phi_{D}^{\theta}(x),\qquad D=(C,\Lambda). Here C=\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} is a set of construction items, and \Lambda stores the evidence associated with them. The evidence includes mechanical roles, feature counts, layout cues, visible structures, relation cues, and parameter information stated in the requirement. The resulting representation provides a common semantic reference for all later stages. Recall uses it to select operations, Relation uses it to recover mechanical dependencies, and Parameters uses it to resolve values and constraints. Maintaining this shared reference prevents the later stages from relying on separate interpretations of the original requirement. The Design state records the required construction structure while leaving executable realization to subsequent stages. Recall: Grounding Intent in Executable Operations Recall maps each construction item to operations from the fixed library. The library is organized into capability families \mathcal{F}=\{F_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}, where each family groups operations that serve related mechanical roles. For each construction item c_{i} , Recall first selects a capability family: F_{i}=R_{\mathrm{coarse}}\!\left(c_{i},\Lambda_{i},\mathcal{F}\right). It then selects one or more concrete operations from that family: V_{i}=R_{\mathrm{fine}}\!\left(c_{i},\Lambda_{i},\mathcal{O}(F_{i})\right), where \mathcal{O}(F_{i}) is the subset of operations in F_{i} . The complete node set and operation graph are V_{O}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}V_{i},\qquad G_{O}=(V_{O},\alpha), where \alpha:V_{O}\rightarrow C links every selected operation to the construction item that motivated it. This evidence mapping makes operation selection traceable to the requirement. The two step selection process reduces the number of operations considered for each item while retaining composition across mechanical roles. Since every node is selected from \mathcal{O} , it already has a known parameter interface and an implementation in the CAD system. The language model therefore selects executable building blocks without inventing geometric procedures. Relation: Composing the Operation Graph A set of valid operations does not yet define a coherent mechanical model. Their ownership, alignment, containment, layout, and construction dependencies must be explicit. Relation augments G_{O} with typed edges: G_{R}=(V_{O},E_{R},\alpha), where E_{R}\subseteq V_{O}\times\mathcal{T}_{R}\times V_{O}. We use the relation vocabulary \mathcal{T}_{R}=\{\mathrm{host},\mathrm{align},\mathrm{contain},\mathrm{refine},\mathrm{pattern},\mathrm{depend}\}. The relations \mathrm{host} and \mathrm{contain} describe feature ownership and enclosure. The relations \mathrm{align} and \mathrm{pattern} express shared axes, orientations, and repeated layouts. The relations \mathrm{refine} and \mathrm{depend} represent construction and parameter dependencies. Relation decisions are restricted by the operation contracts. For an edge e_{uv}=(v_{u},\tau_{uv},v_{v}), the selected type must satisfy \tau_{uv}\in\Gamma(o_{u},o_{v}), where \Gamma(o_{u},o_{v}) is the set of relations supported by the contracts of the two operations. Feature ownership, shared axes, containment, repeated layout, and parameter dependencies are therefore available in the graph before CAD execution. Parameters: Completing Operation Interfaces The Parameters stage resolves the interfaces of the selected operations. Values may come directly from the requirement, be propagated through graph relations, or be derived through rules defined for each operation: P=P_{\mathrm{req}}\cup P_{\mathrm{rel}}\cup P_{\mathrm{rule}}. Here P_{\mathrm{req}} contains dimensions and counts stated in the requirement, P_{\mathrm{rel}} contains values shared through alignment, hosting, and pattern relations, and P_{\mathrm{rule}} contains values determined by operation constraints and valid parameter domains. Parameter completion follows the dependency structure of the graph. Let V^{(\ell)} contain the operations assigned to dependency level \ell . Early levels usually contain primary bodies and reference geometry, while later levels contain hosted features, repeated structures, and refinements. Parameters are accumulated as P=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{L}P^{(\ell)}, where P^{(\ell)}=\Phi_{P}^{(\ell)}\!\left(D,G_{R},V^{(\ell)},P^{(<\ell)}\right),\qquad P^{(<\ell)}=\bigcup_{j<\ell}P^{(j)}. This ordering allows a later operation to use frames, axes, dimensions, and counts that have already been resolved for its dependencies. The completed graph is G_{P}=(V_{O},E_{R},P,\alpha). It is ready for execution when every required operation parameter is available: \forall v\in V_{O},\qquad\operatorname{Req}(v)\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(P_{v}), and every edge satisfies the relation contracts of its incident operations. Unresolved values are retained as typed parameter errors so incomplete commitments remain visible. Once G_{P} is complete, a deterministic compiler converts the graph into an executable CAD operation plan: \Pi=\mathcal{C}(G_{P}),\qquad y=\mathcal{K}(\Pi), where \mathcal{C} is the compiler, \mathcal{K} is the CAD kernel, and y is the resulting CAD model. No further language model decisions are introduced during realization.
