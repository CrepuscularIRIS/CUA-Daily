title: SCI-AGENT-EXAM: Benchmarking Agents on Scientific Research Tasks
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32441

§Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly capable of supporting scientific workflows, but their capabilities across diverse research activities remain difficult to assess systematically. Existing benchmarks often focus on selected scientific capabilities or task types, limiting a comprehensive characterization of scientific agents' strengths and weaknesses in realistic research workflows. We introduce SCI-AGENT-EXAM, a workflow-oriented benchmark comprising 120 expert-curated tasks across 11 scientific fields and 7 research task types defined by the Scientific Workflow Task Taxonomy (SWTT). Each task requires both executable artifacts (e.g., code and scripts) and textual artifacts (e.g., reports and explanations). To evaluate the heterogeneous task outputs, we develop a hybrid evaluation protocol combining deterministic programmatic verification for executable artifacts with a calibrated, expert-validated LLM-based judge for textual artifacts. Beyond outcome assessment, we analyze progress and solution stability along execution trajectories to characterize how agents reach their final results. Experiments with 10 frontier LLM agents reveal substantial performance gaps: GPT-6 Astra achieves the highest overall score of 80.9, yet only 26.0% of its runs exceed the 0.95 success threshold. Further analyses uncover distinct capability profiles across scientific task types and substantial variation in execution efficiency and stability. Our code and data are available at https://github.com/SKYLENAGE-AI/Sci-Agent-Exam.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from systems that primarily generate and transform text into agents capable of multi-step reasoning, tool use, code execution, and interaction with external environments [ 42 , 30 , 7 ] . These capabilities enable LLM agents to move beyond answering individual scientific questions and participate in broader scientific workflows. Each scientific workflow task specifies a scientific objective, required deliverables, and evaluation criteria, enabling assessment under controlled execution conditions. Evaluating scientific agents thus requires examining what they produce, whether they can execute and explain their analyses, and how reliably they reach the results. This raises a central question: What can scientific agents deliver across diverse research activities, and how efficient and stable is their execution and presentation? Existing benchmarks have made substantial progress in evaluating scientific agents across diverse settings, including scientific question answering [ 36 ] , scientific discovery [ 24 ] and analysis [ 7 ] , research coding [ 15 , 31 ] , paper replication [ 32 ] , project-level research [ 45 ] , research in a single field [ 19 ] and executable scientific workflows [ 5 , 35 ] . However, as summarized in Figure 1 and detailed in Table 1 , two limitations remain in existing benchmarks. First, their explicit coverage of scientific activities remains uneven, with some activities only implicitly involved, partially covered, or not covered. This limits a systematic characterization of agents’ strengths and weaknesses across research activities. Second, their coverage of task deliverables remains incomplete. Scientific work not only requires agents to reach answers through executable results but also requires them to explain their analyses in writing. Yet the benchmarks compared in Table 1 do not consistently require and jointly evaluate all three forms of deliverables (answers, executable artifacts, and textual artifacts) for every task. Beyond these two limitations, final-answer scores alone provide limited insight into execution efficiency and solution stability: they do not reveal how many steps agents take to reach a result or how solution quality changes during execution. A workflow-oriented evaluation should therefore combine systematic coverage of scientific activities with comprehensive assessment of task deliverables and analysis of execution dynamics. To address these evaluation needs, we introduce Sci-Agent-Exam , a workflow-oriented benchmark for evaluating LLM agents in scientific research activities. First, Sci-Agent-Exam proposes the Scientific Workflow Task Taxonomy (SWTT) , which organizes scientific work into seven task types: Literature Synthesis, Hypothesis Validation, Tool Orchestration, Code Generation, Error Diagnosis, End-to-End Analysis, and Result Interpretation. The benchmark comprises 120 tasks spanning 11 scientific domains, with each task assigned to one task type and constructed and verified by researchers with relevant domain expertise. This taxonomy-guided design enables systematic comparison of agents’ capabilities across different scientific activities. Second, every task requires three forms of deliverables: a final structured answer, executable artifacts (e.g., code and scripts), and textual artifacts (e.g., reports and explanations).
