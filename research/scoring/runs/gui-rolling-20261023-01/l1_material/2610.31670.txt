title: Sensorimotor Alignment: A Platonic Proxy for Embodied Policy Success
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31670

§Abstract
Robot foundation models are typically evaluated through costly closed-loop rollouts, motivating low-cost offline proxies for model capability. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) suggests that as representations improve, modalities such as vision and language converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. We ask whether motion trajectories form another such modality: as embodied models become more capable, should their sensory representations converge toward the structure of physical motion? To test this, we propose Sensorimotor Alignment ($S^2$), a training-free offline metric that measures how well a model's sensory representation aligns with the geometry of physical motion. We find that $S^2$ correlates positively with downstream robot task success across model families, making it a low-cost offline proxy for prioritizing models before closed-loop evaluation. $S^2$ can also identify promising vision-language model (VLM) backbones before costly fine-tuning into vision-language-action models (VLAs). Beyond this correlation, $S^2$ offers representation-level explanations for phenomena observed in VLAs and world action models (WAMs). With $S^2$, we extend PRH to robotics and make sensorimotor alignment measurable, offering a low-cost, representation-level view of robot foundation models that closed-loop success rates alone cannot reveal.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Robot foundation models increasingly build on large pretrained vision-language models [ 4 , 3 ] , video models [ 50 ] , and world action models [ 15 , 28 , 5 ] . Yet selecting and evaluating these models remains expensive: the most reliable test is still closed-loop rollout in simulation [ 30 , 33 ] or the real world [ 1 , 9 ] , where evaluation is slow, noisy, and benchmark-dependent. This expense motivates low-cost offline proxies for policy capability. A natural approach is to use existing perception-side proxies, such as visual question answering scores for VLMs [ 31 , 57 ] or pixel-level video quality for world models [ 18 , 52 , 23 ] . However, these metrics measure passive perception or generation quality, not whether a representation is organized for action [ 59 , 24 , 47 , 35 ] . Two scenes may look visually different but require similar robot motions; conversely, two visually similar scenes may demand different trajectories. For robot task success, a model must produce different motions across tasks even in similar scenes. This requires aligning the sensory representation with the motor representation. This perspective connects robotics to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] , which argues that as models of different modalities scale and solve diverse tasks, they are converging to a shared statistical model of reality in each of their own representation spaces. For example, images and text are two projections of the same underlying world. As vision and language models scale, they increasingly agree on the relative distances between data points. We bring PRH to robotics and ask: can physical robot motion be viewed as another projection of that same world, such that more capable embodied models better align their vision and language representations with the structure of motion trajectories? We test this prediction of PRH: the more capable an embodied model is, the more closely its sensory representations align with its motor representation. In other words, episodes nearby in sensory representation space should also be nearby in motor representation space. We formalize this as Sensorimotor Alignment ( \mathrm{S}^{2} ), a training-free metric, illustrated in Figure 1 . For each episode in a robot dataset, a model’s image and text features form its sensory representation, and the recorded end-effector trajectory forms its motor representation. We measure alignment by comparing each episode’s nearest neighbors in sensory and motor spaces. More overlap between the two neighbor sets means stronger sensorimotor alignment. In Section 3 , we show that \mathrm{S}^{2} correlates positively with downstream robot task success across model families, in simulated and real-world settings. The correlation is strong for Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) in closed-loop manipulation, indicating that models with higher sensorimotor alignment tend to perform better. \mathrm{S}^{2} computed on raw Vision-Language Models (VLMs) also correlates with the task success of the resulting VLAs, more strongly than their visual question answering (VQA) scores do. For World Action Models (WAMs), \mathrm{S}^{2} likewise correlates more strongly with task success than video-quality metrics such as FID [ 18 ] , SSIM [ 52 ] , and PSNR [ 19 ] , and for real-robot policies, \mathrm{S}^{2} computed offline correlates with physical-robot task success on a shared set of tasks. Across these settings, sensorimotor alignment correlates with task success more closely than VQA or video-quality metrics. Prior work has observed several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs only through closed-loop results, without explaining them at the representation level. We use \mathrm{S}^{2} to supply that explanation, as shown in Section 4 . For example, fine-tuned VLAs often ignore the language instruction and act mainly on what they see; we find that their task success correlates with the \mathrm{S}^{2} of their vision features, not their text features. Our contributions are threefold: • Inspired by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, we propose a new view of embodiment: more capable embodied models should align their sensory representations more closely with the structure of motion trajectories. To test this, we introduce \mathrm{S}^{2} , a training-free offline metric that measures alignment between sensory and motor representations. • Across model families, in simulation and on real robots, \mathrm{S}^{2} correlates positively with task success, making it a low-cost offline proxy for choosing models before closed-loop evaluation. • Furthermore, we use \mathrm{S}^{2} to explain, at the representation level, several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs that were previously observed only through closed-loop results, making it a diagnostic tool for understanding how design choices shape embodied models.
