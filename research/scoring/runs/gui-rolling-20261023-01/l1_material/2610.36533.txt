title: RoboAuto: Autonomous Robot Program Search through Multi-Level Physical Understanding
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36533

§Abstract
Recently, LLM-based autonomous agents have shown strong capabilities in iteratively generating, evaluating, and refining executable solutions. In robotic manipulation, Code-as-Policy lets these agents search over modular and editable robot programs. However, effective program search requires physical understanding: agents must understand how objects can be manipulated, how scene geometry constrains actions, and why interactions succeed or fail. Existing methods rely mainly on execution feedback for program refinement, often leaving these physical questions unresolved. We introduce RoboAuto, a framework for robot program search that explicitly organizes physical understanding at the object, scene, and interaction levels through three components. First, a hierarchical knowledge base combines executable skills with object-centric knowledge of affordances, spatial relations, and action effects, learned from embodied data and online task solving. Second, scene grounding connects this knowledge to the current environment by identifying relevant objects and resolving action-critical quantities. Third, multi-stage planning with self-designed experiments checks whether each stage produces its expected physical effects and actively tests competing hypotheses to resolve physical uncertainties. RoboAuto achieves superior performance across five benchmark families, attaining an 89.1% success rate on short-horizon LIBERO-PRO tasks and more than twice the mean success rate of ASPIRE on LIBERO-PRO Long and LIBERO-Plus. It also outperforms ASPIRE and $π_{0.5}$ on three challenging long-horizon real-robot tasks requiring precise manipulation. We further show that the knowledge base transfers across simulators and embodiments, and that program search performance scales with its size. Project page: https://roboauto-ai.github.io/

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Recent advances in autonomous research and coding agents built on large language models (LLMs) [ 39 , 26 , 47 ] highlight the promise of a self-evolving problem-solving paradigm in which an agent generates an executable solution, evaluates its outcome, and uses the resulting evidence to improve subsequent attempts. Code-as-Policy [ 23 ] naturally extends this paradigm to robot manipulation by representing robot behaviors as executable programs. Given an instruction such as “put the mug on the plate,” a coding agent can generate a program over perception and control primitives, execute it in the environment, and revise it based on the resulting rollout. Recent autonomous Code-as-Policy methods instantiate this generate, execute, and revise loop. CaP-Agent0 supports multi-turn program revision with structured feedback [ 13 ] ; ASPIRE exposes multimodal execution traces and accumulates validated repair knowledge in a skill library [ 25 ] ; and RHO searches over policy repositories using reward and execution feedback [ 11 ] . These methods demonstrate the value of execution feedback and retained experience for robot program search. However, Figure 3 reveals persistent gaps in physical understanding in our baseline runs. In Figure 3 (a), CaP-Agent0 repeatedly attempts a physically unsuitable top-down grasp on a book, indicating that repeated code-level search cannot always compensate for missing knowledge of object affordances. This type of failure motivates a hierarchical knowledge base that complements object-centric physical knowledge acquired from embodied data with executable skills accumulated through online task solving. In Figure 3 (b), ASPIRE attempts to grasp a mug by descending to a hardcoded height transferred from another mug, causing the gripper to close on air. This failure shows that even a valid strategy must be instantiated using the geometry of the current scene, motivating a dedicated grounding stage that identifies task-relevant objects and physical quantities with visual tools. In Figure 3 (c), CaP-Agent0 proceeds to place a pan despite failing to activate the burner, motivating multi-stage planning and grounded verification of intermediate effects. Moreover, observing a failed knob rotation does not establish how far the knob must turn. Resolving this uncertainty requires active physical exploration through autonomously designed experiments that test specific hypotheses. Motivated by these observations, we introduce RoboAuto ( Fig. 2 ), a framework for autonomous robot program search through multi-level physical understanding . Its hierarchical knowledge base combines object-centric physical knowledge about affordances, spatial relations, and action effects with executable skills. Physical knowledge is acquired from embodied data and refined through online experience, while the skill library expands as verified programs are discovered through task solving. For each task, RoboAuto retrieves relevant knowledge and skills and grounds them in the current scene, using visual tools, including Molmo2 [ 10 ] and SAM 3 [ 4 ] , to identify task-relevant objects and measure action-critical quantities. Through multi-stage planning with grounded verification , it plans over stage dependencies and verifies each stage’s physical outcome before proceeding. When available evidence leaves a physical uncertainty unresolved, RoboAuto formulates competing hypotheses and conducts self-designed experiments to distinguish them. The resulting evidence refines the physical knowledge, while verified programs expand the skill library, improving both the current solution and future program search. We evaluate RoboAuto on tasks from five benchmark families. On LIBERO-PRO Long [ 52 ] , RoboAuto achieves a mean success rate of 73.4%, compared with 30.8% for ASPIRE [ 25 ] , the strongest coding-agent baseline on average. On LIBERO-Plus [ 12 ] , it reaches 90.3%, compared with ASPIRE’s 44.7%, while achieving 89.1% on the short-horizon LIBERO-PRO [ 52 ] tasks. On contact-rich Robosuite [ 54 ] tasks, RoboAuto obtains 90.6% mean success, compared with ASPIRE’s 81.0%. To evaluate knowledge transfer, we apply the hierarchical knowledge base acquired in LIBERO [ 24 ] to RoboTwin 2.0 [ 6 ] , which features a different simulator and a bimanual robot embodiment. RoboAuto achieves a 91.5% success rate, compared with 24.5% without a knowledge base. Knowledge-base scaling experiments further show that larger knowledge bases consistently improve program-search performance. RoboAuto also outperforms ASPIRE and \pi_{0.5} on three challenging long-horizon real-robot tasks requiring precise manipulation.
