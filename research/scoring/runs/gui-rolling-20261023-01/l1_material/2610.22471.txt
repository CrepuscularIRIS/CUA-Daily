title: Has GPT-6-Astra Achieved Embodied Intelligence?
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22471

§Abstract
GPT-6-Astra demonstrates a level of competence in robotic manipulation and navigation that challenges the conventional paradigm of building specialized models for individual tasks and embodiments. In this report, we ask not only how capable GPT-6-Astra is, but what its capabilities imply for the field. First, we probe the limits of standalone GPT-6-Astra. We conduct a large-scale evaluation across 12 representative benchmarks spanning tabletop manipulation, humanoids, mobile platforms, and dexterous hands. Under a unified protocol that allows in-context learning (ICL) from training-set demonstrations and substantially increases the number of execution steps, GPT-6-Astra achieves unprecedented performance (Fig. 1). Future research may focus on improving 1) the generality of ICL 2) cost and execution time, 3) physical-state verification, 4) recovery from irreversible failures, and 5) long-horizon preservation of progress. Second, we study the agentic harness for embodied control. We identify that close-loop coordination between a high-level "System 2" reasoner (i.e., GPT-6-Astra) and a low-level "System 1" controller (e.g., π0.5) is costly but effective. Third, we examine GPT-6-Astra on real-world deployment. Our experiments on 8 robot configurations reveal substantial gaps: 1) robot demonstrations required for ICL are expensive to collect, and 2) it is implausible to trade longer execution for higher success rates with physical constraints. Fourth, we envision the next generation of embodied benchmarks. We develop a prototype that 1) derives tasks from real-world needs, 2) provides no in-domain demonstrations, and 3) judges agents on a world clock that keeps running while they deliberate. Taken together, our results suggest that GPT-6-Astra has largely crossed the threshold of embodied competence, but not yet that of practical embodied intelligence.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The advent of GPT-6-Astra marks a major shift in embodied intelligence: a general-purpose model can now tackle robotic manipulation and navigation tasks that have traditionally been dominated by specialized models, such as VLAs and WAMs, while exhibiting capabilities including zero-shot generalization, in-context learning, and failure recovery. In this technical report, we use GPT-6-Astra as a lens to examine a broader question: what does this new level of embodied capability mean for the research community, and where should the field go next? We first conduct a large-scale evaluation across diverse embodied tasks to characterize the extent of its capabilities and identify which problems are already approaching saturation. We then study harness design to understand the performance–cost trade-offs involved in translating these capabilities into effective control. Next, we validate our findings on real robots, asking how far benchmark success carries over to physical deployment and what gaps remain.Finally, we revisit benchmark design and envision what the next generation of embodied benchmarks should look like. First, we probe the upper bound of GPT-6-Astra’s embodied capabilities under a unified protocol. We conduct a large-scale study across 12 representative benchmarks, covering tabletop robotic arms, humanoids, mobile platforms, and dexterous hands. Under this protocol, GPT-6-Astra outperforms existing methods by substantial margins, including recent agentic approaches that also build on GPT-6-Astra. Notably, as shown in Fig. 1 , it approaches saturation on the LIBERO series and SIMPLE, while achieving 81.9% on RoboDojo and 87.3% on DexJoCo. The protocol follows two simple and well-motivated principles: 1) We allow in-context learning (ICL) from the training set whenever successful demonstrations are available, which we consider reasonable since such demonstrations are conventionally accessible when training smaller task-specific policies. GPT-6-Astra studies the available training data and constructs a frozen knowledge package containing task guidance, motion and visual references, executable helpers, recovery procedures, and metadata. At test time, it retrieves and adapts these resources to the current scene. This substantially improves performance when the demonstrations closely cover the test distribution, but the benefit weakens as tasks move away from the data used to build the package. 2) We extensively increase the execution budget , allowing up to 10 \times more interaction steps than the official setting. We find that standard step limits can significantly underestimate GPT-6-Astra’s capabilities. Additional interaction yields large gains on tasks that benefit from repeated correction, such as RoboDojo and Bench2Dex, because GPT-6-Astra can detect failed actions, revise its strategy, and recover through further interaction. This emerging failure-recovery capability is the key mechanism that allows a larger execution budget to translate into higher success rates. Our strong empirical results put us in a position to identify the remaining limitations of GPT-6-Astra more clearly. Specifically, we find that: i) in-context learning transfer poorly beyond the tasks from which the demonstrations are drawn; ii) even simple tasks incur inference cost and execution time far beyond what would be practical for commercial deployment; iii) GPT-6-Astra often struggles to verify physical success and identify which task condition remains unmet; iv) GPT-6-Astra often struggles to identify the cause of task failure; v) it cannot reliably recover from consequential or irreversible failures; and vi) long-horizon execution remains difficult, as the model struggles to preserve and track progress across many subtasks. Second, we ablate the harness design and find that effectively unleashing GPT-6-Astra’s embodied performance depends critically on both the collaboration protocol and the action interface. We first study the interaction between “System 2” ( i.e. , GPT-6-Astra) and “System 1” ( e.g. , \pi_{0.5} [ 13 ] ). Closed-loop collaboration, where GPT-6-Astra reviews and refines VLA-proposed actions before execution, substantially improves performance over standalone or open-loop execution, but frequent System-2 supervision incurs significant token cost. Reviewing longer action sequences improves this trade-off, while combining closed-loop collaboration with ICL yields the strongest performance at the highest cost. We further study action representation and chunking, and identify a representation gap between multimodal reasoning and low-level robot control. Direct joint-angle control underperforms, while more semantically aligned end-effector commands work better; adaptive action chunking further improves performance while reducing unnecessary model calls.
