title: AndroidFlux: Evaluating and Reward Modeling Failure Recovery Capabilities in Mobile Use Multimodal Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34384

§Abstract
Mobile-use multimodal agents are typically evaluated by task success from a clean initial state, which does not isolate their ability to recover from intermediate errors. Because mobile environments change dynamically, a general mobile-use agent must complete tasks robustly even from unexpected states and in unseen environments. This requires knowing when and where an agent makes errors, and whether it can recover from errors and subsequent states. To evaluate this capability, we introduce AndroidFlux, a failure-recovery benchmark built on AndroidWorld. AndroidFlux restores recorded trajectories to controlled checkpoints before, at, and after the first recoverable error, so that different agents continue from the same device state and history. On these checkpoints, a complementary reward-modeling benchmark evaluates recovery-action preferences using downstream rollout outcomes. We further release an offline reinforcement learning (RL) dataset that improves both error recovery and overall task success. Our evaluations show that recovery performance only partially correlates with clean-start success, and that explicit reasoning helps only models trained with GUI-domain specific RL.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Multimodal agents now operate real mobile devices, completing user tasks by reading screens and performing touch and text actions over long horizons ( Rawles et al., 2024 ; Xu et al., 2024 ; Kong et al., 2025 ) . Existing benchmarks evaluate these agents almost entirely by task success from a clean start, and the strongest models are nearing saturation on them. Yet, a clear gap still remains between benchmark scores and in-the-wild performance. Part of the reason is that a single pass or fail per episode hides what happens inside it: the intermediate failures behind the outcome and which actions helped or hurt along the way. Because mobile environments change dynamically, a general mobile-use agent must complete tasks robustly even from unexpected states and in unseen environments. This requires knowing when and where an agent makes errors, and whether it can recover from errors and subsequent states. Recovery matters even more in recent agentic systems, which increasingly optimize inference cost by assigning easier tasks to smaller agents ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Ong et al., 2024 ) . When a smaller agent stalls, these systems often hand over the unfinished task to a stronger model ( Wei et al., 2026 ) , which must then recover from mistakes it did not make, in a context and device state it did not produce. However, observing these behaviors through clean-start rollouts is expensive: failure states arise only by chance, each one costs a full episode to reach, and the score reflects the whole episode rather than the specific steps where recovery succeeds or fails. Although measuring failure recovery is critical for closing the gap to real-world deployment and for building efficient agentic systems, no existing mobile-use benchmark directly evaluates this capability. To address this, we introduce AndroidFlux (Figure 1 ) to evaluate the failure recovery and recovery-action reward modeling capabilities through controlled replay of policy-induced failures in AndroidWorld. We annotate the first error step t and restore checkpoints at t{-}n (correct prefix), t (error committed), and t{+}1 (after a positive or negative recovery action). Target agents resume from matched device states with histories re-rendered in their own formats, allowing us to examine how policies handle the same failure and how recovery depends on the preceding actions. Across human-verified and larger LLM-verified sets, recovery performance only partially correlates with clean-start success, showing that existing benchmarks alone are insufficient for assessing an agent’s robustness. We find that explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning also does not consistently improve recovery: general reasoning ability alone does not ensure effective action in a disrupted GUI state. On our reward-modeling benchmark, reward models trained on generic GUI preferences stay near chance, while GUI experts’ own action likelihoods rank recovery actions better, and the two are correct on largely different pairs, suggesting they are complementary. Furthermore, we release an offline RL dataset of 8.5k decision contexts that improves both recovery and clean-start success, offering a practical resource for studying failure recovery without expensive online RL training.
