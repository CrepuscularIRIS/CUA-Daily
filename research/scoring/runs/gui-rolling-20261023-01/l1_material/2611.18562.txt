arxiv: 2611.18562 (2611.18562v1)
title: Training Reasoning Models in Simulation to Natively Use Real Robots
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18562

§Abstract
Robot-use agents (RUAs) have emerged as a promising approach to general-purpose robot control. However, strong performance has so far been achieved only by frontier proprietary models such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2, whereas open-weight models remain substantially less capable. We show that an open-weight 27B reasoning model can be trained to use real robots natively through reinforcement learning, entirely in simulation and without demonstrations. Like a computer-use agent (CUA), the model observes the environment, reasons in a chain of thought, and generates the next end-effector pose, without any harness. We build a Post-training Engine for Agentic Robotics (PEAR) and use it to train the model on simulated robot tasks at scale with robot-use reinforcement learning (RURL), mirroring how frontier models acquire computer use. We find that the skills acquired in simulation generalize well beyond the training tasks: they transfer to an unseen robot, to the real world, and to novel instructions. After training on LIBERO and RoboDojo in simulation, the model succeeds in 60% of our trials on a real dual-arm robot, whereas the base model succeeds in none. On LIBERO, training raises the success rate from 1.6% to 74.4%, approaching GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol at maximum reasoning effort. Compared with $π_{0.5}$, a vision-language-action model fine-tuned on demonstrations, our model is substantially more robust to changes in the task itself. On LIBERO-Pro, $π_{0.5}$ succeeds in only 0.75% of episodes when assigned a new task in a familiar scene whereas our model succeeds in 57.4%. Finally, the model retains its general capabilities, with no measurable degradation on nine reasoning, coding and vision benchmarks. These results highlight robot-use reinforcement learning as a promising component of foundation-model post-training.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Robot-use agents (RUAs) have recently become a promising direction for general-purpose robot control ( Isola, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026c ; Zhang et al., 2026c ; Lu et al., 2026 ) . Similar to a computer-use agent (CUA) that operates a computer from screenshots ( OpenAI, 2025a ; Anthropic, 2024 ) , an RUA uses a vision-language model (VLM) that observes the robot’s camera images, reasons about the task, and outputs the next end-effector pose. Because the VLM operates the robot directly, RUAs take advantage of the broad world knowledge and the strong reasoning ability of a capable foundation model, which allows them to generalize and to complete complex tasks. RUAs built on frontier models such as GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026b ; Pikus et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) and Claude Fable 5.2 ( Anthropic, 2026 ) have shown exceptional generalization and language following. On RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) and RoboLab ( Yang et al., 2026b ; Yang et al., 2026a ; SenseNova et al., 2026 ) , agents built on GPT-6 Astra have outperformed the best previous policies. In contrast, vision-language-action models (VLAs) ( Zitkovich et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2025b ; Black et al., 2025a ) fine-tuned on task-specific demonstrations often struggle to generalize to new goals and follow novel instructions ( Liu et al., 2025a ; Zhou et al., 2025 ; Fei et al., 2026 ; Fang et al., 2026 ; Kim et al., 2026 ; Hou & Zhao, 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) . Two factors may contribute to the performance of frontier RUAs: the models have been trained for computer use, and they have strong spatial reasoning abilities. We hypothesize that the ability to reason in a chain of thought is particularly important for generalization. However, such performance has so far been obtained only with the strongest proprietary models. Open-weight models have low success rates on the same benchmarks and fail on some simple tasks as well. Several works build a harness that adds skills and components such as the segmentation model SAM 3 ( Carion et al., 2026 ) and turns some motions into a small set of options ( Yang et al., 2026c ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026c ; Chu et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Si et al., 2026 ; RoboDojo Team, 2026 ) . Such a harness allows open-weight models to complete some of the tasks ( Yang et al., 2026c ) , but it may restrict their action space and thereby make other tasks infeasible. As models become more capable, some components of the harness may also be absorbed into the models themselves. Computer-use agents such as OpenAI’s are trained with reinforcement learning (RL) on computer tasks ( OpenAI, 2025a ; OpenAI, 2025b ; Wang et al., 2025 ) , and the close resemblance between the two settings suggests that robot use could be learned in the same way. We therefore train an open-weight reasoning model with robot-use reinforcement learning (RURL), entirely in simulation and without demonstrations. To support this long-horizon, multi-turn RURL at scale, we build PEAR (a Post-training Engine for Agentic Robotics), an infrastructure designed specifically for training RUAs ( Section 3.2 ). In each round, the model observes camera images and the robot’s state, reasons about the task, and outputs the next end-effector pose ( Figure 1 ). Inverse kinematics converts this pose into joint targets, and the robot’s controller executes the movement before the model receives the next observation. Earlier rounds remain in the context, and each episode receives a binary reward indicating whether the task was completed ( Figure 2 ). We provide no perception tools, planners, or skill libraries, so the model itself must localize objects, determine how to manipulate them, and recognize when an action has failed. Because the model specifies poses rather than joint commands and each robot’s own inverse kinematics and controller execute them, the same interface applies in simulation and on a real robot. Although the model is trained entirely in simulation, the skills it learns transfer well to the real world. We train the model first on LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023 ) and then on two tasks in RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . On a real dual-arm robot that it never encountered during training, the model then completes 60% of our trials, whereas the base model completes none ( Section 4.4 ). The checkpoint trained only on the single-arm LIBERO tasks also succeeds in 23% of the trials. These results suggest that simulation-based RL can equip general-purpose reasoning models with robot-use skills that transfer to unseen real-world embodiments. Our main findings are as follows: • Approaching frontier models on LIBERO.
