title: MPIDetector: Detecting Multi-modal Prompt Injection via LLM-based Reasoning
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30202

§Abstract
With the rapid integration of Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) into real-world applications, Prompt Injection (PI) has emerged as a critical security threat. Attackers can conceal malicious instructions within textual narratives or visual inputs to bypass safety alignment. Existing approaches, such as signature- and perplexity-based methods, rely heavily on pattern matching, making them insufficient to detect sophisticated, cross-modal PI payloads. In this paper, we propose a novel detection framework, called MPIDetector, that leverages LLMs' intrinsic reasoning capabilities via few-shot In-Context Learning (ICL). Instead of fine-tuning, we provide the model with specific prompt templates and representative attack examples, enabling it to scrutinize the semantic intent of input across modalities. We constructed a comprehensive evaluation benchmark comprising eight textual and seven visual malicious categories, supplemented with benign datasets to rigorously test for false positives. Our evaluation across multiple leading LLMs, including Gemini-2.0-Flash, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and Doubao-seed-1.6-flash, demonstrates the efficacy and generalizability of this approach. The proposed method achieves up to 90.91% accuracy and 100% recall on textual tasks. In the visual domain, it achieves up to 99.28% accuracy while maintaining 100% precision across all evaluated models. Crucially, when evaluated against 500 highly obfuscated environmental web-agent injections (WebInject), MPIDetector achieved a detection rate up to 99.80%. Comparisons with two baseline approaches for detecting textual PI payloads, along with two additional methods for visual input detection, demonstrate MPIDetector's superior accuracy. These results show that LLM-driven reasoning provides a robust, cross-modal defense against advanced prompt injections.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The rapid proliferation of Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has fundamentally transformed how users interact with artificial intelligence, extending capabilities beyond text to encompass complex visual inputs. However, this expanded attack surface has introduced critical security vulnerabilities, most notably the Prompt Injection (PI) threat. In a PI attack, malicious actors embed hidden instructions within seemingly benign textual narratives or images to hijack the model’s original instructions and bypass safety alignments [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Current state-of-the-art defenses primarily rely on signature-based filtering, perplexity-based anomaly detection, or pattern matching methods [ 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] . While effective against naive textual and image exploits, these conventional practices struggle to generalize against sophisticated, cross-modal injections that obscure malicious intent within complex semantic structures [ 4 , 2 ] . Consequently, exploring how to defend against these evolving, multi-modal threats without constantly retraining defensive classifiers has become one of the critical challenges in AI security. Recent advancements, such as the MalCVE framework [ 8 ] , have demonstrated the remarkable reasoning capabilities of LLMs in analyzing complex malware, prompting the compelling question of whether LLMs can independently reason about and detect adversarial prompt injection. Thus, this study aims to address two research questions. • RQ1: How to leverage LLMs’ intrinsic reasoning capabilities to robustly distinguish between legitimate, albeit ambiguous, user queries and deliberate cross-modal injection attempts? • RQ2: How to design a unified detection framework that effectively generalizes across both textual and visual modalities while maintaining a near-zero false positive rate to preserve the user experience? To answer these research questions, we propose a novel, unified detection framework, MPIDetector , based on few-shot In-Context Learning (ICL). Instead of relying on resource-intensive fine-tuning or superficial feature extraction, our method provides the LLM with carefully curated prompt templates and representative attack exemplars. This empowers the model to act as a security guardian, actively scrutinizing and reasoning about the underlying semantic intent of the input across different modalities. We evaluate our framework across multiple leading LLMs (including Gemini-2.0-Flash, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and Doubao-seed-1.6-flash) on a comprehensive benchmark comprising over 3,800 samples. Specifically, this evaluation utilizes 300 textual payloads, over 2,000 visual adversarial images, 500 highly obfuscated web-agent samples, and 1,000 benign images integrated across modalities to precisely measure the false-positive rate. The results demonstrate the efficacy and generalizability of our approach: it achieves up to 90.91% accuracy and 100% precision in the textual domain, and up to 99.28% accuracy with a 100% precision in the visual domain. Most notably, our framework demonstrates robustness against highly obfuscated web-agent injections (WebInject), achieving a 99.80% detection rate. Comparisons with both the perplexity-based approach [ 5 ] and the fine-tuned safety classifier [ 6 ] in the textual domain demonstrate that the in-context learning (ICL) paradigm of MPIDetector achieves significantly higher recall. In the visual domain, comparisons with rule-based [ 1 ] and visual anomaly–based [ 7 ] approaches further highlight the superior accuracy of MPIDetector . The primary contribution of this work is the empirical validation that LLM-driven reasoning reduces false positives and significantly outperforms traditional baselines, offering a robust, adaptable defense against multi-modal prompt injections. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section II reviews related work in prompt injection and LLM-based security. Section III details our proposed ICL reasoning methodology. Section IV presents the experimental setup and results. Section V discusses the results, and Section VI concludes the paper.

## III Methodology
The core methodology of our proposed framework diverges from traditional static filtering by adopting an active, reasoning-driven approach. We repurposed multiple state-of-the-art Multi-Modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) - specifically, Gemini-2.0-Flash, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and Doubao-seed-1.6-flash - to act as autonomous “AI Security Researchers.” This specific trio was strategically selected to represent distinct paradigms and capabilities within the current MLLM landscape. Gemini-2.0-Flash was chosen to represent top-tier, industry-leading closed-source models characterized by high-speed, advanced multimodal reasoning. Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct serves as a state-of-the-art open-weight representative, critically selected for its exceptional high-resolution image processing and robust Optical Character Recognition (OCR) capabilities, which are vital for scrutinizing typography attacks and visual-semantic perturbations. Finally, Doubao-seed-1.6-flash was included as an emerging, highly optimized proprietary model. Introducing this diverse selection of MLLMs serves a dual purpose: first, it allows us to evaluate the model-agnostic generalizability of our reasoning-driven defense, ensuring its efficacy is not overfitted to a single provider; second, it enables a comprehensive assessment across distinct architectural paradigms and model scales, verifying whether high-efficiency flash models and advanced open-weight reasoning engines can consistently execute complex tasks like visual-semantic decoupling. By feeding these models carefully engineered prompts alongside the target multi-modal inputs, we elicit step-by-step reasoning traces. This not only allows the models to detect adversarial intent but also provides human-readable rationales, thereby ensuring the interpretability of the security analysis. III-A Standard Multi-modal Detection First, we designed modality-specific system prompts that instruct the MLLM to systematically evaluate the input to generate a standardized JSON-like response containing a Verdict, a Summary, and specific Attack Vectors. III-A 1 Textual Payload Analysis For purely textual inputs, the MPIDetector framework instructs the MLLM to review the payload for prompt-injection, goal hijacking, or other adversarial intent, as illustrated in Figure 1 . Within the system prompts, categories such as Prompt Injection, Goal Hijacking, and Data Exfiltration are explicitly provided as examples to guide the model’s structured categorization of attack vectors. The model is constrained to focus strictly on security risks within the text. The output is structured to return a classification verdict (Benign, Suspicious, or Malicious) coupled with a confidence score. Furthermore, it generates a summary of the targeted sensitive resources and categorizes the specific attack vectors detected (e.g., Jailbreak, System Override, or Policy Bypass). III-A 2 Visual Payload Analysis For standard image inputs, the detection protocol requires the MLLM to thoroughly examine the visual payload in a step-by-step manner, as illustrated in Figure 2 . The reasoning process is guided to identify visual injection patterns, such as hidden text, OCR injections, or visual typography attacks. By analyzing the image, the model deduces the underlying intent of the visual input and explains how it attempts to manipulate the AI’s logic, culminating in a structured verdict and rationale. III-B Advanced Defense: Mitigating Web-Agent Injections (EnvInjection) Recent empirical benchmarks have revealed severe vulnerabilities in MLLMs when facing sophisticated, visually integrated attacks. Specifically, the WAInjectBench [ 9 ] demonstrates that current MLLMs consistently fail to detect “WebInject” attacks [ 3 ] , i.e., a class of Environmental Prompt Injections (EnvInjection) where adversaries imperceptibly perturb the visual UIs of webpages to hijack Vision-Language Models. To address this critical blind spot, we engineered a specialized analysis protocol termed Visual-Semantic Decoupling . This protocol forces the MLLM to scrutinize the input through three rigorous stages: 1. Visual Rendering Isolation: In this initial stage, the MLLM is instructed to deliberately suppress its inherent “semantic bias” (the tendency to immediately read and interpret text). Instead, it must focus exclusively on the low-level visual characteristics of the image. The model scans for structural anomalies, optimized high-frequency noise patterns, steganographic artifacts, or irregular typographical formatting commonly associated with visual obfuscation and WebInject attacks. 2. Semantic Intent Extraction: Subsequently, the model shifts to a purely semantic analysis layer. It extracts the legible OCR text, identifies UI components, and parses the underlying contextual instructions. The objective here is to determine the apparent, surface-level intent of the content as if it were a standard, benign web interaction. 3. Cross-Layer Consistency Reconciliation: The critical final stage entails juxtaposing the outputs from the visual and semantic layers. The MLLM evaluates whether the visual presentation logically aligns with the semantic intent. If a profound discrepancy is detected, such as a seemingly benign semantic message (e.g., a standard webpage layout) overlaid with stealthy, adversarial visual noise engineered to override agent instructions, the framework definitively flags the input as an environmental injection. By strictly adhering to this decoupling protocol, the MLLM outputs a highly accurate verdict (Benign, Suspicious, or Malicious) alongside a detailed rationale. The prompt is shown in Figure 3 . III-C Experimental Configuration and Label Mapping To elicit the intrinsic reasoning capabilities of the models, we employed a few-shot In-Context Learning (ICL) setup. Rather than operating in a zero-shot capacity, the MLLMs were provided with a fixed context window containing four distinct demonstrations (two benign user queries and two representative injection attacks) sampled from a separate training holdout. These demonstrations included both the multi-modal input and the expected JSON-like reasoning output to guide the model’s structural response. As illustrated in Figures 1–3, the models were instructed to output a three-way classification verdict: Benign , Suspicious , or Malicious (Adversarial) . To define a deterministic post-processing rule for our binary evaluation metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and False Positive Rate), all Suspicious predictions were strictly mapped to the Malicious class . This conservative mapping prioritizes system security by assuming that any ambiguous or suspicious prompt should trigger safety guardrails rather than be permitted to execute.
