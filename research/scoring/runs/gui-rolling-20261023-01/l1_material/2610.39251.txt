title: Where to Learn and How Far to Roll Out: Efficient Adaptive Training for GUI Agents
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39251

§Abstract
Trajectory-level GRPO is a common approach for training multi-step GUI agents, but repeatedly rolling out complete trajectories from the task root can be inefficient: task-level rewards provide coarse supervision over which intermediate decisions require improvement, while already-mastered prefixes are repeatedly re-executed before reaching difficult states. We introduce a two-stage adaptive training framework that jointly addresses where to learn and how far to roll out. We first construct reusable intermediate checkpoints from successful expert trajectories and select informative pivots using action disagreement, which measures uncertainty directly from sampled GUI actions. Stage I performs one-step RL at these pivots to improve uncertain local decisions. Stage II then progressively expands policy responsibility through adaptive Local, Suffix, and Root training according to task progress. Recoverable checkpoints enable repeated training from intermediate states without replaying the preceding trajectory. Across MobileGym and CUA-Gym-Hub, our method reduces environment interaction by 56-61% relative to Vanilla GRPO while improving task performance, demonstrating that jointly allocating training across states and rollout ranges can substantially improve GUI-agent RL efficiency.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Reinforcement learning has become an effective paradigm for improving agents on multi-step interactive tasks. A common training setup starts the agent from the task root, rolls out a complete trajectory, and uses the final task outcome to construct policy updates over the decisions made along that trajectory. While simple and effective, this trajectory-level training can be inefficient for GUI agents, where every rollout requires repeated interaction with an external environment and successful completion depends on a sequence of interdependent decisions. We identify two main sources of inefficiency. First, task-level outcomes provide only coarse supervision over which intermediate decisions actually require improvement, making credit assignment difficult across a multi-step trajectory. Second, repeatedly starting from the task root re-executes prefixes that the policy may already handle reliably before reaching the states that still need learning. As illustrated in Figure 1 , efficient GUI-agent training therefore requires deciding both where to learn and how far to roll out . Recent work has explored intermediate-state learning through local pivot optimization, uncertainty-guided branching, and rollback-based correction ( Yi et al., 2026 ; He et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026 ; Hao et al., 2026 ) . These approaches show that intermediate states can provide a more targeted unit for agent improvement, but several challenges remain in GUI environments. Weak policies may rarely reach useful downstream states on their own, existing selection signals may overlook states where the policy is uncertain but reward variation has not yet appeared, and recovering an intermediate GUI state through action replay can be both costly and unreliable under dynamic interfaces. More importantly, existing methods typically decide either which intermediate states should receive training or where additional rollouts should branch. We instead study these two choices jointly: where optimization should begin and how much of the remaining trajectory the policy should be responsible for . To address these issues, we introduce a two-stage adaptive training framework for GUI agents. We first construct reusable intermediate checkpoints from successful expert trajectories, which provide reliable downstream states and local next-action references even when the current policy cannot yet reach those states consistently. In Stage I, we identify informative states using action disagreement , which measures uncertainty directly from the GUI actions produced by repeated policy samples, and perform one-step RL at the selected pivots. This concentrates early optimization on uncertain local decisions without requiring successful completion of the remaining task. Stage II then progressively expands these local improvements into multi-step task-solving ability through adaptive hybrid training. Tasks with low success continue to receive local updates, partially mastered tasks are trained from intermediate pivots using suffix rollouts whose starting frontier moves progressively earlier as the policy improves, and reliably solved tasks use only lightweight root-level rollouts. In this way, the framework adapts both the training location and rollout extent to the policy’s evolving capability. Recoverable checkpoints support both stages by allowing repeated training and branching from intermediate GUI states without replaying the full preceding trajectory. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce an action-disagreement criterion that selects informative intermediate states by measuring uncertainty directly from sampled GUI actions. • We propose a progress-aware two-stage training framework that jointly adapts where a rollout starts and how much of the task it covers , progressing from local pivot updates to suffix and root-level training as the policy improves. • We develop a checkpoint-supported training pipeline for efficient intermediate-state learning without replaying full trajectory prefixes, improving both training efficiency and task performance across GUI environments.
