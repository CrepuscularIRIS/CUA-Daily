title: AgentShield: Fortifying Web Applications Against AI Agents and Agentic Browsers
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41181

§Abstract
LLM-powered browser agents are transforming web automation by autonomously interacting with websites and executing complex workflows. However, the effectiveness of existing anti-bot defenses against these systems remains unclear. We present a systematic empirical evaluation of modern agentic browsers against widely deployed anti-bot mechanisms, characterizing their capabilities, limitations, and behavioral differences from humans. Our results show that many defenses designed for traditional automation struggle to reliably detect autonomous AI-driven agents. Accordingly, we introduce AgentShield, a behavioral fingerprinting framework that targets the Perception-Reasoning-Action (PRA) loop underlying agentic user clients. By leveraging dynamic web forms that progressively reveal content and capture fine-grained interaction telemetry, AgentShield exposes behavioral artifacts that enable differentiation between human users and autonomous agents. We further demonstrate Extinction-induced Policy Drift (EPD), a novel attack in which repeated obstruction of task completion causes agents to relax their safeguards and engage in unsafe behaviors. Finally, we present defensive prompt injection techniques that exploit agent-specific weaknesses to mitigate emerging forms of autonomous web abuse that rely on content extraction. Our results highlight both the shortcomings of current web defenses against agentic user clients and the feasibility of practical detection mechanisms tailored to this new generation of web automation.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Fundamentally, cyberattacks rely on automation to achieve scale. Credential stuffing [ 1 , 2 ] , fake-account creation [ 3 , 4 ] , spam [ 5 , 6 , 7 ] , and scraping depend on emulating user activity across sites. Historically, attackers have used scripted bots built on frameworks (e.g., Selenium, Puppeteer), often through headless browsers [ 8 ] . Services have responded with anti-bot defenses [ 9 ] , including CAPTCHAs [ 10 , 11 ] , browser fingerprinting [ 12 , 13 ] , and IP reputation [ 14 ] . These defenses exploit artifacts of traditional automation in browser APIs [ 15 ] , JS execution, timing and resource access [ 16 ] , and network behavior [ 9 ] . The threat landscape is now shifting as LLMs enable increasingly capable autonomous agents. Cloudflare reports 18% year-over-year growth in AI-crawler traffic, with GPTBot’s share rising from 5% to 30%, while only \sim 14% of top sites publish robots.txt rules targeting AI agents [ 17 ] . Recent LLMs demonstrate sophisticated reasoning, planning, and tool use [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] , enabling agentic systems to autonomously browse the web, fill forms, execute transactions, and interact with online services. Products such as Perplexity Comet and OpenAI’s browser agents are bringing these capabilities to consumers, while reports already document their use in phishing and large-scale scams [ 22 , 23 , 24 ] . Recent studies have also shown that agentic browsers can be manipulated by untrusted web content, including hidden instructions that drive account takeover, fraudulent purchases, and credential submission [ 25 , 26 ] . Thus, agentic browsers and AI-powered web automation tools pose a major new challenge to securing the web ecosystem, as existing anti-bot assumptions based on static browser fingerprints or deterministic automation patterns may no longer hold [ 27 , 28 ] . As agentic browsing becomes more widely deployed, it will become increasingly important for web services to reliably identify, regulate, or selectively restrict such clients in order to preserve platform integrity, defend monetization models, and prevent widespread abuse. Accordingly, the research community has begun to investigate the security implications of agentic web systems. Recent studies demonstrate indirect prompt injection attacks against browser agents [ 29 , 30 , 25 ] , social engineering attacks targeting autonomous workflows [ 31 ] , and fingerprinting techniques that distinguish AI-driven browsers from human users [ 32 ] . However, despite increasing concern regarding the abuse potential of agentic clients, we currently lack a systematic evaluation of emerging agentic systems against existing anti-bot defenses. In this paper, we fill that gap by presenting a systematic empirical evaluation of agentic user clients against widely deployed anti-bot defenses. We study the capabilities of agentic browsers and automation frameworks when interacting with widely-available protection systems, including Google’s reCAPTCHA, and Cloudflare’s Turnstile. Through extensive experiments across multiple browsing tasks, we characterize the extent to which current agentic systems bypass, evade, or falter against existing bot mitigation techniques. Our findings provide one of the first large-scale empirical perspectives on how agentic clients meaningfully alter the current balance between web task automation and anti-bot defenses. Building on these observations, we present AgentShield , a novel behavioral fingerprinting framework that detects agentic clients by exploiting inherent limitations and behavioral artifacts in how autonomous agents navigate and interact with websites. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate how AgentShield detects modern agentic clients using specially crafted dynamic forms that capture granular information during form interactions and target the Perception \rightarrow Reasoning \rightarrow Action (PRA) loop [ 33 ] that drives agentic clients. Modern web apps can readily deploy our approach to fortify critical processes (e.g., account creation). Moreover, while prior work has demonstrated prompt injection techniques that can reveal the use of agentic clients (or exploit them), modern agents attempt to counteract such techniques through explicit safeguards [ 34 , 35 , 36 ] . To that end, we demonstrate an attack we call Extinction-induced Policy Drift (EPD), wherein interaction with state-changing web elements (e.g., a form submission button) does not result in any changes, leading to a failure mode where repeated obstruction of task completion causes an agentic browser to relax constraints and pursue unsafe actions in search of reward completion (i.e., the agent overrides its internal safeguards). ## III Methodology
This section details our analysis pipeline for agentic user clients, which combines prevalent existing tools and services with a series of novel mechanisms we have developed. III-A Current Defenses We test three classes of existing defenses against our AI client set: network signals , environment-validation challenges , and self-declared identity . Table II maps each defense to its class. Most fall in one class, though Cloudflare’s Crawl Control and AI Bot Blocking spans both network and self-declared identity signals. The server records the source IP and classifies it as data-center or residential through an IP-reputation provider. This signal targets agents that use cloud infrastructure. Agents running on the user’s local machine or behind residential proxies are not detected at this layer. TLS Fingerprint (JA4). A Zeek SSL logger [ 50 ] deployed on the server records the JA4 fingerprint of every TLS handshake. Real browsers produce a standard Chrome fingerprint, whereas raw HTTP libraries and atypical TLS stacks produce non-standard fingerprints that we flag. Dual-Request. Some local-browser agents issue a separate server-side request from their cloud backend alongside the local browser session. We flag any pair of requests to the same page from two different IPs within a short time window. A normal human visit does not produce this pattern. Cloudflare Crawl Control and AI Bot Blocking. We route the test site through Cloudflare with AI Crawl Control [ 46 ] and AI Bot Blocking [ 47 ] enabled in managed mode. Cloudflare uses IP reputation, declared User-Agent, and its own signed-agent registry to decide whether to block. reCAPTCHA v3. We deploy Google’s reCAPTCHA v3 [ 51 ] on a test page. The challenge runs silently in the background, scoring the client on behavioral signals and cross-site browsing history, and returns a bot-likelihood score between 0 and 1. We treat a score below the default threshold of 0.5 as a block verdict. Cloudflare Turnstile. We deploy Turnstile on a similar test page. Turnstile issues a combination of proof-of-work, proof-of-space, and browser-API challenges that target headless browsers and non-standard environments. Each agent receives either a pass or a failure verdict on each session. User-Agent. The server logs the UA string on every request. An agent that declares itself through a UA suffix (e.g., Perplexity-User/1.0 ) self-identifies at this layer. Request Signing. The server inspects each request for the Signature-Agent , Signature-Input , and Signature headers defined by RFC 9421 [ 49 ] and Cloudflare’s Web Bot Auth draft [ 48 ] . A signed request cryptographically attests to the agent’s identity. III-B Dynamic Forms Forms constitute a foundational component of consequential web interactions (e.g., account creation, sign-in, checkout, job applications, and onboarding workflows) and represent a persistent friction point for users. This friction is precisely the capability that agentic browsers advertise as a primary value proposition. Perplexity’s Comet markets form-filling as a core feature of its sidebar AI assistant [ 52 ] ; Manus positions its Browser Operator around eliminating manual clicking and form completion [ 53 ] ; OpenAI similarly frames ChatGPT Atlas’s agent mode as automating multi-step browser workflows, exemplified by converting a recipe into a grocery order through tab opening, cart population, and order placement [ 54 ] . Given that forms gate substantial portions of the authenticated web, and browser vendors explicitly promote form automation as a distinguishing capability, we employ dynamic forms as our initial investigation into agentic behavior. Dynamic forms are web forms in which fields are concealed from the user and revealed sequentially upon completion of the preceding field. We implement concealment via CSS properties ( opacity: 0 and zero dimensions), so that fields remain present in the DOM but invisible until triggered. This design necessitates that any agentic client observe the page after each field reveal before proceeding, thereby making the latency of the agent’s Perception \rightarrow Reasoning \rightarrow Act loop directly measurable and comparable across humans, browser autofill, and AI agents. Form Design. Our form is a signup page containing 22 conditionally-revealed fields that span the input types commonly found in real-world forms: text and telephone inputs, email, date pickers, single-select dropdowns, radio buttons, checkboxes, and a free-form textarea. The reveal sequence is branching rather than linear: selecting a country exposes a country-specific sub-field (e.g., US \rightarrow state \rightarrow city, Canada \rightarrow province); choosing a volunteer role reveals a role-specific panel (field research, public education, fundraising, or remote support); and certain selections (full-time availability, social-media or friend-referral as the source) trigger additional follow-up fields. These branching structures enable a single session to exercise multiple input types and yield sufficient events for stable statistics across each metric. A JavaScript handler attached to each preceding field reveals the subsequent field upon event completion. Text fields trigger on the input event once a minimum character threshold is satisfied; selects, radios, and checkboxes trigger on the change event. Upon reveal, the handler appends a visible class to the target field’s container, which the MutationObserver detects as the reveal signal. This mechanism ensures that field visibility transitions are observable events, enabling precise timestamping of each reveal. Behavioral Metrics. The form’s design is motivated by four behavioral metrics that collectively distinguish agentic from human interaction patterns. (i) Dwell Time (duration between keydown and keyup for each keystroke, reported as per-session mean and standard deviation) is measurable because the form’s sequential reveal isolates individual keystrokes within discrete interaction episodes. Auto-repeat presses from held keys are excluded to prevent count inflation. (ii) Field Reaction Time (interval between a field’s reveal and the client’s first interaction with it) is directly enabled by the CSS concealment mechanism: the MutationObserver provides reveal timestamps (triggering on the concealed-to-visible transition), while the field’s input and change events provide interaction timestamps. This interval captures the end-to-end Perception \rightarrow Reasoning \rightarrow Act loop latency, which for agents is dominated by LLM inference time rather than typing or navigation speed. (iii) Keypress Count (total number of keydown events per session) reveals clients that inject or paste values rather than typing them, as these produce near-zero counts. (iv) Mouse Movement Count (total number of mousemove samples per session at a 40ms sampling rate) is meaningful because the form’s multi-field layout requires pointer navigation across spatially distinct targets. Because browser-automation frameworks typically emit pointer events only at click targets rather than along a continuous trajectory, agents produce near-zero counts, whereas humans generate continuous traces across the form. Human typing exhibits high variance across the timing metrics due to finger transitions, corrections, and cognitive pauses; agents produce mechanically consistent values with near-zero spread. Client-side JavaScript injected into the form page captures browser events at fine granularity. The DOM Event API [ 55 ] records mouse activity, keystrokes, and form-level input and change events; the MutationObserver Web API [ 56 ] records field-reveal timestamps by triggering on every DOM mutation that transitions a concealed field into a visible state. Mouse clicks ( mousedown ) are recorded individually, and keydown / keyup timestamps are captured per keystroke. Every event is timestamped, enabling full session-timeline reconstruction. Deployment. The dynamic form has two variants: 1. Standard variant : includes dropdown fields. However, we found that certain agents cannot navigate dropdown menus reliably. 2. Simplified variant : excludes dropdown fields to establish a common baseline across all clients. III-C Content Modification While our dynamic forms detect agentic behavior through interaction patterns, certain agentic workflows may not include such interactions (e.g., an agentic scraper). As such, our goal of providing a holistic defense against modern agentic clients necessitates a complementary technique: content-based poisoning of pages consumed by agents. Unlike traditional scrapers, agentic clients interpret page content through an LLM, making them susceptible to semantic manipulation. Even when perimeter defenses are bypassed, poisoned content can corrupt agent outputs by inducing compliance with hidden instructions or relaying fabricated information. This vulnerability is intrinsic to the PRA loop, whose reasoning module processes DOM text regardless of whether it is intended for human consumption. Testbed Configuration. We deploy a WordPress-based test site combining an e-commerce store and news blog, with injections placed in (i) news and blog articles covering food, health, and environmental topics, and (ii) product listings, including descriptions, prices, and attributes. Hidden Prompt Taxonomy. We embed prompts in the DOM that are invisible to humans but accessible to agents. We evaluate five manipulation strategies, using one fixed prompt per category across all clients and runs: Defensive Prompt claims that content is proprietary and extraction violates site policy. Success occurs when the agent refuses, halts extraction, or treats the notice as a reason for caution. Hidden Promotion instructs the agent to include a fabricated marketing offer absent from the visible page; success occurs when the offer appears in its response. Opposite Information instructs the agent to replace a visible claim with a contradictory statement; success occurs when the contradiction is presented as factual. Additional Information asks the agent to append an unsupported but plausible detail; success occurs when the injected detail appears in its output. Abusive Prompt injects a threatening or alarming warning intended to derail the response; success occurs when the agent reproduces or treats the warning as a genuine page-level safety concern. Of these five categories, only Defensive Prompt has a direct analogue in prior work.
