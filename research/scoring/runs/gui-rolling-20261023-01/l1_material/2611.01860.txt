title: Advances in Agentic Computer Graphics
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01860

§Abstract
Computer graphics is built on code. Large language model (LLM) agents can now generate graphics code and iteratively refine it through various feedback. This capability opens the door to a potentially new paradigm of computer graphics, with graphics algorithms increasingly built around agentic systems to automate progressively complex tasks. Over the past years, research on agentic graphics has expanded rapidly across a diverse range of tasks. This rapid growth makes it increasingly difficult for manual literature reviews to keep pace with new developments. At the same time, similar ideas are often described using different terminology across these tasks, further complicating efforts to develop a systematic understanding of the field. In this report, we address these challenges through two contributions. First, we introduce a unified taxonomy for characterizing agentic systems across diverse computer graphics tasks. Second, we develop an agentic literature system that can assist human researchers in analyzing existing and newly published papers at scale. Building on these contributions, we analyze emerging research trends and discuss open challenges in coordinating agents and tools across graphics tasks, designing interfaces and representations for human-agent collaboration, sustaining long-horizon planning and refinement, and reliably evaluating interactive graphics systems.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
In computer graphics, code defines executable procedures for creating and manipulating visual content [ 22 , 91 ] . Recent advances in large language models (LLMs), particularly their capabilities in code generation, reasoning, and tool use [ 19 , 160 ] , open up new opportunities for automating complex graphics workflows. By interacting with graphics environments, LLM agents can execute operations, inspect the results, and use feedback to guide subsequent actions [ 45 , 43 , 84 ] . We refer to systems that leverage these capabilities to perform computer graphics tasks as agentic graphics systems . Agentic graphics systems have demonstrated strong capabilities across diverse graphics tasks, including 3D asset creation and iterative editing [ 84 ] , articulated object animation [ 189 ] , and dynamic scene construction with rigid-body and fluid simulations [ 78 ] . For example, instead of manually specifying and adjusting shader programs [ 22 ] , users can guide an agent’s material edits with a short language instruction and an optional reference image [ 45 ] . The rapid growth of agentic graphics research (Fig. 1 a), and the strong capabilities demonstrated by current agentic graphics systems motivate our survey. The demonstrated capabilities of these systems have attracted growing research interest across graphics applications. This expanding body of work poses challenges of both scale and terminology. First, the literature is large and growing at a seemingly exponential rate: our collected corpus includes more than 300 papers published through September 2026 (Fig. 1 a). This scale and pace of growth make it increasingly difficult to organize existing studies and synthesize the field’s methods and findings. Second, studies across applications such as CAD, animation, and simulation describe functionally similar components using different terminology. Fig. 1 b illustrates this variation in the names of feedback (top; Sec. 3.3.2 ) and planning (bottom; Sec. 3.3.1 ) modules. These differences can obscure shared design choices and complicate comparisons across works. These challenges make it difficult to assess the field’s overall progress and identify broader research trends across graphics applications. To support a coherent understanding of this rapidly growing field, we address these challenges through a unified taxonomy and an agentic literature survey system built on it. First, we introduce a taxonomy that characterizes agentic graphics systems along four dimensions: specification , base model , harness , and graphics interface and tooling (Fig. 2 ). Within each dimension, we define the key design choices and provide a common vocabulary for describing system components and their roles. This allows similarities and differences in system design to be examined across applications, even when individual works use different terminology. Second, we develop an agentic literature survey system that searches for relevant papers, reads their content, and annotates their designs using this taxonomy (Sec. 4 ). The system records taxonomy labels, design insights, and limitations alongside supporting source evidence, producing structured reports that support comparison across papers and analysis of broader research trends. By retaining these records across runs, it allows new papers to be incorporated and existing annotations to be revisited as further evidence becomes available. The taxonomy and the resulting literature analysis enable us to synthesize shared design patterns across applications (Secs. 6 , 7 , and 8 ), trace emerging research trends, and identify open challenges in agentic graphics (Sec. 9 ). We conclude by summarizing the survey’s main findings (Sec. 10 ).
