arxiv: 2610.18579 (2610.18579v1)
title: Reading Right, Acting Wrong: Diagnosing Decision Failures of System One Models in Agent Loops
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18579

§Abstract
System One models choose an agent's next action by scoring a supplied set of options in a single pass. This makes them fast and cheap, but when they choose wrongly there is nothing to inspect: did the model misread what the agent has seen, or read it correctly and still act wrongly? We answer behaviorally. On a frozen benchmark of agent decision states from simulated environments, customer-service policies, tool use, web navigation and coding, we pair every next-action question with factual probes about the same state, each answered independently. Across seventeen fast systems and a reasoning model, the answer is consistent: most wrong actions follow correct reading, and the more accurate a system, the more of its errors take this form. These errors are not noise; they gather on the same states across systems of every kind. Where the probed facts provably determine the action, models that read every fact still fail to combine them, and their own answers, composed by code, repair the failure. On real states, what is missing is often not a fact but the next sub-goal.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
An agent acting in an environment repeatedly reads the feedback of its previous actions, works out where it stands, and chooses what to do next. In most current agents each such step is a call to a large language model that writes out its reasoning and then names an action ( Yao et al., 2023 ) . A growing family of System One models takes a different route ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ; Liquid AI, 2026 ; Kwok et al., 2026 ; Palmer, 2026 ; Convai Innovations, 2026 ; Cloudflare, 2026 ) . Given the agent’s state and a set of candidate answers, they return a probability distribution over the supplied options and generate no text; their output is consumed directly by code, and their developers emphasize intelligence per dollar ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) . The System One models we evaluate answer a complete state in a median of 27–225 ms, against 317–947 ms for likelihood readouts of open-weight language models and 2.3 s for a reasoning model (§ 4 ). We study only their choice interface , in which the model selects among supplied options. When such a model picks the wrong action, there is no chain of thought to read. The error has two very different explanations. The model may have misread what the agent already knows, for example by missing that the last action failed or losing track of what it holds; or it may have read all of it correctly and still chosen badly . The first points to the state representation or the context budget, the second to the decision itself. Final-action accuracy cannot tell these cases apart, and generated rationales need not reflect the computation behind an answer ( Turpin et al., 2023 ; Lanham et al., 2023 ) . Conversely, a correct action does not show that the model understood the trace. We therefore ask, behaviorally: can System One models understand the information an agent has obtained and act correctly on it, and when they fail, can we tell failures of comprehension from failures of decision? On a single agent decision state we ask, each in isolation, the next-action question and factual probes whose answers the environment determines, such as whether the last action succeeded, what the agent carries, or whether an action is permitted. Crossing “all measured facts correct” with “action correct” gives four observable outcomes (Figure 1 ). Around this measurement we build a frozen benchmark of 65,228 records, 19,663 of them paired test states, from simulators with exact ground truth (ALFWorld, BabyAI-Text, TextWorld), customer-service policies ( \tau^{2} -bench), tool use, web navigation and coding traces, with every admissible action in context and gold labels computed only from what the agent has seen. We evaluate seventeen fast systems, fourteen System One models of three kinds (hosted APIs, open-weight models, and recipes that read a frozen language model) and three likelihood readouts of open-weight language models, with a reasoning model, GPT-6.1, as a reference. The short answer is that these models mostly read right and act wrong. Our main findings are: • Most wrong actions follow correct reading. For the six most accurate systems, 73–85% of decision errors occur on states where every measured fact is answered correctly, and the share grows with accuracy (§ 5.1 ). • The errors are structured. All 14 competent fast systems fail with every fact right on the same 141 states, where chance predicts none (§ 5.3 ). • When the probes suffice, the failure is composition. On generated states whose probes determine the action, models that read every fact and decide perfectly from the facts alone lose 20–45 points when they must do both from the trace. Their own fact answers recover 15–28 points, and composing the action in code from them reaches 0.96–1.00 (§ 6.1 ). • On real states, the decision itself is often missing. Correct answers to the benchmark’s probes add at most 7 points for all but two systems. On ALFWorld, stating the next sub-goal adds 16 points for JEV, any block of facts at most 5 (§ 6.2 , § 6.3 ). • Accuracy hides the rest. Removing the history collapses accuracy where earlier observations matter, policy edits are read correctly but acted on in only 22–72% of cases, and calibration error ranges from 2.6% to 26.6% (§ 7 ). We also contribute a model-free criterion for when probes can support attributing an error to the decision (§ 2.4 ), states that satisfy it by construction, and an evaluation of eighteen systems that combines the paired diagnostics with cost, latency and calibration. The paired outcomes are behavioral evidence: they do not identify a model’s internal computation, and we do not claim that the probes exhaust every relevant fact.
