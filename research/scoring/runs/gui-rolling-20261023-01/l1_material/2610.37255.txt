title: Your Policy Is Secretly a Harness: The Decision Geometry of Agent Interfaces
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37255

§Abstract
Language model agents are post-trained and deployed through a harness, the prompts, tools, and control logic that present a task to the model and execute its outputs. Harnesses are often revised and judged by how an already trained model performs under them, which assumes that what the model learned under one harness carries over to the next. We ask what a policy learns from its harness and what survives when the harness changes. Under KL-regularized control, we show that the log odds of an optimal policy recover how strongly its harness favors each action over the others, so a trained policy carries the preferences of its harness. Deployed return then equals the ceiling of the harness, the best return it offers, minus the mismatch of the policy, the return lost to its own suboptimal decisions. Training changes only the mismatch, while changing the harness moves both terms. Comparing harnesses with a fixed model therefore mixes what each harness offers with how well that model uses it. In MiniWoB++, post-training makes a model's preferences specific to its training harness. Reversing the order of the observation fields leaves every optimal decision unchanged yet lowers a trained model's success from 85.0% to 19.2%. Conversely, every new harness that a fixed-model comparison rejects but that raises the ceiling outperforms the original agent after further training under it, and ceiling and mismatch measured beforehand predict these gains. For coding-agent harnesses on Terminal-Bench Lite, ceiling minus mismatch estimated with a stronger model as a stand-in for the optimum reproduces the accuracy ranking, while either term alone does not. Code is available at https://github.com/2020-qqtcg/HRP.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Language model agents are post-trained and deployed through a harness, the prompts, tools, and control logic that present a task to the model and execute its outputs. Interface design and prompt formatting change agent performance ( Yang et al., 2024 ; Sclar et al., 2024 ) . Automated harness design revises prompts, tools, and workflows and scores each candidate by how a fixed model performs under it ( Hu et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ; Fernando et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Such a score assumes that what the model learned under earlier harnesses carries over to each candidate, yet the same interface change can raise one model’s accuracy and lower that of a fine-tuned model ( Mu et al., 2025 ) . At the same time, training can move behavior that a prompt or a harness supplies into the weights ( Snell et al., 2022 ; Ye et al., 2026 ) , and recent methods adapt harnesses and weights together ( Hebbar et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) . These practices raise the question we study: What does a policy learn from its harness, and what survives when the harness changes? Two experiments make the question concrete. In the first, we take a web agent post-trained under one harness and reverse the order of the fields in its observation, a JSON object with fields such as the task instruction, the page state, and the available actions. The fields keep their contents. We also hold fixed the actions, their execution, the rewards, and the reference policy that regularizes training, so every optimal decision stays the same. The model still changes its most probable action at about half of the states, and its task success falls from 85.0% to 19.2%. Keeping the decisions a model should make does not keep the decisions it makes. In the second, a new harness adds batched clicks, which execute two clicks in one decision. The new actions raise the best achievable return, yet the trained model’s success drops when we move it to the new harness without changing its weights. A frozen comparison, which evaluates candidate harnesses with the same trained weights, would therefore reject the new harness. We then train the model further for the same budget under each harness, starting from the same trained weights, which we call the source checkpoint. We call further training under the new harness adaptation, and further training under the original harness continuation. The adapted model succeeds more often than both the source checkpoint and the continued one. The harness that first hurt the model gives the best agent. We explain both results in the control problem that a harness induces over semantic actions, the operations an agent executes together with their arguments. Under KL-regularized control, the optimal policy is a softmax over action scores. Each score adds an action’s long-term value to its log probability under the reference policy, so an optimal policy’s log odds between two actions are proportional to the difference of their scores. A policy trained to optimality under a harness therefore carries the relative action scores that the harness induces, even though it contains none of the harness’s code, tools, or execution machinery. In this sense, a policy is secretly a harness. In our controlled study, real post-training moves a model’s action scores toward the optimum of its training harness, and each harness leaves its own preferences in the model. Action scores also show why a trained policy can fail after it is moved to a new harness. The scores a policy carries are the ones it expresses through its training interface, which we call its readout through that interface, and the same weights can express different scores through another interface. A policy is thus secretly its training harness, as read through that harness. A new harness can therefore change which actions are optimal, by moving the optimal scores, and separately change which actions the model picks, by moving the readout. Reversing the observation fields leaves the optimal scores in place and moves only the readout. Scores fix decisions but not how much return a harness offers, since raising every reward by the same constant changes no decision. To convert score changes into return, we therefore add this value back by applying the regularized performance-gap identity ( Geist et al., 2019 ; O’Donoghue, 2022 ) to the harness-induced problem. Deployed return equals the harness’s ceiling C , the best return any policy can reach under it, minus the policy’s mismatch M , the return the policy loses by departing from the optimal policy. The mismatch is the KL divergence from the optimal policy, accumulated along the policy’s own trajectories. The ceiling does not depend on the model’s weights, so training under a fixed harness changes only M , while a harness change can move both terms.
