arxiv: 2610.21046 (2610.21046v1)
title: PremiseCheck: Efficient Fact-Checking of Human and AI Peer Reviews
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21046

§Abstract
Peer review is under strain from the growth of submissions, and review comments written by humans or AI can misstate what a paper says. We introduce ReviewFact, a benchmark that labels such factual errors in review comments under one definition across NLP, machine learning, and the natural sciences, and that measures cost and latency alongside accuracy. A frontier LLM checks comments well but must read the whole paper for each check, while cheap decision models, which score whether a statement holds in a text, fall far short on whole comments that mix several implicit assertions. PremiseCheck is a framework that combines the two. An LLM rewrites each comment as explicit premises without reading the paper, and a decision model checks each premise against the paper. When each comment is checked on its own, PremiseCheck reaches accuracy comparable to GPT-5.5 across all three fields at about half its cost, and on NLP reviews at a quarter of its cost and less than half its latency.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
The number of research papers has grown explosively in recent years, and reviewing them now places a heavy burden on the scientific community ( Shah, 2022 ; Kuznetsov et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2025 ) . A review comment is useful only if what it says about the paper is true, yet factual errors appear regularly in both human-written and AI-written comments ( Du et al., 2024 ; Ryu et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) , and AI systems now help write a growing share of reviews ( Liang et al., 2024a ; Thakkar et al., 2025 ; Biswas et al., 2026 ) . Such errors mislead authors and distort decisions, and at the present scale they can no longer be caught by hand. An area chair cannot reread each paper to check every comment, and an author can correct an error only in the rebuttal. We therefore study the fact-checking of review comments as a task of its own. Given a paper and one comment from a review of it, a system scores how likely the comment says something that the paper does not support. Errors of judgment, such as whether a concern matters, are out of scope. Existing expert annotations of review comments label factual errors inconsistently, if at all. Our benchmark, ReviewFact, builds on them ( Du et al., 2024 ; Ryu et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) and labels every comment under one definition of factual error. Because a system would run on every comment of every review, ReviewFact evaluates cost and latency together with accuracy. The task is hard because each error is something an expert reviewer overlooked or misread, and the evidence against it may lie anywhere in a long paper. A comment also rarely states its claim directly, as a question such as “Did the authors test other models?” presupposes that the paper does not test them. When each comment is checked on its own, existing systems are either accurate or cheap, but not both. A frontier LLM that judges each comment on its own ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Ryu et al., 2025 ; Thakkar et al., 2025 ) must reread the full paper for every comment, a cost that multiplies across tens of thousands of reviews. Instead of generating text, a decision model such as Jev returns the probability that a statement holds in a document, typically within a fraction of a second and at negligible cost ( Almeida, 2026 ; TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) . Yet on whole comments, Jev performs at chance level on the NLP reviews and well below an LLM on the other two parts. A review comment mixes several assertions, many implicit, whereas a decision model, like single-claim fact-checkers ( Laban et al., 2022 ; Zha et al., 2023 ; Tang et al., 2024 ) , is reliable only on explicit, self-contained claims. On single premises that human annotators wrote out from review comments, however, Jev is as accurate as GPT-5.5 (Appendix D.1 ). Our key insight is that understanding what a comment asserts needs an LLM but not the paper, whereas reading the paper is the expensive part and a decision model can do it once the assertions are explicit. PremiseCheck is a framework that combines the two kinds of models. An LLM rewrites each comment as a few explicit premises without reading the paper ( Min et al., 2023 ; Wei et al., 2024 ; Ryu et al., 2025 ) . A decision model then decides how to check each premise and verifies it against the paper, and the comment receives the score of its weakest premise. Figure 1 contrasts PremiseCheck with either kind of model alone. With a medium-reasoning decomposer, PremiseCheck stays within 0.3 AUROC points of holistic GPT-5.5 or above it on every part of ReviewFact at about half its cost, and on the NLP reviews a decomposer without reasoning cuts the cost to a quarter while remaining non-inferior. The accuracy comes from decomposition rather than from one model, since four decision models from different providers, including the open-weight Kev-27B, come within one point of holistic GPT-5.5 as verifiers. Our contributions are as follows. • We propose PremiseCheck, a framework that turns the verification of a review comment into decision problems, combining the flexibility of an LLM with the efficiency of a decision model. A decision model checks each premise against the paper or against general knowledge and asks several phrasings of its question in one call. • We define the task of fact-checking single review comments and build ReviewFact, a benchmark that labels factual errors in human-written and AI-written review comments across NLP, machine learning, and the natural sciences and measures cost and latency alongside accuracy. ReviewFact holds 9,267 comments on 217 papers. • We validate PremiseCheck on all three parts of ReviewFact, where it checks each comment on its own with accuracy comparable to GPT-5.5 at a fraction of its cost. A cascade costs more than PremiseCheck yet is less accurate on every part.
