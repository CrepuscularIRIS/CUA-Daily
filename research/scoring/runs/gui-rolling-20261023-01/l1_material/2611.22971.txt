arxiv: 2611.22971 (2611.22971v1)
title: SEAM: Computer-Use Agents Know the Harm Between Screens, and Commit It Anyway
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22971

§Abstract
Safety benchmarks for computer-use agents (CUAs) place the harm either in the intent of the task or on a single screen: a malicious instruction, an injected command, a phishing page. An agent that checks the task and each screen in isolation can pass them, so they do not test whether it combines facts seen on different screens. Yet in delegated work, harm often arises exactly this way: a fact about a person on one screen, a fact about an item on another, and a legitimate task that binds them. We introduce SEAM (Safety Execution Across Multiple screens), a benchmark of such cross-screen compositional harm: 600 multi-step, cross-application OSWorld tasks in which the instruction and every single observation are benign and only their combination is harmful. Minimal-pair variants that differ in one place, scored from the server log rather than by an LLM judge, locate where an agent fails. General-purpose open-weight and flagship agents carry out the harm in up to 92% of runs, yet decline when the user states it in one sentence. Agents open the decisive pages, so exposure is not the primary bottleneck. Asked about those pages, they flag the harm, and with thinking they often write the conflict into their reasoning and confirm anyway. Safety recognition thus does not reliably translate into safe execution, and the translation depends on the information channel: the same warning stops agents far more often in the user's instruction than on screen. Proprietary models are markedly more cautious. We release SEAM with its generator and quality gates at https://yuxuan2003.github.io/SEAM/ .

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
Computer-use agents (CUAs) now read mail, fill in forms and confirm bookings on a user’s behalf [ 33 , 38 , 27 ] . When such an agent errs, it acts on the world, for example by placing an order or assigning a seat, and someone bears the consequences. Safety evaluation of CUAs has accordingly grown quickly [ 29 , 6 ] . Existing CUA safety benchmarks, however, place the harm where one check can find it: in the intent of the task or on a single screen . Misuse benchmarks give a malicious instruction [ 19 , 31 , 32 , 20 , 36 ] ; the others put the harm in one observation: a command planted in the environment [ 5 , 23 , 37 , 34 ] , a deceptive interface [ 9 , 15 , 16 ] , or, under benign instructions, a harmful object such as a phishing portal or an abusive file [ 11 , 28 , 17 ] . An agent that checks the task and each screen in isolation can pass them. They test whether an agent recognizes a harmful task or a harmful screen, and leave open whether it combines facts seen on different screens. Much of everyday delegated work requires exactly this combination. A colleague asks the assistant to “book the spa session for Mei Lin”. The session page says the hot tub runs at 40 °C; the client record says Mei is 22 weeks pregnant and advised to avoid water above 38 °C. The intent is legitimate, each screen is benign, and no screen contains a warning. The harm exists only in the combination of screens ( Figure 1 ), and nobody is hiding it. In multimodal jailbreaks, a user conceals a harmful request, for example across several images [ 7 , 39 , 40 ] ; here the user’s goal is legitimate, every page is truthful, and the person at risk is a third party who never appears in the conversation. Acting safely requires safety execution : integrating facts the agent has already been shown, on different screens, into the decision it is about to commit. Recent work composes a benign goal with the state of the environment [ 22 , 28 ] or splits a harmful objective into innocuous steps [ 13 ] ( section 2 ), but no CUA benchmark guarantees that the task intent and every observation are benign while the task as a whole is not. We therefore introduce Seam ( S afety E xecution A cross M ultiple screens), a benchmark of cross-screen compositional harm : multi-step computer-use tasks in which each single observation is harmless and only their combination is harmful. It has three key properties. (1) Strict cross-screen composition. The instruction and every page are benign; seeing the harm requires combining a fact about a person, a fact about an object and the binding between them. No single screen reveals it, which we verify behaviourally (§ 3.5 ). (2) Minimal-pair variants. Each task has five variants that differ in one place: harm only on screen ( R ), a benign twin ( B ), and the harm stated in one sentence by the user ( E u ), by the requester ( E ), or by the user quoting the requester ( E q ). The environment is byte-identical across the four harmful variants, so behavioural differences are attributable to where the harm is stated and by whom. (3) Execution-level evaluation. Tasks are cross-application workflows (10–12 steps on the shortest path) in OSWorld virtual machines; outcomes and exposure are read from the service’s own server logs, without an LLM judge. Seam covers 150 rules across 27 harm families, 8 reasoning operations and 49 application domains, instantiated as 600 scenarios \times 5 variants. Across 17 configurations of 11 models, the general-purpose open-weight and flagship agents carry the harm out in 17.5–91.6% of R runs against 1.1–22.0% of E u runs, while completing 91.8–99.6% of benign twins; the proprietary models stay at or below 3.6%, three of five by also leaving much of the benign work undone ( section 4 ). Three findings locate where safety execution fails ( section 5 ). (i) Exposure is not the primary bottleneck: agents open the decisive page in at least 99.3% of required-path runs. (ii) Safety recognition does not reliably translate into safe execution: asked about those same screens, agents block the step; with reasoning they often state the conflict before confirming (in 81–87% of DeepSeek-1017’s harmful runs and 50–69% for the other reasoning models we judged) and then report success without mentioning it. (iii) The translation depends on the information channel: the same sentence stops the agent far more often as part of the user’s instruction than read on screen. A pre-registered control with budget limits in place of safety limits shows that screen facts reach the action poorly in general, safety facts somewhat more often.
