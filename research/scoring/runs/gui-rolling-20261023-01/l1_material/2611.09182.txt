§Abstract
Recent experimental demonstrations of Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) have attracted significant attention for their ability to establish information-theoretically secure encryption keys for next-generation communication systems. As practical deployment accelerates, the realization of compact, scalable, and high-throughput discrete-variable (DV) QKD systems has become increasingly important. While advances in photonic integration are enabling low-size, low-weight, and low-power QKD hardware, the security and performance of these systems rely heavily on efficient post-processing techniques to generate identical, secure keys. This tutorial presents a comprehensive algorithm-level review of practical post-processing techniques for DV-QKD, with particular emphasis on information reconciliation methods based on Cascade, Winnow, low-density parity-check (LDPC), and polar codes. We also analyze the theoretical asymptotic and finite-size secret key rates (SKRs) of DV-QKD and benchmark these bounds against the practical SKRs achieved using actual post-processing algorithms. Furthermore, we present a server-client model to implement the entire QKD post-processing pipeline. The performance is evaluated in terms of QBER and SKR across various system parameters, considering the complete post-processing pipeline, including sifting, information reconciliation, privacy amplification, and authentication. This tutorial also presents a graphical user interface (GUI) for the QKD post-processing methods, enabling researchers, industry practitioners, and academic users to visualize and analyze the impact of different post-processing techniques and system parameters, such as QBER, on the SKR performance of DV-QKD systems.

Secure communication forms the foundation of today’s digital ecosystem. It enables the secure exchange of information across a wide range of applications, including financial services, private communications, cloud computing, and data transmission. Modern communication systems primarily rely on asymmetric cryptographic algorithms to ensure secure communication [ 1 ] . One of the most widely deployed and established schemes is the RSA algorithm [ 2 ] . In these algorithms, security relies on assumptions related to computational complexity. In other words, solving mathematical problems like integer factorization or the discrete logarithm problem is considered computationally infeasible [ 3 ] . As a result, cryptographic systems are fundamentally secure because they assume that adversaries possess computational limitations. Advances in algorithmic techniques or the emergence of large-scale quantum computing could render hard problems easier to solve [ 4 ] . Consequently, there is an urgent need for alternative approaches to enhance security that extend beyond classical computational methods. Unlike conventional cryptography, which depends on computational complexity, quantum cryptography utilizes quantum mechanics to enable information-theoretically secure quantum communication. One of the basic principles of quantum mechanics is the no-cloning theorem, which states that an unknown quantum state cannot be copied [ 5 ] . This security is rooted in the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, which limits the precision of simultaneous measurements of non-commuting observables [ 6 ] . Consequently, any attempt to intercept or measure quantum information would cause disturbances. Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) has emerged as a promising approach for achieving information-theoretically secure communication [ 7 ] . There are various implementations of QKD protocols that can broadly be classified into discrete-variable (DV-QKD) and continuous-variable (CV-QKD) approaches, depending on the nature of the quantum states used for encoding information. In this work, we focus primarily on the BB84 DV-QKD protocol proposed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984, one of the earliest and most influential protocols in QKD [ 8 ] . In the BB84 scheme, the sender (Alice) prepares quantum states in randomly chosen bases and transmits them to the receiver (Bob) over a quantum channel. Although quantum transmission provides a means of detecting the presence of an eavesdropper, the transmission process alone is not sufficient to generate a secure secret key. In a practical quantum communication system, the transmitted quantum states are influenced by channel noise, photon loss, detector imperfections, and other hardware-related limitations [ 9 ] . However, the raw keys obtained by Alice and Bob may differ and cannot be used directly as a shared secret key [ 10 ] . A sequence of classical post-processing procedures is therefore performed to transform the raw measurement outcomes into a common and secure key. These procedures generally include basis sifting, error-rate estimation, information reconciliation, and privacy amplification. Among these stages, information reconciliation plays a central role because it enables Alice and Bob to identify and correct discrepancies between their respective raw keys while carefully limiting the amount of information revealed over the public communication channel. Several information reconciliation approaches have been investigated for QKD systems, with Cascade and Winnow being among the earlier and widely adopted protocols [ 11 , 12 ] . More recent approaches employ structured error-correcting codes, such as low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes [ 13 ] and polar codes [ 14 ] , to perform information reconciliation. The choice of reconciliation scheme is not merely an implementation detail; it directly influences the performance of the QKD system. In particular, the amount of information disclosed during reconciliation, the computational and communication requirements, and the protocol’s ability to operate reliably at different error rates all affect the achievable secret key rate. These considerations have motivated continued research into post-processing methods for DV-QKD, with particular emphasis on improving reconciliation efficiency while limiting information leakage and maintaining reliable key agreement under realistic channel conditions. The design of efficient post-processing procedures is increasingly important for realizing QKD systems that can operate reliably under practical deployment conditions [ 15 ] . Although substantial research has focused on optimizing individual stages of QKD post-processing, several fundamental challenges remain open. In particular, the design of post-processing algorithms that minimize classical computational and communication overhead while preserving rigorous security guarantees remains an important research problem [ 16 ] . ## III DV-QKD System Model
In DV-QKD, secure key establishment is achieved by encoding classical information into quantum states and transmitting them over a quantum channel. The overall process can be modeled through three primary components: the source where the quantum state is prepared, the quantum channel for the transmission, and the detection system where the quantum state is measured. III-A Source The BB84 prepare-and-measure QKD protocol can employ weak coherent pulses (WCPs) as a practical approximation to an ideal single-photon source. The photon number in a WCPs pulse follows a Poisson distribution. For a pulse with mean photon number \mu , the probability of containing exactly n_{\mathrm{ph}} photons is given by: P(n_{\mathrm{ph}})=\frac{\mu^{n_{\mathrm{ph}}}e^{-\mu}}{n_{\mathrm{ph}}!} (1) where P(n_{\mathrm{ph}}) denotes the probability that a pulse contains exactly n_{\mathrm{ph}} photons, and \mu denotes the mean photon number per pulse. The total channel transmission efficiency, \eta_{\mathrm{ch}} , depends on the transmission medium and the distance ( d ) between Alice and Bob. For the fiber optic channel, transmission loss is represented using exponential attenuation, with \alpha_{f} the fiber attenuation coefficient denoted in dB/km. A base channel efficiency, \eta_{0} , is also included to account for practical losses arising from imperfect optical coupling, connector losses, and alignment errors. The effective fibre transmission efficiency is therefore given by: \eta_{\mathrm{ch}}(d)=\eta_{0}\,10^{-\alpha_{f}d/10}. (5) III-D Performance Metrics In DV-QKD, system performance and security are commonly evaluated using the QBER and SKR. QBER measures the mismatch between the transmitted and received key bits and reflects the effects of channel noise, device imperfections, and possible eavesdropping. In contrast, SKR represents the rate of secure key generation after the required post-processing steps. These two parameters provide a practical basis for assessing transmission accuracy, noise tolerance, key generation efficiency, and security under realistic operating conditions. III-D 1 QBER It measures the fraction of errors between the sifted keys obtained by Alice and Bob, and serves as an indicator of channel quality and potential security threats. It is defined as Q=\frac{n_{\mathrm{err}}}{n_{\mathrm{sift}}}. (6) where n_{\mathrm{err}} denotes the number of mismatched bits and n_{\mathrm{sift}} is the sifted key length. An increase in QBER may indicate eavesdropping, since an eavesdropper’s interaction with the quantum states introduces additional disturbances. III-D 2 SKR It represents the amount of secure key that Alice and Bob can obtain per transmitted signal after error correction and protection against information leakage. It can be expressed using the Devetak-Winter bound as [ 54 ] : R_{sk}=I_{AB}-\chi_{AE}. (7) where, I_{AB} denotes the classical mutual information shared by Alice and Bob, and \chi_{AE} represents the Holevo information between Alice and Eve. Thus, the achievable SKR is determined by the information shared by the legitimate users and the information potentially available to Eve.
