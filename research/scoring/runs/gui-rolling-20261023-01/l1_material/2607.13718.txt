title: How Agents Ask for Permission: User Permissions for AI Agents, from Interfaces to Enforcement
source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13718

§Abstract
As AI agents grow in prevalence, so too do their risks to users, creating a need for robust and usable systems for agent permissions. Many recent proposals implement product-level permissions, where the same security policy is applied for all users of a given agent framework. Yet different users have varying needs, necessitating support for user-level permission policies in agentic AI systems. We survey 21 proposed user-level agent permissions systems and construct a taxonomy of approaches to specifying, deriving, and enforcing user-level permissions. We further analyze and compare against five commercial agents. We find that despite a wide diversity of proposals, sizable gaps remain in usability, transparency, and user control, particularly between academic and commercial agents.

§Introduction (from cached fulltext; cut at ~9000 chars)
AI agents that combine LLMs with executable tools to take on autonomous tasks are increasingly prevalent. While agents are powerful, they also pose serious dangers [ 23 , 25 , 34 , 37 , 12 , 9 , 5 ] . Agents amplify all the existing risks of LLMs—from prompt injection and jailbreaking, to disclosing sensitive information [ 40 , 16 ] —with the ability to act directly on files, over network connections, and more. Secure agentic systems address this challenge by restricting an agent’s actions according to a security policy. Often, policies are defined at a global or product level, and applied uniformly across all users. Yet what makes for “correct” security policies, or permissions, depends on the user and context [ 7 , 8 , 5 ] . For example, one product-level security policy might block passport information from being sent to any websites other than recognized airlines. Such a policy does not account for users who want their information shared with a specialty travel booking service, nor for those who never want it shared at all. The context-dependent nature of permissions drives a need for systems that support not just product-level, but user-level agent security policies. In this work, we explore, first, the current state of the art on handling user-level permissions in agentic AI systems; and second, where the agentic security community should go from here. We approach these topics via three research questions: RQ1: How do proposed agent permissions systems allow users to specify their policies? RQ2: How are user-level policies internally specified, derived from user-selected options, and enforced? How much user transparency and control is there at each step? Finally, RQ3: What gaps remain in the existing work on user-level permissions systems for AI agents, both (a) within the literature and (b) between the literature and commercial agents? We address these questions by reviewing 21 secure agentic systems that implement user-level permissions as of the early summer of 2026. From this survey, we construct a taxonomy of approaches in agentic systems to specifying permissions policies, both at the user interface and internally; deriving internal policies from user input; and enforcing polices at run time. We further analyze five prominent commercial AI agents to understand how agent permissions systems deployed in practice compare to those in the literature. We identify several cross-cutting concerns as themes in the literature, including minimal user overhead, formalized policy specifications, and deterministic enforcement. We also find substantial gaps: none of the proposals we review fully address all three concerns, though a few come close. On the other hand, the commercial agents we evaluate often force users to choose between high-overhead user-in-the-loop permissions, and LLM auto-reviewers with little transparency or user control. Commercial agents do, however, allow users to modify policies over time, an option largely overlooked in the literature. Our contributions: • A survey of proposals for handling user-level permissions in the agentic security literature. • A taxonomy of approaches to specifying, deriving from user input, and enforcing user-level permissions policies in AI agents. • A comparative analysis of user permissions handling in five commercial AI agents. • Important gaps and areas for future work on user-level permissions in agentic systems.
